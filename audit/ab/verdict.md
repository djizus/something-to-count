# Verdict

Read cold, without knowing the book or which version is older.

## Scene 1

### 1. Which made me laugh or smile more

X, clearly.

From X:

- The postman asking the mayor where to find the mayor, and the mayor letting him. 'If you see the mayor, you might tell him it's come.' / 'I'll tell him,' said Larrère. This is the best joke in either version of either scene.
- 'He looked at the man in the car, who was looking at the church.'
- 'Nobody has written to the president.' / 'He was in the room. All three times.' / 'He was in the room as mayor.'
- 'I haven't asked for one.' / 'I'm saying it before you do.'
- 'We had eighty-eight because the hall was set for eighty-eight. People can count chairs.'
- 'They're in bed by half past eight.' / 'They're in bed every other night of the year.'
- 'You needn't have been so quick.'

From Y:

- 'It was. The three notes could be consulted in the register of deliberations at the mairie during opening hours.' A real smile, and X does not have it.
- The address 'four hundred metres from the mairie's letterbox, in which a letter could be left for nothing.' Drier than X's postman saying the same thing aloud.
- 'Marie-Jo had printed cards for two hundred. The year before, eighty-eight people had come.'

Y's jokes are all the narrator's. In X the people are funny themselves. The lines both versions share ('Put it in forty-two', the diesel deducted from her pay, 'Sixty-three' again, the mayor walking round the van) worked equally in both.

### 2. Which makes me more likely to read on

X. The mayor sitting a metre from the pen and letting her sign is the moment I started disliking him properly, and after it the ending, where he walks round the van and goes in 'to sign his post', pays off. In Y that ending is only a neat image, because I never watched him dodge the letter.

### 3. Where it is clearer what each person wants

X.

- The mayor: in Y he recites instructions. In X he wants not to be the man who received letter 41, and not to be the president when the winch comes up.
- Jeannot: only X tells me why he pays to register letters: 'so as to have that man's name on a card, and it's little Lesca who signs for them.' In Y I had to guess.
- Marie-Jo: in Y she wants Maylis on the bar. In X she wants not to be shamed in front of Hontaube's mayor's wife, which is a want I can follow into Saturday.
- Maylis: about equal. See below.

### 4. What is worse in X

- Maylis talks back ('It's the association's winch. You're the president.'). She wins the exchange on points, and then 'After 2013 she had bought grease and stopped writing' lands softer than in Y, where she says nothing and the line arrives cold. Y's Maylis is more shut, and her standing in the parking space is her only protest all day. In X it is her second.
- The postman explains the four-hundred-metres joke out loud. Y's narrator does it better in half a sentence.
- X loses 'during opening hours'.
- The chairs argument runs one exchange long. The television and the mayor's wife were enough.

## Scene 2

### 1. Which made me laugh or smile more

Y, by a distance.

From Y:

- The card players turning the score pad over to tot up her visit: 'Five. She'd a loaf under one arm and the pot in the other hand.'
- 'Was it bronze or yellow? I've been given both.' / 'Bronze.' / 'Bronze,' he told the table, and wrote it.
- 'If that's a statement, I'll have a copy.'
- The gendarme: 'Go on. I'm not here.' Then 'Is it duck now? I can sit down for duck.'
- 'She's changing the subject.'
- 'Now she wants him kept in.' / 'That's so we'd not think it,' the scorer said, and he wrote that down too.
- 'If I stick, the commune pulls me out, and I write to it about the ruts.'
- 'After a time Larrère drank it himself.'

From X:

- 'Pau sends a yellow each time it rains on Pau.'
- 'They live behind a dyke. They know there's a river.'
- 'In sixteen years the café had found nothing to say about the two of them, and it was making up the time.'

X's three lines are good, and two of them belong to the mayor. But X tells me the café has been gossiping and Y puts me in the café.

### 2. Which makes me more likely to read on

Y. It ends with a man going down to sit in a hole in a meadow on the night the river goes over, sent there by the one person who warned him not to go, and nobody in the room has heard the warning as a warning. 'Nobody had asked her about the river' is the hook. X ends on a threat and a closed window. That is a good ending, but nothing is in motion after it.

### 3. Where it is clearer what each person wants

Y for the scene as a whole, X for the mayor.

- In Y: the café wants the story, the gendarme wants to hear nothing official, Jeannot wants his water and not to be handled, Maylis wants him kept off the meadow, and every true thing she says is taken as a move in the affair. I followed all of that without help.
- The mayor is muddier in Y. He wants Jeannot out at the tonne, then he wants to be seen to have no part in it, then he orders her off the house. That is three wants in a page, and I only understood the first after the gendarme spelled it out ('he can't write to anybody from there').
- Kévin: I could not tell what he wants. The line 'It was six tides since a bucket had come up that track to his van' meant nothing to me.
- In X the mayor wants two things, the gate shut and her away from that house, and both are plain.

### 4. What is worse in Y

- The mayor is smaller. In X he puts the window down all the way ('She had not seen that done in eighteen years') and says 'That's not advice.' That frightened me. In Y he goes 'red to the collar' and makes a speech to a café. 'There was no car window between them' is a weaker use of the same idea.
- Y drops the flood-watch letter and the dyke line. In X I know the mayor has been officially warned and is sitting on it, and that is what made me worry about the people at the Port. In Y the danger rests on Maylis's word alone.
- It is crowded. There are eight people, four of them known only by a job, and I lost 'the man who had asked on Sunday who was dead' entirely.
- One paragraph defeated me: 'It lay under his duckboards, folded wet', the pad at home, the cellar 'to the second rung'. X also leans on earlier chapters, but in one sentence, not five.
- The gendarme's 'ten days to do' and 'till the eighteenth' I half followed.

## Verdicts

Scene 1: X, 4.

Scene 2: Y, 4.
