# Cold read: second

## 1. Would I hand it to that friend

Yes, as a fast weekend read, with three warnings. Scudder's speech in chapter 1 about "a little white-faced Jew in a bath-chair" and the narrator's own aside in chapter 6 ("When a Jew shoots himself in the City") are ugly, and the book only half takes the first one back. There is no woman with more than four lines. And the hero is saved by luck so often that he says so himself.

## 2. By criterion

- **Stakes.** Large but far away. "Men would be lying dead in English fields" (ch. 4) is a headline; the only stake I felt was "a six-foot hole in the garden by the evening" (ch. 6).
- **Tension.** Strong in single scenes. Decided by the roadman under three pairs of eyes: "You've a fine taste in boots. These were never made by a country shoemaker" (ch. 5).
- **Progression.** One real turn: "The little man had told me a pack of lies" (ch. 4). Otherwise chapters 3 to 6 play the same beat four times (hide, change clothes, slip away), and chapter 7 puts him in bed for ten days.
- **Comedy.** I smiled about six times, never more. Best: the drunk shepherd who kept the pledge by "nip-nippin' a' day at this brandy" (ch. 3), and "It's a wise conspirator that knows his own name" (ch. 7).
- **Characters.** The voice is the book. Everyone else gets one scene and leaves. The best of them, Scudder, is dead by the end of chapter 1; the one I liked most was Turnbull, who "took the money at last without a thank you" (ch. 7).
- **The ending.** The recognition is very good: the old man "sat back for a moment in his chair, with his fingers tapping on his knees." Then the arrest, the escape, the yacht and a world war are done in under a page. I wanted the scene after.
- **Clarity.** Read twice:
  - ch. 1, Scudder's account of who wants the war and why.
  - ch. 3, how the name Julia unlocks the cypher.
  - ch. 4, the opening pages: what in the first story was false, what survives, and whether the day is the 14th or the 15th.
  - ch. 5, the roadman's dialect; he calls himself Trummle and is Turnbull a page later.
  - ch. 6, the aerodrome hidden in the ring of trees.
  - ch. 8, the telephone call that proves the visitor was false.
  - ch. 9, why 10.27 at Bradgate confirms 10.17.

## 3. Chapter by chapter

1. Afraid for Scudder from the card in his letter-box. The knife is a hook; would not stop.
2. Mild worry while the milkman is late. Would not stop.
3. First real fear: he runs across an empty moor from nothing, then the plane appears. Would not stop.
4. No fear; the speech is fun. The chapter ends on a bicycle ride, a soft place to put it down.
5. Afraid at the boots. Jopley is a treat. Would not stop.
6. The best chapter. Afraid in the storeroom and on the dovecot roof. Would not stop.
7. No fear. I nearly stopped in the fever pages; the last line (Karolides shot) pulled me back.
8. No fear, since he is cleared. The glance in the hall is a fine turn. Would not stop.
9. No fear; men at a desk with tide tables. The lion story is the best thing in it. Would finish only because one chapter is left.
10. Afraid he would be made a fool of, not killed, and that works better than the guns did. Read to the end.

## 4. Against the comparison

**Below**, for a reader who likes both.

The one thing: luck does the hero's work and he loses nothing. Each time he is cornered a stranger, a hedge or an unlocked cupboard of explosives gets him out, and he walks into the enemy's own house by chance. He lists it himself: "The milkman, the literary innkeeper, Sir Harry, the roadman, and the idiotic Marmie, were all pieces of undeserved good fortune." A Crouch hero pays for every escape with something he loves; that show's people are changed by what happens to them. This man ends as he began, cheerful and unhurt, so I was entertained and never afraid for long.
