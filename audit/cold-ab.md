# Cold read: ab

Read once, 00 to 17, in order, at thriller speed.

The five kinds, as numbered in the brief:

1. read a sentence or paragraph twice
2. went back to an earlier page to check a fact, a name or a date
3. did not know for a moment who, whose head, which village or whose hand
4. a word I would have to look up
5. could not tell how or why something happened

No word sent me to a dictionary. The Basque is translated on the spot, and "glass eels" and "the catch" are explained in the sentence that brings them in.

### Chapter 1

**1.1, kind 1.** "She put the stone with the rest of her work and told nobody."

I read it twice to see that "her work" meant the graves. For a second I thought she had put a real stone somewhere.

Change: "She counted the stone as one more job and told nobody."

### Chapter 2

**2.1, kind 1.** "That left what Madame Etcheto gave it herself, which was the stars she could see or the ones behind the cloud."

"What … which was the stars" reads like a slip, and I had to go round it twice to find the choice she was being offered.

Change: "That left Madame Etcheto's stars. There were the ones you could see and the ones behind the cloud."

### Chapter 3

**3.1, kind 3.** "Lesca was lying on her back in a bed that wasn't hers, listening to the rain on the roof."

For the first lines I took "Lesca" for Maylis under her surname and did not know whose head I was in. The last sentence of the paragraph, about handing the coat in when she became mayor, settled it.

Change: none needed. It cost three seconds and the paragraph pays them back.

**3.2, kind 5 (why one of them wakes in the other's bed). Understood late.** "The warmth had got up to go. Lesca had stayed sitting."

The one who stayed in the chair is the one who turns up in the other's bed. That is the opposite of what "stayed" tells me, and I did not have it until the motion at the end of the chapter, "That the mayor remain seated."

Change: "The warmth had got up to go. Lesca had stayed sitting, and had gone where the warmth went."

**3.3, kind 1.** "By then the jaw was no use to Lesca."

I read it twice to see why. The next sentence gives the reason, but this one arrives first.

Change: "She had it back too late."

### Chapter 4

**4.1, kind 2.** "She had Maylis's hands too, on the steel arms of the chair, the left one open and the right one closed in a fist."

I went back to chapter 3, where "Lesca was lying in the middle of the bed with her left hand closed" and "Her left hand went up still closed." I thought the hands had been swapped by mistake. Chapter 5 explains the right fist with the scar, and then both are right, but here it looks like an error.

Change: "the left one open now and the right one closed in a fist."

**4.2, kind 5 (why one of them wakes in the other's bed). Understood late, in chapter 6.** "She didn't stand up and turn around, because that meant another meeting."

By now staying in the chair means crossing. So when Maylis "didn't stand up" I expected her to wake in Lesca's village, and she woke in her own with Lesca driving. I had to work out that this "stand up" was only about facing Lesca. Chapter 6 made it plain with "when the warmth beside her in the chair got up to go, she stayed sitting."

Change: "She didn't turn around to look, because that meant another meeting."

### Chapter 5

**5.1, kind 1.** "It closed on the handle, which ran on through her fist until Lesca got the left hand to it and stopped it."

I could not picture a handle running through a fist until I saw the cut in the next paragraph.

Change: "It closed on the handle, and the handle went on spinning inside her fist until Lesca got the left hand to it and stopped it."

### Chapter 6

**6.1, kind 5 (how long she can stay). Understood from what I was shown, on the second reading.** "She gave Inguma every metre of roadside she had cut in this village since 2008, to count before it came to take her home."

Everywhere else the words send Inguma away at once. Here she says them at 11, yet it must still come at 2:51 to put her in the chair. I stopped to ask how it could do both. "Before it came to take her home" answers it, but only if you read slowly.

Change: "It could come for her at 2:51 as it always did. For afterwards she gave it every metre of roadside she had cut in this village since 2008, to count before it came to take her home."

**6.2, kind 1.** "'I want that,' Maylis said."

I did not know what "that" was: the timer, the chair, or the room waiting for her. The secretary guesses the timer and Maylis moves on, so I am still not sure.

Change: "Maylis pointed at it. 'I want that.'"

### Chapter 7

**7.1, kind 1.** "When Maylis woke at 6, Lesca was still sitting in the chair."

As written, Lesca is left behind in the steel room. The next sentence has her getting Maylis out of bed. I read the pair twice.

Change: "When Maylis got up out of the chair at 6, Lesca stayed sitting in it."

**7.2, kind 5 (what the rain has to do with them). Understood from what I was shown.** "On Wednesday and Thursday nights Maylis had pushed Inguma off until 4, and Lesca had stayed at home. Thursday and Friday were the only dry days since the 24th."

I understood it here, and this is the right place to learn it. But I read the paragraph twice to match each night to its day.

Change: "On Wednesday night she had pushed Inguma off until 4. Thursday was dry. On Thursday night she had pushed again. Friday was dry."

### Chapter 8

**8.1, kind 1.** "But she was watching Maylis the way she would have watched a child stuck in the middle of a prayer."

The "But" made me look for a contradiction with the line before, and there is none.

Change: "She was watching Maylis the way she would have watched a child stuck in the middle of a prayer."

**8.2, kind 1.** "'Ask me tomorrow and I'd have you. But it's the other one's bed, and she's in this wrist hearing every word, and she hasn't asked me into it.'"

If the trouble is the bed and the wrist, tomorrow changes nothing. I read it twice to find what tomorrow was set against.

Change: "'Ask me tomorrow and I'd have you. Tonight it's the other one's bed, and she's in this wrist hearing every word, and she hasn't asked me into it.'"

### Chapter 9

**9.1, kind 2.** "'When she comes over into me she brings her river with her.'"

I went back to check. The rain began in the night before the 24th. Lesca first woke in Maylis's bed on the 28th. On that first night Maylis pushed Inguma off after three minutes, and it still rained all Tuesday. So the rain cannot come from Lesca being inside her. It seems to come from the two of them meeting under Inguma, and Maylis's own answer two lines later ("my first night on my back") says as much.

Change: "'Every night she reaches me she brings her river with her.'"

**9.2, kind 5 (why a word will not come out in the steel room). Understood from what I was shown.** "'It's the ti—' Lesca said. She started again, and it stuck on the same word."

At the line itself I thought she was choking or being stopped by Maylis. "'It came out in my kitchen.' 'This isn't your kitchen.'" gave it to me: in there she cannot say what she knows is false. Chapter 11 ("In this room it came out") and chapter 17 ("It came out whole") confirmed it.

Change: none needed.

**9.3, kind 5 (where a person ends up and why). Understood late.** "'That's where I sit if I stay with you,' Lesca said."

I could not see why staying in Maylis's village would seat Lesca among the dead. I had to go back to chapter 2 for Madame Etcheto's "The one it cannot take home dies in that bed." Lesca points at it ("The old woman in room twelve told you") without saying what was told.

Change: "'The old woman in room twelve told you what happens to the one it can't take home.'"

### Chapter 12

**12.1, kind 1.** "'That's something.'"

I could not tell what Kévin meant: that it was a relief the van was not his, or that it was a great deal to have had in front of her.

Change, if he means the second: "'That's a lot for one night.'"

### Chapter 13

**13.1, kind 5 (where a person ends up and why). Never fully understood.** "Inguma hadn't taken her home. It had put her down where her card was."

Maylis has told it to take her home. Lesca has let go of the wheel. She lands in the hall of the dead all the same. Later Inguma picks her up again and does take her home. I do not know why it set her down there first, and I do not know why it then let her off. I guessed two reasons and the book confirmed neither: that she held the wheel for a ring too long, or that her own body had been left empty too often.

Chapter 16 gave me the other half late: when twelve was called "somebody sat down", and that was Madame Etcheto. I still cannot tell whether she sat in the place laid for Lesca.

Change: I cannot write it without knowing the reason. Half a line of what Lesca herself takes the reason to be would do, for example: "She had held the wheel one ring too long for that."

### Chapter 14

**14.1, kind 2.** "'The handle's on,' she shouted. 'It sticks at nineteen. Get your shoulder under it.'"

If the handle is on, I did not see why she threw him the key. I had to think back to chapter 12, where she locks the padlock on the platform behind her at 11.

Change: "'The handle's on,' she shouted. 'That's for the padlock. It sticks at nineteen. Get your shoulder under it.'"

**14.2, kind 1.** "By 4:10 the corridor was empty from the front doors to the fire door, but for one chair."

Two lines on, "Every name on it had a line through it except one." I took the one chair and the one name for the same person. Then the chair turned out to be Madame Poey's and the name Madame Etcheto's, and I read the three paragraphs again.

Change: "By 4:10 the corridor was empty from the front doors to the fire door, but for Madame Poey's chair."

### Chapter 17

**17.1, kind 1.** "Maylis measured it in her head, from the trapdoor to that door."

I did not know which door. Sarthou's is open behind him, Madame Darrigrand's is two doors up, and the care home's are open too.

Change: "Maylis measured it in her head, from the trapdoor to Madame Darrigrand's door."

**17.2, kind 1.** "As it went, the steel under its leaves knocked against the steel of the frame, like one part of the room touching another."

I read it twice before I saw what it was telling me: that Inguma and the steel room are made of the same thing.

Change: "As it went, the steel under its leaves knocked against the steel of the frame, and the two rang the same."

## 2. Errors

**Count: 4.** I found no typos, no punctuation slips and no spelling that is not British. Inguma is "it" throughout. Clock times and dates are in figures throughout.

**Chapter 6. Pronoun with an unclear owner.**

"A doctor would put Maylis in an ambulance to Bayonne, and this was the bed she had to come back to."

Every "she" in this scene has been Coralie up to here, so this one reads as Coralie for a moment.

Corrected: "A doctor would put Maylis in an ambulance to Bayonne, and this was the bed Maylis had to come back to."

**Chapter 11. Pronoun with an unclear owner.**

"She had left her own Castagnet there."

The last "She" was Arlette ("She looked at Lesca's hands on the desk"), and Arlette has just spoken.

Corrected: "Lesca had left her own Castagnet there."

**Chapter 14. Pronoun with an unclear owner.**

"Then his headlights came down the hill again and across the glass."

Kévin was last named four paragraphs earlier. In between come a woman holding Maylis's sleeve and something big floating by in the street.

Corrected: "Then Kévin's headlights came down the hill again and across the glass."

**Chapter 14. Correct, but reads as a mistake.**

"And the old wing was two steps lower."

Chapter 12 and the next scene both give the old wing a ramp. "Two steps" made me think the building had changed.

Corrected: "And the old wing was lower still, at the bottom of its ramp."

Not counted, because it is usage and not spelling: "baby carriage" (chapter 17) and "dish towel" (chapters 1, 5 and 11) are American terms in a text spelled the British way. The British words are "pram" and "tea towel". Both American terms are easier for a reader like me, so I would leave them unless a British ear matters more.
