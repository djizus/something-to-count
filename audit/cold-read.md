# Cold read

Chapters read: `manuscript/01.md` to `manuscript/15.md`, in order. Nothing else opened.

## Part 1. As you go

### Chapter 1

Maylis Lesca, 43, is the only outdoor worker of a village called Peyrebarthe. She wakes at 02:51 unable to move, with a weight on her chest that nothing visible is making. The day before she cracked a rib on the winch of a sluice gate. The mayor wants the gate raised, a duck hunter called Castagnet wants it left, and the village is about to be merged into a bigger one, which may cost her the job.

I do not understand the stone. "She also had a stone in her head" (ch. 1): I took it at first for a headache, then for a dream, and then the book says "She remembered the stone the way she remembered where she had left the van", so it is a memory of something that is not there. I do not know whose memory. I do not know what "the tide" is doing in a village with meadows ("before the tide turned"), and I am guessing the river is close to the sea.

Guesses: raising the gate lets water out of the meadows, because Castagnet "sat over mud" after she raised it. "A hand of water" I take to be a hand's depth. The weight smells "of cold steel, and of old tobacco", and the box was a steel room in a tobacco barn, so I am guessing the weight has something to do with the afternoon in the box in 2014. I know "loto" is bingo and "mairie" is the town hall only from outside the book; neither is explained. "Saint Catherine's" is given as a date and that was enough.

### Chapter 2

The same night and the same minute, but this Maylis Lesca is the mayor, and has been for eleven years. In her village Jeannot Castagnet drowned in a flood on 12 December 2014, the stone is real and stands in the parking space with his name above hers, the meadows are a gravel lake, and Coralie sleeps across the landing. She wakes with the other woman's Monday in her head (the winch, the rib, forty letters where she has twelve). So I now think there are two versions of one woman in two versions of one village, and at 02:51 each gets some of the other's memories.

I do not understand why her name is on the stone under a drowned man's when she is alive. "A verb was proposed at each sitting and lost at each" (ch. 2): I am guessing the verb would have said what she did for him or to him that night, and nobody could agree. I do not understand why the mayor has had the weight "on most nights for twelve years" when the other woman seems to be having it for the first time. "She had brought an afternoon up with her" lost me for a paragraph. I do not know what a "firework bull" is, only that it costs nine thousand euros and brings six thousand people.

Guesses: she mends the widow's roof out of guilt ("no deliberation covered it"). "Who was meant to go round the tonnes that night" I take to mean somebody was supposed to warn the hunters and did not, and it may have been her. "Deliberation" seems to mean a council decision. "The Port" seems to be the low part of the village by the river, not a harbour. In this village Hontaube is asking to join Peyrebarthe, the reverse of chapter 1, which I take to be because of the gravel money.

### Chapter 3

Back with the worker Maylis. On the second night she does not push and the weight does not get worse. She wakes with a second foreign memory: herself in the mayor's sash before a full square, behind the stone. She works out that the stone is not a gravestone, and that the scene is neither her past nor her future. Kévin Labat, who helps her hang the lights, nets glass eels at the gates at night and once offered her two hundred euros to leave a padlock off. He teaches her a trick for knowing you are dreaming: count your fingers every hour.

"So it was not to come, and it had not been, and she remembered it" (ch. 3) took me three readings; I think it means she has decided the memory belongs to a life she is not living, but she does not say so and I am not sure she has got that far. I do not understand what the padlock is on or why leaving it off helps Kévin: "to leave the padlock off for a night". I do not know why the church belongs to the commune because it "had been built before 1905"; I took it as a rule and moved on.

Guesses: the finger-counting is going to be how she takes control at 02:51, because she asks "Is it you in it? Your own hand?" The glass eels were explained well enough that I followed (what they are, the price, where they are caught). I am guessing "men in green from the biodiversity office" are wardens and that the eel netting is illegal, because he waited for the gendarmes; the book does not say it is illegal.

### Chapter 4

The mayor again. Villagers bring their complaints about the gravel lake to her mother's kitchen instead of the mairie; her mother has not forgiven her for the ponies. The mayor asks her mother what the old people called the thing on the chest and is told "the sarramauca", "the chest-squeezer", with four rules, one of which is the same finger-counting Kévin taught the other woman. She starts a notebook and writes down what she received in the night: the other woman's Tuesday (letter 41, Castagnet alive and laughing, his van where the stone is).

The mayor does not seem to know what she is looking at. She notices "He has sent one since Tuesday" and underlines "his", but the book gives no word for it: "with no word in it for what it was" (ch. 4). I think that is deliberate, but I still do not know whether she thinks it is a dream, a ghost or another life. I do not understand why the mayor's Wednesday night "left her nothing by morning except the sound of rain", when the other woman got the sash that night; I do not know what decides who gets what.

Guesses: the sarramauca is a local name for the creature, and a folk belief; I do not know whether the book wants me to believe in it. "To be sure you're back" suggests the grandmother went somewhere too. "Monsieur le maire" said to a woman mayor is a running joke about nobody accepting her; I got that. I am guessing the Coralie story is that the mayor's silence cost her the marriage and the worker's has not yet.

### Chapter 5

The worker wakes with a third thing: the meadows under still grey water, seen from a height with something like a roof tile in her hand. I know from chapter 2 that this is the mayor on the widow's roof looking at the gravel lake. The worker does not, and she builds a wrong story from it: the winch jammed with the gate down, the water stayed, she had warned them, "and they gave me the mairie". Later her hand signs a delivery slip with the mayor's signature, flourish and all, which she has never made. So skills cross over, not only memories.

I am not sure what the gate does. In chapter 1 raising it left Castagnet "over mud", so raised lets water out. Here the fear is the winch stopping "with the gate down and the river over the line" (ch. 5), and Castagnet's 1952 story is that "the river came over in February with the gate down ... and they couldn't raise it. It lay till Easter". So a shut gate traps water in the meadows. But I do not know whether a shut gate also keeps the river out, or how the river gets in when it is shut ("came over" what?). I do not know what "the line" on the gauge marks.

Guesses: the mayor's hand will do something of the worker's in the next chapter. The card player's "She's checking the box gave them all back" makes me think the box in 2014 is where this started, but nobody has said what was done to her in it. I do not know who the "member from the high meadows" is to anybody; he seems to be there only to fail at the winch.

### Chapter 6

The mayor finds a third way to meet the weight: not pushing and not letting her breath go either. It holds for a quarter of an hour and then she is sitting in the steel room from 2014, awake, able to move, with no weight. Two lamps are lit where in 2014 one came on once. The chair arms are "warmer than she had made them", and the card she sealed in 2014 lies open with writing on it. She wakes before reading it. By day she plans the commemoration on the plots of four houses she had demolished for the new dyke, counts her fingers during a vote, and the priest asks her for the twelfth time to come to confession.

I do not understand what the room now is. Is she dreaming it, or is it a real place the two women can both get to? "So she was back, by the rule, and she was in the chair. She had both at once" (ch. 6) I could not turn into a plain statement. I do not know what the two lamps mean. My guess is that in 2014 one of two lamps came on, each woman saw a different one, and that is the split between them; two lit means both are present. That is a guess from having read about such experiments in a newspaper, and the book has not said it.

Guesses: the warm chair means the other woman has been sitting there. The mayor confessed something about the flood night to the priest in January 2015, and "You said it to me" means he thinks she owes it to the widow. I still do not know what she did. Maïté looking "at a place on it to the left of the dredger" I take to be where her husband's tonne was.

### Chapter 7

The worker's Saturday: the loto, with her behind the bar. In the night she got a fourth thing, Kévin on the commune's mower in her yellow waterproof, seen from a car. I take this to be the mayor's village, where Kévin is the outdoor man. The worker reads it as her own job gone and is short with him. Castagnet has filed a complaint about the water being lowered on Monday, with her rib as evidence. Marie-Jo tells her she was asked onto an election list in 2020 and never answered, and they lost by thirty-eight votes.

I do not understand why Castagnet "won't file against Larrère by name". Sarthou "did not say why not" (ch. 7), and I have no guess beyond Larrère being a big man locally. I do not understand what crime there is in lowering the water, or why the gendarme treats it as a real file. "They'll be glad to hear it at the barn" I did not get: the university team left, so who is at the barn? The loto calls ("on two lines", "on a full card") I followed only because I know bingo.

Guesses: the worker thinks the pictures show her future. She has not worked out that they are another woman's present, and I am not certain the book wants me ahead of her. The 2020 list is the worker's version of the road to the mairie that the mayor took in 2015. "Where the twelve would be" is the care home closing in June. The empty chair Marie-Jo keeps for her is about her never sitting down with the village.

### Chapter 8

The mayor wakes with the other village's loto, Castagnet alive and shouting for the ham. She opens the envelope she has kept since 2014: her card says "right". She then works out a method (let the weight take her at 02:51, get up at four for twenty minutes, lie down again, hold without pushing) and reaches the room, where the card on the shelf says "left" in her own handwriting. She concludes somebody with her hand saw the other lamp. This is the first place the book says it nearly outright, and it confirmed my guess from chapter 6. We also learn that in her village Kévin went to prison for eel netting on her camera evidence and she then gave him the outdoor job.

I do not understand why Dr Kessler "had called her the friend all that afternoon" (ch. 8). It reads as if it means something and I do not know what. I do not understand why 02:51 and not some other minute. I do not know what the "Natura grant" is beyond sixty thousand euros a year for stopping the netting. I do not understand why she is so afraid of the door on her left, unless she expects the other woman to come through it.

Guesses: the experiment made one woman into two by showing each a different lamp, and the room is the one place both still share. I do not know how a lamp can do that and the book has not tried to tell me. The method with the four o'clock alarm I followed step by step, but why getting up and lying down again works I took on trust from the grandmother's rule ("if you lay down again it was waiting").

### Chapter 9

The worker's turn at the same discovery. Larrère has seen the strange signature and tells her to sign the old way, and denies having telephoned the order to raise the gate. Kévin gives her the same method the mayor found (up at four, twenty minutes, lie down, do not push, do not drop off). Her own card says "left". She reaches the room, the card there says "right", and while she sits in the warm chair three things come to her from the mayor's life: the new dyke with the priest on the bench, kissing the widow who wipes it off, and nets hidden under the floor of Castagnet's tonne. She writes "There are two of us. She is the mayor."

This chapter repeats the envelope scene of chapter 8 almost word for word, and I knew what the card would say before she opened it. I do not understand how the cards get onto the shelf in the room: the mayor read "left" there two nights before the worker had opened her own envelope. "That's the door" (ch. 9) is Kévin's word for the weight and I do not know if he means it as a figure of speech. I do not understand why the chair is "narrower than it had been" for both of them.

Guesses: the nets in the tonne are eel nets and Castagnet was a netter as well as a duck hunter, which may be why he "won't file against Larrère by name" (Larrère knows). The mayor found those nets at some point, I am guessing on the flood night or just after. Whoever sits in the chair second gets what the first one left in it; I am not sure of that. The two women's days are now one day apart (the mayor reached the room on the night of Sunday, the worker on the night of Monday).

### Chapter 10

The mayor works out the rule: whatever one of them carries in her head all day reaches the other within a night or two. So she rehearses one memory all Monday to send it on purpose, the prefect pinning a medal on her in February 2015, and keeps another out of it (a van at the end of a track). The citation finally says what she did: on the night of the flood, alone and against an order, she opened the gate, which saved forty houses and the care home, and Castagnet died. She goes into the room fully awake this time and gets back a picture of the other woman chaining Castagnet's van to the tractor at dawn. From that she decides the other one "went down the track and knocked" and that is why he is alive there.

I think the mayor is wrong, and I am not sure I am meant to think so. Chapter 1 said the tractor pulled his van out of the mud in January 2017 (letter 17), so the chain picture need not be the flood night at all. If I had not remembered letter 17 I would have taken her conclusion as fact. "Nobody in the square that morning had said the word" (ch. 10) lost me: I think the word is "van". "She had not cried since the funeral, where she had not been entitled to" I only half follow.

Larrère's speech is the first time the gate was explained so I could hold it: "raised on a falling river and shut on a rising one ... It is a door in a bank." So on the flood night she opened it on a rising river and let the river into the meadows, which is what drowned a man sitting in a hut out there. I am guessing that is also what took the pressure off the houses. I still do not know what happened to the houses in the worker's village, where the gate was presumably kept shut.

### Chapter 11

The worker uses what she saw in the room. She tells Castagnet she knows about the nets under his duckboards and that Kévin drives the catch to Irun, and gets him to withdraw the complaint and letter 41. He stops calling her "little". Kévin is angry and a little afraid of her. She falls asleep on the mower from the four o'clock wakings and knocks over a bollard. That night she goes in awake with no gap and receives the medal ceremony whole, exactly as the mayor rehearsed it, and writes "I want that." Then something the mayor did not mean to send follows it: a round crooked reflector on the back of a white van in headlamps at the end of a track, and a hand turning a winch fast. The worker decides "She drove into him."

I think the worker is wrong again, the same way the mayor was wrong in chapter 10. My reading is that the mayor saw his van parked at the end of the track, knew he was out in his hut, and opened the gate anyway without going to fetch him. The book has not said that. "That's the fours" (ch. 11) I took to mean the four o'clock alarms. "Can't place the winch" confused me until I remembered the mayor's village replaced the hand winch in 2016, so in 2014 it was the same winch; I do not see why the worker cannot place it.

Guesses: each woman now wants what the other has (the worker wants the medal and the square; the mayor wants Castagnet alive and Marie-Jo using her name). The worker checking the reflector on the real van is her proof that what she sees is true. I do not know what "Is it here?" "No" costs her with Coralie, but it reads as the start of the worker's marriage going the way the mayor's went.

### Chapter 12

The mayor's version of the worker's chapter 11. In the room she is given three scenes from the other life: Marie-Jo asking the worker onto the 2020 list, the worker mowing a verge with the phone ringing until the deadline passed, and the worker pushing Kévin's two hundred euros back. By day she falls asleep in the chair at the budget committee and Larrère gets an amendment through while she sleeps; she wakes and reverses it. Then she asks Kévin and Marie-Jo "what if I had done nothing" questions, and learns that the woman who did nothing still has both of them speaking to her. Marie-Jo will not let her in.

Little was unclear here. "With the platform left unlocked" (ch. 12) finally told me what the padlock in chapter 3 was for: the netters need to get onto the gate platform. "Line 6232" and "prevented, in the sense of the article" are council language I skimmed. I did not understand why the mayor "kept her hands where they were ... and did not count them" at six, or what Marie-Jo "saw" in that; my guess is she chose to stay in this conversation and not check whether it was real.

Guesses: the two halves are being built as mirrors (asleep on the mower / asleep in the chair; one uses the other's knowledge to act, the other uses the other's inaction to see what her acting cost). I expect each to try to live more like the other, and I expect it to go badly. I notice the mayor has not yet received the worker's "I want that".

### Chapter 13

The worker wakes with a raised burn across her right palm, where the mayor has her scar from December 2014. So now a mark on the body has crossed, not only memories and a signature. In the room she was shown the gravel lease, the lake, and her mother's ponies going up a ramp, and she corrects herself: "Not a flood. A lake." Coralie reads the pad, cannot explain the hand, notices she is on none of the pages, takes it that "I want that" is about Maïté Castagnet, and moves to the box room, as the other Coralie did in 2015. Castagnet withdraws his letter and his complaint. Her mother recognises the mark as a winch burn and asks what she has been turning.

I do not understand the burn. The mayor's is twelve years old and is called "a seam"; this one is "like a thing some days old" and Coralie says "It's been healing a week" (ch. 13). I do not know why it arrives as a one-week-old wound, or why it arrived the night after she wrote "Can't place the winch". I do not know whether the mayor has lost hers or got the rib. I am not sure whether Coralie thinks there is an affair with Maïté or only that Maylis wants a life Coralie is not in; "She's on four pages" reads like jealousy.

Guesses: the bodies are starting to become one body, and that is a danger, not a gift. The school-roof sum ("One year of the lake was six school roofs") is the worker starting to think the mayor's way, and her question to her mother is her testing the price. I expect the mayor to turn up with a cracked rib.

### Chapter 14

The mayor sees the worker's Port from inside the worker's van: old dyke, four houses still standing, no flood plates on the walls, Sarthou clipping his hedge. She also receives the fact of letter 17, and strikes out her chapter 10 conclusion: the chain was a tow in 2017, not the morning after the flood. Then she notices it has been raining in everything she has been given, checks the 2014 file, and sees the river readings match. The other village has not been spared the flood. It has not had it yet, and it is coming there on 12 December 2026. She composes a message of five words to send across, "Go to the tonne first", and repeats it for hours. The weight takes her in daylight at a traffic light, and she learns that staring at a fixed lamp holds her in place. She uses the same trick to stay in the room nineteen minutes past the alarm.

This is where the book came together for me, and also where the biggest hole opened. "The rain had not fallen there" (ch. 14): I do not understand why one village had the 2014 rain and the other did not, when the only difference between them is which lamp a woman saw. I do not understand why the same rain then falls on the other village exactly twelve years later. "It was not the lamp that had come on for her in 2014" I read as her holding on to the other woman's lamp to stay on the other woman's side; not sure.

Guesses: Sarthou's biscuit tin of notebooks holds something about the flood night that is not in his reports, in spite of what he says ("There's nothing in them that isn't in my reports. She had not asked whether there was"). I think it is that he saw her that night, or that he was the one meant to go round the tonnes. "I was at the left front corner" I take to be the coffin. Coralie closing her door is the mayor's marriage getting worse while she tries to mend the other village.

### Chapter 15

The worker cannot look at Castagnet, because on her pad he is dead. She buys funeral chrysanthemums without deciding to and takes them to his wife, asks about a leaking gutter she has no way of knowing about, and looks at Maïté as at a widow. Maïté has a packed suitcase on the wardrobe with no dust on it. The weight takes the worker in daylight on the tractor; Coralie sees it at last and offers to come back across the landing, and Maylis does not ask her to. In the room she gets the cameras and Kévin's prison, then more of the flood night: the van standing still in her headlamps, the winch tearing her palm, then the hatch of the tonne with black water to the second rung. She stays past the alarm by holding the other lamp, and the fourth wall of the box is gone: beyond it is the loto hall, with rows of tables that do not end, everyone slightly doubled, her mother at seventy and at thirty side by side, and one empty chair with a card.

I do not know what the endless hall is. "Each one was a little more than one, like a photograph of somebody who has moved" (ch. 15): my guess is that it is everybody in every version at once, and that the empty chair is an invitation to stay there and belong to none of them. I do not know if it is a good place or a trap. I do not understand "She went for him. She got to the tonne too late": the order she was shown was van, then winch, then hatch, so I think the mayor opened the gate first and went to the tonne after, which is also what "Go to the tonne first" implies. The worker's version is kinder than what I think happened.

Guesses: Maïté is planning to leave Castagnet (the suitcase), which nobody in either village knows. Castagnet "not been down to the tonne" for four nights because of the blackmail may matter when the flood comes. The mayor's five words had not arrived by the end of this chapter. The book stops here unfinished, six days before the twelfth.

## Part 3. Questions

**1. What is it that happens to her at 02:51?**
She wakes and can move nothing but her eyes. A cold weight, about a sack of cement, sits on her breastbone with nothing there to see, smelling of cold steel and old tobacco. It pushes back exactly as hard as she pushes, and after a few minutes it puts her under. If she neither pushes nor gives way, she passes awake into the steel room from 2014.

**2. Is there a creature in this book? If so, describe it.**
I don't know. The mother calls the weight the sarramauca, the chest-squeezer, a thing that "sits" on you, and it has no hands and no face, is cold, and smells of steel and tobacco. Nobody ever sees anything. My guess is there is no creature and the weight is the box, or the other woman, pressing through.

**3. Why are there two of her?**
Because in the steel room in June 2014 one of two lamps came on, and one of her saw the left one and the other saw the right one; each still has the card with her word on it. From then on there are two women in two Peyrebarthes. How a lamp can do that I don't know. I would guess the experiment was built to do exactly that to a person.

**4. What was the afternoon in the box for?**
I don't know. A university team rented the tobacco barn, shut her in a steel room for three hours, one lamp lit once, she pressed a buzzer and wrote which lamp on a card, and they paid her 180 euros and never collected the card. I would guess it was a test of whether a result is one thing or the other until somebody looks at it, with her as the somebody.

**5. What does the sluice gate do when it is opened, and when it is kept shut? Who gains and who loses each way?**
It is a door in the bank between the river and the low meadows. Opened on a falling river it drains the meadows: the maize and grazing people (Larrère's association) gain, and Castagnet loses the water at his duck hut. Kept shut it holds water on the meadows, which suits Castagnet, and on a rising river it keeps the river off the meadows, which suits the landowners and anyone out there but leaves the river high against the houses of the Port. Opened in a flood it lets the river onto the meadows: the houses and the care home gain, and whoever is in a hut on the meadows drowns. I am not sure of the last part; the book never lays it out.

**6. What happened on the night of 12 December 2014?**
In the mayor's village, after rain since 24 November, the river was ninety centimetres over the red line and rising. Larrère telephoned the order to keep the gate shut. She was the commune's worker then, with the key; she opened it alone against the order, tore her palm on the winch, and forty houses and the care home stayed dry. Castagnet was in his tonne on the meadows and drowned. She had seen his van at the end of the track, and she got to his hatch and found water to the second rung. I believe she opened first and went after. In the worker's village nothing happened that night; the rain did not fall there.

**7. What is a tonne? What are the men doing at the gates at night?**
A tonne is a half-buried hut out on the flood meadows that a man sits in at night to shoot duck, with a hatch, a ladder and duckboards; it wants a little water standing at it. The men at the gates are netting glass eels, finger-long transparent young eels that come up the river on the night tides between November and March and sell for four hundred euros a kilo over the border. It is against the law. Kévin drives them; Castagnet keeps the nets under his floor.

**8. What are the two women each learning to do, and how does it work?**
Both are learning to go into the weight awake and reach the steel room. Let it take you at 02:51, get up at four, stay up twenty minutes, lie down on your back, and when it comes do not push and do not let go; keep your eyes on the red clock figures until they become the two lamps. Once in, count your fingers and read the card twice to be sure it is not a dream. The mayor is also learning to send: she goes over one thing every hour all day and it arrives whole in the other woman's night. Both have learned to stay past the alarm by fixing on a lamp. The worker is learning to use what she receives on people in her own village.

**9. What does each woman want?**
The worker wants what the medal stands for: a full square turned towards her, and something done in Peyrebarthe because she said so, after a life of being told ("I want that"). The mayor wants Castagnet alive and people using her first name again, and since she cannot have that, she wants the other woman to go to the tonne before she touches the gate. I think the mayor also wants to be forgiven by the widow and cannot ask.

**10. What do you expect to happen by the end?**
The flood comes to the worker's village on 12 December 2026. Larrère telephones to keep the gate shut, the winch sticks at the nineteenth turn, and she has to choose between the houses and the man, with five words from the other side in her head, possibly garbled. I expect she gets Castagnet out, or finds he is not there, and still pays for it some other way. On the same day the mayor stands at the stone before the prefect and six thousand people and either says the missing verb aloud or seizes up under the weight in front of them. I expect one of them to be tempted to sit down in the empty chair and not come back, and Coralie to be what decides it.

**11. Where were you bored, and where did you most want to read on?**
Bored: the second envelope scene in ch. 9, which I had already read in ch. 8; the first half of ch. 12 (the budget read line by line); the siting of the marquee in ch. 6; and, across the book, the vote counts and sums of euros, which I stopped reading as numbers by about ch. 6. Larrère's "I'll be brief" and the "still in the box" joke had worn thin by ch. 9. Most wanted to read on: the end of ch. 8 (the card says "left"), ch. 14 from the missing flood plates to "It had not had it yet", the end of ch. 13 ("What have you been turning, Maylis?"), and the last pages of ch. 15.

**12. In one sentence, what would you tell a friend this book is about?**
A village odd-job woman and the mayor she would have become, split apart by an experiment twelve years ago, start waking in each other's memories, and the mayor realises the flood in which she let a man drown is about to happen to the other one.
