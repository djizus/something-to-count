Read once, 00 to 17, in order, at thriller speed. English is not my first language.

### Chapter 1

**1.** Kind 1.

> He nodded towards the flood fields, where he shot ducks from his hut. It was a cellar dug into the field, under a trapdoor in the grass.

A hut is above ground in my head, so "It was a cellar" sent me back to see what "It" was. The sentence also falls in the middle of Jeannot's speech, between his first line and "One."

Change: "He nodded towards the flood fields, where he shot ducks. His hut was a cellar dug into the field, under a trapdoor in the grass."

### Chapter 2

**2.** Kind 5. Understood late.

> 'It hasn't come for you, girl. It comes to your bed.'

I could not tell why it should come to that bed and not to Maylis. Coralie asks the same thing at the end of the scene, so I knew the book meant to leave it open, and I read on. I had the answer in chapter 4, when Lesca says it has sat on her every night for twelve years, and I remembered the leaf crumbs in the blanket.

Change: none. The question is asked out loud and it is answered.

### Chapter 3

**3.** Kind 3. Slight.

> Lesca, the mayor, was lying on her back in a bed that wasn't hers, listening to the rain on the roof.

For this one sentence I did not know whose head I was in. The mayor so far is Larrère, and Lesca is Maylis's surname. By the yellow raincoat at the end of the paragraph I had it, and from then on "Lesca" and "Maylis" never got mixed up.

Change: none. It lasted one paragraph and I think it is meant to.

**4.** Kind 5, and kind 1. Understood late.

> Lesca had stayed sitting, and had gone with it.

I read it twice. "Stayed" and "gone" pull against each other, and this is the sentence that carries how she gets across. I understood it properly a few pages on, at "That morning she had stayed sitting, and she had woken up over there with a mouth." After that the idea was clear every time it came back (chapters 6, 7, 13).

Change: "Lesca had stayed sitting, and it had taken her with it."

### Chapter 4

**5.** Kind 5. Understood on the second reading.

> 'I can't. Inguma has sat on me every night for twelve years, and I've never kept it off. I stopped trying in the first month.'

Maylis tells her to stay in her own bed. The answer is about Inguma sitting on her, and I had to stop and join the two: the weight is what puts her in the chair, so she is there every night whether she wants it or not, and only Maylis can keep them apart, by pushing.

Change: "'I can't. Inguma has sat on me every night for twelve years and put me in this chair, and I've never kept it off.'"

### Chapter 5

**6.** Kind 1.

> Then she looked across the table for the other half of the laugh.

I read this and the two sentences after it twice before I saw that the other half is Maylis laughing back.

Change: "Then she looked across the table for Maylis to laugh with her." The next two sentences can stand; "another half" in the last one then reads at once.

### Chapter 6

**7.** Kind 1.

> She was the one driving.

There are vans and cars on every page, and she has just woken in a bed. For a second I took it as a real vehicle.

Change: "This time she had the legs, the left hand and the mouth."

**8.** Kind 1.

> 'After I lay them, you— Don't kiss me, up there. Not this year.'

"Up there" had nothing to point at. No stage has been mentioned yet, and the kiss at the ceremony is only explained in chapter 11.

Change: "'After I lay them, you— Don't kiss me, at the stone. Not this year.'"

### Chapter 7

**9.** Kind 4. Slight.

> The pulse went down the chart from 64 to 34, beside two columns of ticks headed *eyes* and *turned*.

I did not know "turned" as a word from nursing. I guessed it, and chapter 17 confirmed it ("who gets turned and who's left to sleep").

Change: "headed *eyes* and *turned her*."

### Chapter 8

**10.** Kind 1.

> 'Ask me tomorrow and I'd have you.'

I read it twice. I could not tell whether she meant tomorrow as a day or as a way of saying "nearly". Chapter 9 takes it as the day ("It's tomorrow"), and then it was clear.

Change: "'Ask me in the morning and I'd have you.'" Chapter 9 can keep "It's tomorrow".

### Chapter 9

**11.** Kind 1.

> 'It's tomorrow, and I've got one question. Is that chart right?'

For two chapters "the chart" has been the pulse chart, and Wednesday's is on the pad at the top of this scene. I thought she was asking about the pulse. She means the three columns she has just filled in.

Change: "'Is that page right?'" and, lower down, "Coralie tore the page off the pad".

**12.** Kind 5. Understood on the second reading; confirmed in chapters 11 and 17.

> 'It's the ti—' Lesca said. She started again, and it stuck on the same word.

I read the whole short scene twice. On the second pass I had it: in that room she cannot say a thing she knows is false. A week ago "I don't know" was true, so it came out. The line that held me up is the last one, "Maylis waited for her to say so again. She didn't." It can be read as Lesca choosing not to.

Change: "Maylis waited for her to say so again. Lesca opened her mouth and shut it."

### Chapter 12

**13.** Kind 3, and kind 1.

> The old wing came first. At its far end a ramp went up to a fire door, which stood open. Beyond that was the main corridor, where the night shift had lined up the wheelchairs.

I read the paragraph twice to build the building. Then Maylis is on the back step while Coralie, Bixente and Madame Etcheto talk to her from beyond the old wing and up a ramp, as if they were a pace away. I did not know where anybody was standing. In chapter 14 the same place is easy, because I come at it from the other end.

Change: "The old wing came first, only four doors long."

### Chapter 13

**14.** Kind 5. Understood late.

> Then Inguma was on her chest, where she stood, with its leaves under her chin.

It has just put her down at her card because she held on too long. Now it comes for her again, and I could not tell why it was allowed to. I understood at the top of chapter 15, when twelve is called and somebody pulls a chair in, and chapter 16 says it outright.

Change: none. The wait was two pages and the answer is shown.

### Chapter 14

**15.** Kind 4.

> The bed had been wound up as high as it went.

I read "wound" as an injury first.

Change: "The bed had been raised as high as it went."

### Chapter 17

**16.** Kind 5. Half understood.

> As it went, the steel under its leaves knocked against the steel of the frame, like one part of the room touching another.

I read it twice. I took it to mean that the thing is made of the box and is leaving it for good. That fits the rivets and the smell of the barn in chapter 1. But two old women had words for Inguma long before any box, and I could not make the two fit. It did not spoil the ending. The lamps going out and the empty wardrobe told me what I needed.

Change: "As it went, the steel under its leaves knocked against the steel of the frame, and the two gave the same note."

### The things the brief names, one by one

- **How one woman gets into the other's village.** Understood late. First shown at the end of chapter 2; the sentence in item 4 slowed me; clear by the motion in chapter 3.
- **Why one of them wakes in the other's bed.** Understood from what I was shown. Whoever stays in the chair goes where the one who gets up goes.
- **How long she can stay.** Understood from what I was shown: until Inguma finishes its count and comes for her. Twenty minutes with nothing to count, a day with the stones, too long with the roadsides, a night with the corn. The falling pulse told me the cost without help.
- **Why a word will not come out in the steel room.** Understood on the second reading (item 12).
- **What the rain has to do with either of them.** Understood from what I was shown, in chapter 7: two nights of pushing, two dry days. Maylis's three words at the bar landed at once because of it. One small wobble afterwards: in chapter 9 she says "When she comes over into me she brings her river with her", but the rain began on the night of the 23rd, before anyone had come over.
- **Where a person ends up and why.** Understood late for Lesca (item 14). Understood at once for Madame Etcheto: "Twelve", the chair, and "That's my door" three times before it.

Nothing was never understood. Item 16 is the only one I am not sure I read the way it was meant.

## 2. Errors

Total: **3**. All three are pronouns or articles. I found no typo, no spelling slip, no punctuation slip, no clock time or date in words, and Inguma is "it" everywhere.

**1.** Chapter 5. Reads as a slip for "her".

> Maylis had her legs back, and her mouth, and a left hand on the rail.

Corrected: "Maylis had her legs back, and her mouth, and her left hand on the rail."

**2.** Chapter 9. "She" and "her" between two women, with a third "her" just before.

> She sat down across the bread from her.

Corrected, joined to the sentence before: "Lesca was sitting in Maylis's chair at the kitchen table, with the left hand around a bowl of coffee, when Coralie came down in her dressing gown and sat down across the bread from her."

**3.** Chapter 12. "She" can be Maylis or Coralie.

> Maylis stood on the step with a bag of sand held against her good side and watched Coralie do eleven chairs before she looked up.

Corrected: "Maylis stood on the step with a bag of sand held against her good side, and Coralie did eleven chairs before she looked up."

Not counted: the pronoun in item 1 of part 1 ("It was a cellar") is the same kind of slip and is fixed by the change given there. Also not counted, because it is vocabulary and not spelling: "dish towel" (chapters 1, 5, 11), "baby carriage" (17) and "truck" (11, 14) are the American words in a text that is otherwise British.
