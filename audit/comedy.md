# Comedy

Read for this: `PLAN.md`, `bible.md`, `outline.md`, `manuscript/01.md` to `20.md`, `audit/cold-read.md` and `audit/laughs.md`. The first reader stopped at chapter 15, so chapters 16 to 19 carry my verdicts alone. Where the reader and I differ I say so. Whether any of this is funny is JC's call, and the two samples in `trial/` are there so that he can make it.

## 1. Diagnosis

### In short

The book is wry. It is funny in single dry lines that close a scene between two people, and it almost never lets a scene get out of hand. The first reader counted nine laughs in fifteen chapters and called the rest smiles. Two sequences build: the mairie counter in 13, across several beats, and the chrysanthemums in 15, across several beats and several people, each making it worse. Both come after the halfway mark.

The three hypotheses in the brief, tested against the text:

- **One device.** Half right. The book has two comedies. One is the narrator's: captions, tallies, running lines, comparisons from the job. The other is people with small wants talking: Jeannot, Kévin, Père Honoré, Sarthou, the secretary. The first is what wore out. The second works nearly every time it appears, and it appears as a two-hander with Maylis or Lesca standing still for it.
- **The merge flattened both.** Right for the daytime, wrong for the nights. By day most scenes carry a little of each register, so few go all the way in either. The nights are already a separate, graver book, and the reader felt the gear change each time. Where the flattening does damage is listed under "What the comedy costs the dark".
- **Almost nothing is funny as a situation.** Wrong as stated. Of the 131 comic intentions I list below, thirty are situations and twenty-six of them work. What is true is narrower. Three of the best reach the reader as a report after the event (the vote taken while the mayor sleeps in 12, the café's verdict in 17, the pump and the nets in 19). Most of the others end after one exchange. Only the counter and the chrysanthemums build.

One more finding, which the brief did not ask for. The village's secrets are in the bible and mostly not in the book. See "The secrets" below. Farce needs two people in a room who each have something to hide, and until Maïté's kitchen in chapter 15 the manuscript has no such room.

### What the nine laughs have in common

From `audit/laughs.md`: the timer and the rule made for one man (2), complaints heard in the mother's kitchen (4), the grandmother's rules (4), the landowner beaten by the winch (5), Sarthou at the microphone (7), Kévin looking up at the camera (8), the budget voted round a sleeping mayor (12), the letter that cannot be un-received (13), the chrysanthemums (15).

In every one a person with a want meets a rule, a machine or another person. None of the nine is a running line. None is a sum or a vote count. None is a comparison from the job.

The reader's list of things that tried and failed has twelve entries. Nine are repetitions: "still in the box", "I'll be brief", the timer, "not to worry", "the man from the commune", "nine on Sunday", "always hard", the comparison from the job, the vote counts. The pattern is plain enough to build the method on.

### What the comedy costs the dark

- **Office words in the nights.** Lesca's "procedure" appears with the creature on her chest in 2 and is "amended" in 4, 6 and 8. Her notebook closes "Next sitting tonight, 02:51." It is in character and the reader smiled. It also makes the creature an item of business from her first page, and it is the narrator's own joke about the council, moved into the bedroom. By day and in somebody's mouth the same joke is free ("Madame le maire minutes her nights now").
- **Three places where the reflex lands on a blow.** The fifty-seven euros at the red light (14). The initialled correction when she loses the village where she knocked (14). "May suspend the sitting" straight after the creature has held her standing (16).
- **The motion to remain seated (16).** The gravest decision in the book is written in the register of the marquee, so it reads as more procedure.
- **The numbers are spent.** The word "euros" occurs forty-three times in chapters 1 to 19 and there are at least eight vote counts. The reader had stopped reading figures by chapter 6. So when the flood night says "It was twenty-five minutes on foot to that hatch. The bank had ten", the book's one sum that counts arrives in a currency the reader has learned to skip. I think this is part of why JC found the flood night light. The other part comedy did not cause and can mend. In 19 the forty houses are shutters and the man is the sole of a wader. The reader weighs a man they have laughed with against houses they have never been inside. The loto in 7 is the one scene where the Port sits down in a room, and it is where the forty houses can get faces.
- **The laugh in 16 is not shared.** Leak 4 needs the reader to find Jeannot and his diesel funny before Lesca does.

### What the dark costs the comedy

- **Scenes are cut off by the rib.** The landowner fails at the winch and within two lines she cannot breathe (5). Sarthou's announcement turns to her washing cups (7). The bollard is played as alarm (11). Each cut is defensible. Together they mean no comic scene runs longer than a page.
- **She is a straight woman in pain.** In the comic two-handers the other person performs and she endures. She is allowed to be funny, and she is in four or five lines ("Put it in forty-two", "since you like the coffee", "Then be paid quietly until twelve"). There is room for more.
- **The closing line.** Most scenes end on one dry sentence. It is the habit the reader described as "one dry line that closes a scene. Nothing gets out of hand."

## 2. Method

Eight rules.

1. **A comic scene is a want hitting a rule or another want, in the room.** Before writing one, say in five words what each person in it wants. At least one of them is hiding why. If the laugh is in a sentence of the narrator's and would go if he kept quiet, the scene is not comic yet.

2. **Procedure belongs to the people.** It is a tool or a weapon in somebody's hand: the secretary's missing column, Larrère minuting her fingers, Marie-Jo's timer. The narrator states a rule once, plainly, when somebody is about to use it. He does not caption a joke the scene has made, offer a tally as a punchline, report afterwards a comic event that could be staged, or make more than one comparison from the job in a chapter. He still never winks, never calls anything absurd and never jokes.

3. **Let it get out of hand.** A comic scene has at least four beats, and a second and a third person make it worse. It ends on something done. A chapter marked for comedy has one such scene. Two-handers that end on one dry line are capped at two a chapter.

4. **A figure appears when somebody in the scene is counting it for a reason of their own.** "The fête has thirty-one euros" is Marie-Jo's defeat. "Clause 9 carries a hundred and fifty euros a breach" is nobody's. The outline's ration for 16 to 25 applies to the whole book: one vote count and two sums a chapter. Four numbers are never used in a joke: forty houses, one man, ten minutes, twenty-five minutes. Nor is 02:51.

5. **A running line gets three uses: plant, turn, pay.** Each return changes the speaker, the target or the meaning, and none is word for word. A chapter carries two running lines at most, one being planted or turned and one being paid. Once paid, a line is retired. Kévin's gutter (3, 9, 11) and "forty-two" (1, 5, 13, 25) show how it is done. The ledger below says what to do with the rest.

6. **The reader is let in before the room is.** Each village secret shows the reader a symptom well before anybody explains it. Comic scenes are built where two people with something to hide are in one room and neither can say why he wants what he wants. They accuse the nearest innocent to cover themselves. One secret is never played for comedy: the van at the end of the track.

7. **Maylis and Lesca are funny on purpose and are never the joke.** Each may be dry, petty and wrong. When the village has a theory about her, the scene shows that the theory is wrong and gives her a line that lands. No joke is on what she is, on Coralie, or on the two of them. And nothing in the book depends on a French word, a name or a pun. If a joke would not survive being acted in plain English, it goes.

8. **A comic scene leaves the reader more worried than it found them.** While the room laughs, one of four things moves. A person goes nearer the water. A warning is not heard. The one who could act is told to wait. Or a paper is fixed with her name on it. The means are these. A clock is visible in the scene: the gauge, a date, the first of January. Somebody who sleeps behind the dyke is in the room and is busy with something else. The laugh is at the thing that will do the harm. And the scene ends on what is now in motion, with the last joke one line before the end. The test is one sentence: after this scene, what is worse? If nothing is, the scene is a sketch, and it is cut or it is tied to the river.

### Neighbours

Village farce run with procedural seriousness has two well-known neighbours: *Clochemerle* (Chevallier, 1934), where a commune goes to war over a public urinal, and *Don Camillo* (Guareschi, 1948), a priest against a mayor. Both are farce all the way down. Here the farce has a drowned man under it and stops for him.

## 3. Tone map

### Rules for switching

1. **One register to a scene.** Mark it before writing. In a **comic** scene nobody is in danger and nobody is grieving in the present of the scene. In a **dark** scene nobody says a funny thing and the narrator uses no office word. A **held** scene is comic business with a death or a loss in the room, and it may carry one line that is both ("He'd have sent the mairie a registered letter about it"). Held scenes are where the book is most itself. A chapter has two such lines at most, or it is back in the even register.

2. **The switch falls on a break or on a hinge.** A hinge is something that happens: a rib stops a laugh, a timer rings, a telephone is answered, the creature arrives. It is never a turn of the narrator's phrase. One hinge a chapter.

3. **What may follow what.** Comic may go straight to dark, and it is the strongest move the book has, since the reader has just been laughing with the man in the cellar. Dark goes to comic only across a break with a change of place and hour, or through a held scene. The exception is a comic line that is the relief the dark was waiting for: Jeannot alive and swearing at five in the morning. No scene goes comic, dark, comic.

4. **Every chapter up to 22 has a scene in which the reader can laugh.** In most it is comic all the way through. In 11, 14 and 16, which each carry a leak or a loss, it is a held scene. In a Maylis chapter it is built on a villager's want. In a Lesca chapter it is built on somebody using her own rules against her, which is the engine those chapters already have (the timer, the kitchen complaints, the minuted fingers, the vote taken round her).

5. **The nights are dark.** That covers the creature, the steel room and the hall. Lesca's office words stay out of any paragraph where the creature is present. People may be funny about the nights by day.

6. **From the night of 19 the dark takes the book.** 19's night, 21 from the back door of the home, 22 from the front row and all of 23 have nothing comic in them. 24 is dark and its last line is held. 25 is dark and held. It has no comic scene. Its one laugh is that the letter has a number, and it comes from a man who is about to pick up a shovel.
