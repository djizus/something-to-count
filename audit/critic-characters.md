# Critic's report: Characters

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `25.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

**Verdict against the bar: not there yet.** The village is better company than anything in Crouch and stands beside The Good Place's ensemble. The two women, who are the premise, do not reach either comparison's central pair. They never share a scene, the one who sends the message never learns what it did, and the partner who carries the book is left on a landing in both worlds.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "It was twenty-five minutes on foot to that hatch. The bank had ten." (19) The sum is plain and personal. The second stake, one woman taking the other's body, never has a victim, because neither woman asks what becomes of the one she would displace.
- **Tension: 6.** "She looked down at her own hand on the wheel and counted that first, starting with the thumb." (3) That is a chapter ending, and chapters 1 to 7 mostly end at that pitch. From 13 onward it is level with Crouch.
- **Progression: 6.5.** "It was the night she had had on Thursday, and she had more of it." (15) Almost every scene is lived by one woman and then received by the other. The reader gets the loto, the medal and the hall twice, and the flood night three times (11, 15, 19).
- **Comedy: 7.** "There's nothing here that lets me un-receive it. There's no column." (13) The people are funny in themselves. Nobody argues the moral problem aloud, which is where The Good Place gets its laughs.
- **Characters: 7.** "I've waited nine months behind a bowl of apples for you to want it one way or the other." (17) The ensemble is an 8, Coralie higher. The pair is a 6.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "It was the laugh of a woman who has been docked for a morning's diesel by a man who then complained of her." (16) A joke from one world goes off in the other world's minute of silence. Nothing in the comparisons does this.
- **Prose: 7.** "She counted her fingers, both hands, and made ten." (21) Some form of that count appears 36 times across 20 chapters. The sentences are exact and all the same length.
- **The ending: 7.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (25) Jeannot and Maïté are paid in full. Coralie, Larrère and the two women's debt to each other are not.
- **Clarity: 6.** "The rain had not fallen there, the two skies not having matched since the June of the box." (14) The second half turns on that sentence, and it has to be read three times. The affair that explains the mayor's telephone call is given in one clause: "He came when Jeannot's van went down the lane, on a duck night." (24)

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes, but I nearly stopped in chapter 3. What kept me was one line in chapter 2: "He had been dead for twelve years less nineteen days."

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who likes a slow village comedy, yes, with a warning about the first seven chapters. On a Crouch reader, not as it stands.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**The village: above Crouch, level with The Good Place.** Crouch's townspeople are functions. Here each villager is wrong in a private way and has a habit that shows it. Larrère lowers his window "a few centimetres". Marie-Jo addresses the wall "through the chair". Arlette has "stopped asking". Sarthou retires "with my files shut". Jeannot numbers his grievances. That is the Good Place method, and it works.

**The partner: above Crouch as a person, below him as a marriage.** Daniela in Dark Matter is a prize to get back to. Coralie has her own twelve-year secret (the call log in 20) and her own evasion ("I wanted you in the house. I thought I could have the one without the other, if I kept a landing between."). But Crouch spends his first chapter making the reader want the marriage. This book never shows the marriage wanted, in either world.

**The two women: below both.** Dark Matter turns the other self into a person with a face, and their meeting is the climax. The Good Place makes people find out what their actions did; that is the engine of every reversal. Here the other self is a notebook entry, the two never face each other, and the one reversal that would re-read the whole book (what the five words cost) is kept from the woman who sent them.

**The questions of the lens, answered directly.**

- *Distinct?* By circumstance, yes: rain and frost, rib and seam, shopping pad and notebook, "Maylis" and "Lesca". By voice, no. See fault 5.
- *Wanted-for?* Each wants the other's life, and the wanting is unflattering and good: "*I want that.*" (11); "She wanted that village all the same." (16)
- *Softened or excused?* Lesca is, twice. Her confession is followed within thirty lines by two other people's guilt ("Then that's the gendarme, and you. And I'll give you the third."). And she is spared knowing what her message did. Maylis is not softened in the act (the blackmail in 11 is left ugly), but she is cushioned afterwards. See faults 1 and 6.
- *Described from outside as a woman?* No. Wherever it happens a villager is doing it and the joke is on him: "Is the man from the commune about?" (1), "Monsieur le Maire stands here" (22), "she had always been hard. In the same week it called Larrère a character" (6).
- *Who would be missed?* Jeannot first, then Marie-Jo, Arlette, Sarthou, Monsieur Daguerre. I missed Kévin from chapter 13 to the end.
- *Which relationship carries the book, and does it pay?* Coralie's, in both worlds. It does not pay in either.
- *Does the reader care who lives?* For Jeannot, completely. For Coralie, yes, and the book builds that fear in 21 ("Be in the house.") and then does not use it. For Madame Etcheto, only from chapter 21, two chapters before she dies.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Lesca never learns what her five words did.** Chapter 24. "Nothing had come after she stood up. She did not know whether five words had got across, or what a woman in a van had done with them."

The reader has just watched those words sink a van, break a bank, flood forty houses and drown a woman in room twelve. Lesca then confesses a twelve-year-old act, is relieved ("Nothing came onto her breastbone"), and the book treats her account as settled. She also suspects she sent the rain itself: she writes "*24 November*" at the foot of a page (18) and "gave it no heading" (22). The book lets her leave both unheaded. She is excused from the only thing she does in the present.

*What I would do.* Let the result cross. She wakes on Saturday with the hall in her head: forty chairs filled, the last one empty, Coralie's voice saying "Forty-one". Then the speech scene needs no new words. She reads "No one came to tell him" a metre from Madame Etcheto, alive, with her lips moving, having drowned her in the other village that morning. The line "she looked along the row once, as far as the old woman at the end, and stopped reading" finally has a reason. The date at the foot of the page gets its heading. It costs about three hundred words and it is the reversal the book has built and not sprung.

**2. The two women have no scene together, and the body-theft has no victim.** Chapters 16, 17, 22, 23. "On that side it had a back as well, turned to where somebody else might have stood, and nobody stood there." (23)

Both women plan to take the other's bed. Lesca lists what she would leave behind ("The first deputy would act, and the speech was typed. Kévin Labat could be shown the Castagnet roof.") and never asks where the other woman would go. Maylis does the same on the back of a form. Then each gives up the plan alone, in the same room, at the same minute, without seeing the other. The five words arrive "as she would have had a line off a notice, read once in passing". The central relationship of the premise is conducted entirely by overhearing.

*What I would do.* At 02:51 they are standing either side of the thing in the chair. Let each see the other once: the forearm held against the rib, the right hand shut on the seam. Let Maylis hear the five words said, in her own voice, by someone looking at her. And in chapter 16 give Lesca one line item she cannot cost: the woman whose bed it is. A mayor moving a motion to evict her other self is the Good Place joke this book has not yet made.

**3. Coralie is not paid in either world.** Chapters 24 and 25. "Coralie had not looked down. She had heard the line. She knew now which had been done with it." That is the last of Lesca's Coralie. And: "Maylis heard her on the landing, and a door, and did not listen for which." That is the last of Maylis's.

The book asks one question for twenty-five chapters: will the pillow cross the landing. One unanswered ending is a choice. Two is avoidance.

*What I would do.* Pay Lesca's. After Maïté's kitchen she comes home, and the scene promised since chapter 4 happens on the landing. Leave Maylis's door unheard; that line is good enough to keep.

**4. The marriage is never shown as something to lose.** Chapter 1 onward. "Coralie found Maylis's wrist and laid two fingers on the inside of it and watched the clock."

Every touch between them in both worlds is clinical: a pulse, two fingers on a rib, a dressing, a strapping. We are told "sixteen years" and shown boxes on a shopping pad. A reader cannot fear the loss of something never seen.

*What I would do.* One scene before chapter 5 where they are good at each other, and funny with it. In Lesca's world, one remembered moment from the five years of first names. The coat in 10 ("He put the pin through the lining. I mended it.") is the nearest the book comes, and it shows what is missing.

**5. The two women think and speak in one voice, and Lesca's own voice is withheld.** Whole book. "It was forty houses and a care home against three hundred hectares of grass, and it took her under a minute." (10) "She spoke against it, and it took her under a minute." (12)

Four times we are told the mayor's answer took under a minute. We never hear the minute. Lesca is a politician who won two elections, and on the page she is as terse as the woman who cannot say what she wants: "It's the rib." (1) / "I went once." (6). Kévin gives the difference in one line, "You'd not have pushed it back. You'd have filmed me holding it out" (12), but the women's own sentences do not show it.

*What I would do.* Let Lesca talk. Give the forty-houses minute in full, once, early (the dyke in 6 is the place). Let her speak first, interrupt and over-answer. Maylis's liveliest moment in the book is when Lesca's voice comes out of her by mistake: "I want her in my office at nine." (17) That contrast should be available from chapter 2.

**6. The cost falls on the softest victim, who arrives too late to be missed.** Chapters 21, 23, 25. "She said she'd not go out to a minibus in her nightdress, and to come back when I had her coat." (25)

Madame Etcheto is ninety-four, she chose to go back to her room, and she refused to leave it. Coralie then takes the death on herself: "That's mine." Jeannot arrives with a letter in Maylis's favour. Chapter 25 gives 1,330 words to a drowned village; the loto had 2,575. Maylis has no line of her own about any of it. Before chapter 21, Etcheto is a name in Coralie's mouth, a duck on a lap and a face at a window.

*What I would do.* Keep her as the one who dies; the words, the counting and the empty chair make her right. Give her a scene with Maylis in the first ten chapters. Remove one cushion, either the coat or "That's mine". Give Maylis her own count in 25: opening the gate at 02:55 would have kept forty houses dry.

**7. Larrère carries the real guilt and gets no scene.** Chapters 24 and 25. "Guy Larrère was at the back, under the fourth tree."

In both worlds he gave the order from another man's kitchen, and in both he leaves the book without a word. He does not appear in 25 at all. For twenty-three chapters he is a voice through a gap in a car window. The reveal then asks the reader to believe a woman let him into her kitchen for four years, and nothing shows what she saw in him.

*What I would do.* One scene on foot, out of the car, with no gap to speak through. In 25 that is the Port, in the silt. And one sentence from Maïté about what he was to her.

**8. Kévin disappears after chapter 12 with three debts open.** "The summer's accounts had come out three thousand four hundred euros short. Kévin had had the key to the box in July, and Larrère had countersigned them as they stood and asked nothing." (7)

He gives both women the method, his jacket is the image of the exchange, and he is not in the book's last nine chapters. The missing money is never mentioned again. Nor is where a man who nets at the gates every night was when the bank went.

*What I would do.* Put him in the water in 23 with his van, and in 25. Pay the accounts thread or cut it.

**9. Silence and finger-counting have become method.** "Maylis did not answer." (3) "Lesca did not answer." (18, 22, 24)

"Did not answer", "did not say", "did not ask" and "said nothing" occur 25 times. The finger count occurs 36 times. After chapter 8 the count tells the reader nothing new, and the non-answers make three different people (Maylis, Lesca, Coralie) silent in the same way.

*What I would do.* Keep the count where it turns something: the signature (5), the minuting (6), the laugh (16). Cut two-thirds of the rest. Replace half the non-answers with wrong answers, as "I've the ponies to do" (15) already does.

**10. Two people the reader depends on have no names.** "The secretary took off her glasses. She had been at that counter since 2003." (13)

The secretary speaks in eight chapters and has the best procedural lines in the book. The first deputy moves the motion against the mayor. Neither is named, and a reader cannot miss a job title.

Slips noticed on the way:

- Madame Etcheto "was in twelve" in December 2014 (22) but has "slept in room twelve for nine years" in 2026 (21).
- In 8, two speeches run together on one line: "'I'm saying it to you. I'm not saying it to the mairie.' 'Tell Madame le maire the fête committee wants the hall keys...'"
- In 10, "The secretary looked up. He had not been thanked in that room since April 2015" reads as if the secretary were Larrère.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The bedroom in chapter 20.** "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." Coralie has known the minute for twelve years and never asked, "Because you'd have told me."
2. **Jeannot's letters, start to finish.** "Put it in forty-two." "It's getting long." "'Who's dead?' said Jeannot Castagnet." Letter 42 brought by hand, and the second shovel.
3. **The ledger in chapter 18.** Received, Removed, "*Nil.*" "She could go there to knock. She could not go there to forget." It is the best piece of thinking either woman does.
4. **Arlette setting foot in the mairie (22).** "I've given it my ponies. There were eleven, and they went up a ramp, and I counted them."
5. **Monsieur Daguerre's "Here" and what it sets up.** The call at the loto (7), the call at each sandbag and then "Nobody called" (21), the last chair empty and "Forty-one" (23).
