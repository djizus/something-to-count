# Critic's report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `25.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

Verdict against the bar: below it. The book is drily witty on almost every page and funny out loud on almost none. Its construction is at the level of the comparison; its volume is not.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Either you opened it for forty houses, and I live with the mayor. Or you opened it because I was in that building." (20) The stakes are there and personal, but no clock runs on them until chapter 14.
- **Tension: 6.** "It came at 02:51, and she let it have her. At four she walked the yard. At twenty-five past she went in by the red figures and counted five and five." (19) That sentence, near enough, opens a dozen nights. Chapter 23 ("02:59. She got out and ran.") shows what the book can do when it stops repeating it.
- **Progression: 5.** "*There are two of us. She is the mayor.*" (9) Maylis writes this at 39% of the book. The reader has known it since the first line of chapter 2.
- **Comedy: 6.** "There's nothing here that lets me un-receive it. There's no column." (13) This is the top of the range, and it is a smile.
- **Characters: 8.** "I've waited nine months behind a bowl of apples for you to want it one way or the other." (17)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 7.** "He thinks it's the loto. Leave him. He's been one off the strimmer since November." (21) The switch is clean, partly because the comedy never raises its voice and so never has far to travel.
- **Prose: 8.** "It ran into her ears. She had not cried since the funeral, where she had not been entitled to." (10)
- **The ending: 8.** "But they're saying you went for a walk, and I was what you walked to. That goes in writing." (25)
- **Clarity: 5.** "The rain had not fallen there, the two skies not having matched since the June of the box." (14) The sentence the whole plot turns on is a subordinate clause. A reader working out which village she is in is not laughing.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. I would have stalled between 8 and 10 if the loto had not been behind me.

**Would I press it on a friend?** Not on the friend the bar describes. That friend expects to laugh aloud by page five and to be in the premise by page thirty, and gets neither.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, on comedy.** Three things put it there.

1. **Rate and range.** The Good Place has four people who are funny in four different ways, and a joke every few seconds in several registers: silly, cruel, physical, verbal. This book has one register, the dry procedural understatement closed by a flat one-line topper, and every villager speaks it. It produces steady amusement and no peak. The loudest the book gets is a man calling "Here" at the wrong moment.

2. **The metaphysics is not funny.** The Good Place makes its rules the funniest thing in it. Here the rules of crossing (four o'clock, the lamp, the palms, the nine breaths) are delivered as solemn ritual a dozen times. The only person with a comic view of them is Kévin ("People on the forum would pay for that"), and he leaves the book at chapter 12.

3. **Level on the reversal that re-reads.** Larrère's order turns out to have been telephoned from Castagnet's kitchen (24). That re-reads the ticking clock behind his voice, "A man's wife is no business of the commune's", the window going all the way down, and the whole chrysanthemum farce, in which the village suspected the wrong visitor. This is as good as the comparison. So is the way each comic runner becomes an instrument of the disaster: "Here", the timer, the counting, "Forty-one".

**On making the moral problem funny without making it small:** it keeps the problem large and makes only its surroundings funny. The village's handling of the choice is comic (three sittings on a verb, a firework bull on four families' floors, "It is not a tap, Madame le maire"). The choice itself is never joked about until Jeannot's "I told you not to knock" in 23 and "what you walked to" in 25, which are the two best lines of that kind and come in the last three chapters. The Good Place gets blood on Chidi in the trolley; this book keeps its hands clean for 22 chapters.

Against Crouch the comparison is one of pace. Crouch is not funny, but he moves too fast for the reader to notice. This book moves slowly enough that the comedy has to carry chapters 3 to 13, and it is too quiet to carry them.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The farce engine is rationed. (Structural; 5 to 18.)**
The book has one comic idea nobody else has: a woman behaving in her own village on the facts of the other. "'It's the sixth of December. Nobody carries those after the second of November unless there's a hearse. Where are you going with them?'" (15). Every funniest moment comes from it: the prefect's signature on a delivery slip (5), snubbing Kévin over a dream (7), "Thank you, Guy" (10), the chrysanthemums (15), "I want her in my office at nine" (17), "Are you on earlies tomorrow?" (18). That is seven firings in 25 chapters. Only the chrysanthemums are public and allowed to compound, and on the mayor's side each slip is punished by the creature within a sentence, so it never gets to be funny.
*Do:* make this the engine from chapter 5. Give each woman three or four more slips, in public, with consequences that pile up. Maylis picks up the timer, or signs the road-sign form with the flourish, or tells Larrère his three minutes are up. Lesca reaches for a key that is not on her belt, or turns up to do Kévin's job. Let the village keep a tally of them, as Lafitte does of the pot. Let two of the mayor's slips run a full scene before the creature comes.

**2. One voice for everyone. (Structural; throughout.)**
"I know what it took. I counted it." (Marie-Jo, 6). "I know what he was paid. He told me." (Père Honoré, 6). "I know what the commune pays." (Kévin, 3). "I know what a coincidence is." (Coralie, 20). "I know what was there to be seen." (Sarthou, 24). The construction appears seventeen times from eight speakers. Everyone also counts: chairs, congregants, ponies, pulses, sandbags, fingers. The motif is deliberate, and the cost is that every person is funny in the same way and every scene ends on a number. Only Larrère ("I'll be brief") and Kévin ("I'd fly") are funny in themselves.
*Do:* give the retort to one owner, Marie-Jo, and take it from the rest. Make one person a talker, one a liar, one who gets things wrong aloud. Jeannot should be the talker: he has written forty-one letters and speaks in four-line turns.

**3. The crossings are a ritual with no comic variation. (Structural; 6 to 19.)**
"It came at 02:51, and Lesca let it have her. The alarm went at four. She got up, by the rule, and stayed up." (8). The same procedure is narrated in 8, 9, 10, 11, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19 and 22. Fingers are counted and "made ten" or "five and five" thirty-three times. It is roughly a seventh of the book, and nothing in it is funny.
*Do:* after the second crossing, cut the approach to one line. Spend the saved words on fault 1. And use what is already there: the mayor treating a haunting as mairie business is the book's second-best joke ("Item delivered three times, complete"; a stock sheet headed Received and Removed; "That the mayor remain seated") and is played entirely straight. Let it be funny.

**4. Kévin leaves at chapter 12 and never comes back. (Structural.)**
"I got as high as a gutter. What have you got, Maylis?" (11). He is the funniest person in the book, the only comic lens on the premise, and the one man whose trade puts him at that gate on a night tide with a van. He does not speak in the last thirteen chapters in either village. "I'd fly" is never paid.
*Do:* account for him on the Friday night, on the dyke. He has a van that could tow one out of a dip, or he is at Irun with it. Give him a beat in 25.

**5. Larrère is offstage for his own reversal. (Structural; 24, 25.)**
"He rang you from here." (24). The comic antagonist of 24 chapters is exposed twice, once in a widow's flat speech and once in the summary of a letter, and he is on the page for neither. His last appearances are a car window in 21 and "at the back, under the fourth tree" in 24. The Good Place gives the reveal to the man revealed.
*Do:* one short scene in each village. In 25, his car in the square with the window up and letter 42 on its way to his own tray. In 24, Lesca passing him on her way down from the kitchen. Let him say "I'll be brief" once more and have nothing.

**6. The bull never runs. (Structural; 2, 6, 12, 16, 20, 24.)**
"He couldn't abide fireworks. They put the duck off for a week." (6). Nine thousand euros of firework bull is voted in 2, sited in 6, cut and restored in 12, tested one horn at a time in 20, and is "three hours" off in 24. The chapter ends and the book moves to the other village. A commemoration with an eleven-thousand-euro bar is the mayor's side's one grotesque set piece, and it is withheld.
*Do:* light it. Lesca goes up the ladder to look at the gutter and sees it go through six thousand people on the dyke, and Maïté's line about the duck pays.

**7. Permission to laugh comes late and quietly. (Structural; 1, 2.)**
"Marie-Jo Labat, president of the fête committee, set a white kitchen timer to one minute and stood it on the table, and the room listened to it." (2). A minute's silence on a kitchen timer is a very good joke, and it is delivered among frost, a widow and a stone, so the reader does not know laughing is allowed. Chapter 1 runs nine hundred words of suffocation before the first comic line. The first chapter that is actually funny is 3.
*Do:* get to the square inside four hundred words. The postman looking past a woman with a rake for "the man from the commune" is the book declaring what it is; it belongs on page two.

**8. The best comic situations happen offstage or in summary. (12, 16, 19.)**
"He did it at length and without repeating himself. He began with the commune and went back through her father to the association of 1962" (19). That is a comic aria reported in two sentences. The same is done to the council that passes the opposition's amendment over its sleeping mayor (12, reconstructed afterwards), to the café's two versions of the laugh (16, "By noon the café had it, in two versions"), and to Larrère's three minutes (2).
*Do:* play at least two of them. Jeannot's swearing down the telephone at five in the morning is the last laugh before the flood and should be heard.

**9. Scenes stop on a button where they should build. (Throughout.)**
"'It wants grease,' he said. / 'It's had grease since 2013.'" (5). Seven landowners are on that dyke with a theory each; two try the winch, and the scene closes. The pattern is set-up, dry exchange, topper, section break, at three to six hundred words. The loto (7) is the only comic scene that runs long and compounds, and it is the best chapter in the first half for that reason.
*Do:* let three scenes run to loto length: the winch (5), the mairie counter (13), the café adding up twenty minutes in a kitchen (17).

**10. The narrator explains the joke; one echo cheapens a death. (3, 11, 16, 25.)**
"It was the laugh of a woman who has been docked for a morning's diesel by a man who then complained of her." (16). The reader had the diesel in 1, 10 and 14 and is laughing already. Also "'I will put it with the roof,' he said, and she understood that both were now prayed for" (3), and "She had done it with something that was not hers" (11). Separately: "'Forty-one,' she said, to the stack" (25) reuses the last line of 23 as a chair-count six hundred words later, and takes something from it.
*Do:* cut the three explanations. Cut Marie-Jo's "Forty-one".

On the two questions the lens asks directly. A joke weakens a dark moment once, in fault 10. The dark smothers the joke across the whole mayor's side until chapter 12, in the night rituals, and in chapter 2.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The loto (7).** The baby counted to make ninety-one, "The person unknown had forty cups to wash and washed two of them", Daguerre's "Here", the cut on the ham.
2. **The mairie counter (13).** "So I've taken it back and you've kept it." / "Yes." / "That's the mairie." And "forty-two is spoken for".
3. **The chrysanthemums, as far as the telephone (15), and what the café and Larrère make of them (17).** "She said, 'Chrysanthemums.' She said, 'To the house.' Then a chair scraped on the tiles. She had sat down to it, in her coat." And the window going down as far as it went.
4. **The words and the counting (21, 22).** Madame Etcheto counting nineteen sandbags with Daguerre calling "Here" at each, and Arlette giving it her ponies: "It can go and find where they are now. That should keep it till morning."
5. **The timer on "did you" (20) and the last page (25).** The rule made against Larrère saving her from his question; then letter 42 by hand, "It's four sides, and it doesn't thank you", and the waders going into the grave.
