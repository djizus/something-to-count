# Critic's report: prose

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `25.md`, in order, in full, about 57,000 words. Nothing else. Counts below are whole-manuscript counts taken from the chapter files.

Verdict against the bar: **below it for the reader named in the brief**, and the cause is on the surface of the page. The sentences are better made than Crouch's. The book is harder to follow than Crouch at every point where a Crouch reader needs to be told something plainly, and the middle repeats one procedure until the eye slides.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes, but from chapter 3 to chapter 7 I was reading for the sentences and not for the story, and a reader who does not read for sentences stops there.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On the friend who reads Penelope Fitzgerald, yes. On the friend who reads Dark Matter, no, not as it stands. That friend is the one the bar names.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Against Crouch: below, on the two things his prose is for.**

1. *Crouch says the premise in a sentence a reader can repeat at dinner, early, and says it again.* This book says it once, in chapter 1, inside a paragraph that is explaining a village joke, in the passive, through a paraphrase of a council minute. It never says it again in plain words. The mechanism of the divergence gets one absolute clause in chapter 14.
2. *A Crouch chapter ends on an open question.* Here chapters 1 to 8, 10 and 12 end on a closed gesture: a window goes up, a card is stood against a clock, a door is shut, a sitting rises. Each is well judged and each lets the reader put the book down. The hooks arrive at 9, 11, 13 and 14, and from 19 they do not miss.
3. *Where it is above him:* the echo. A phrase from one village turns up, word for word, in the other, and re-reads what came before: "shouted for the ham" (7, 8), "of the kind that hangs in a kitchen" (19, 23), "a round reflector, screwed on crooked" (11, 15, 19, 21). Crouch gets his re-reads from plot. This book gets them from sentences, and that is the harder trick.

**Against The Good Place: below on voices, level on the gag that turns.**

1. One line from Eleanor, Chidi, Tahani or Jason needs no attribution. Here cover the speaker's name and Marie-Jo, Arlette, Coralie, Sarthou and Kévin share one syntax and one comeback (fault 7). Only Père Honoré (no contractions) and Larrère ("They tell me", "I'll be brief") can be picked out blind.
2. The show's jokes come back as plot. So do these: the three-minute timer cuts Larrère off on "did you" in chapter 20; "still in the box" is retired in chapter 25; "Put it in forty-two" is delivered by hand at a grave. That is level with the show.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. The premise is never said in plain words (ch 1, ch 14). Structural.

> "It was to establish whether a person shut in a sealed room, who has seen one of two lamps light, is for those outside two persons until the door is opened. The café had it as the cat that is alive and dead until somebody lifts the lid, with Maylis for the cat. [...] The woman in charge had called her the friend all afternoon, which was what the one inside was called in the argument the thing came from." (ch 1)

> "The rain had not fallen there, the two skies not having matched since the June of the box." (ch 14)

The first is a legal paraphrase with the subject held back to the end, followed by "the argument the thing came from", which names nothing. A reader with no physics takes away "some experiment, a joke about a cat". The second is the only sentence in the book that says the two villages have had different weather since 2014, which is why one has had its flood and one has not, which is the whole plot. It is an absolute construction hung off a negative.

What I would do: give the premise to the one person in the book who talks plainly. Chapters 3 and 9 are the clearest pages in the manuscript because Kévin explains things in his own mouth ("In a dream the hand comes out wrong. Six, or seven, or they won't keep still."). Let him, or Dr Kessler in a remembered line, say it in twenty words: two lamps, one comes on, she saw one, and the question was whether somewhere she saw the other. Say it by the end of chapter 3. Then at chapter 9 and chapter 14 have a character restate where things stand in a sentence a child could repeat. And rewrite the "two skies" sentence as three plain ones: since June 2014 the weather has not been the same in both; there it stayed dry that December; this is the first wet one.

### 2. The way in is narrated over and over (ch 6 to ch 22). Structural.

> "It came at 02:51, and Maylis let it take her. The phone buzzed at four. She walked the yard in the rain, and at twenty past she lay down on her back." (ch 11)

> "It came at 02:51, and she let it have her. At four she walked the yard. At twenty-five past she went in by the red figures and counted five and five." (ch 19)

The sequence is: 02:51, let it, up at four, twenty minutes, lie down, red figures, count fingers, read the card, warm chair. It is walked through in chapters 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 15 and 19 and summarised in 12, 13, 14, 16, 17 and 18. "02:51" appears 20 times, "red figures" 14, "read the card" 9, a finger count ending "made ten", "made five" or "five and five" 24 times, "before she moved" 11. By chapter 12 the reader knows the liturgy and skims it, and what is skimmed is the paragraph that leads into each chapter's new material.

What I would do: full dress three times only. Chapter 6 (first entry), chapter 8 or 9 (the method), chapter 22 (the one that fails). Everywhere else one clause: "She went in at half past four." Hourly finger counts by day: keep the ones that are seen by someone (the dyke vote, the café, Sarthou's door) and cut the ones that are only reported. This is the largest single cut available in the middle third, and it costs nothing the reader has not already been given.

### 3. The thing has no noun, and "it" collides with everything else called "it" (ch 14, 16, 22, 24).

> "The motion had one fault. It had fetched her back that morning after nine breaths. To stay gone she would have to keep it off her" (ch 16)

> "The light went red again. She took hold of it. She fixed on the one red lamp, past the edge of its back" (ch 14)

> "He had it eleven minutes before she did." (ch 24)

In the first, grammar says the motion fetched her back. In the second, "it" is the light, then "its back" is the creature's, with nothing between. In the third, "it" is the sight of a van, in a book where "it" has meant the creature for 24 chapters. Chapter 22 does this for a whole page: the chair, the seat, the cold and the creature are all "it".

Withholding the name is right for chapter 1. As the standing rule of the narration it puts the book's central object behind a pronoun at exactly the moments the rules are being stated. The women name it in chapters 3 and 4 (Inguma, Mauma). What I would do: after chapter 4 let the narrator use one fixed noun, sparingly, and adopt a hard rule that "it" means the creature only when no other neuter singular noun has appeared since the last paragraph break.

### 4. The reader has to work out which woman, and which "she" (ch 4, 5, 6, 7, 13, 23, 24).

> "Coralie stayed a little longer. Then she went back across the landing. She left the light on and, for the first time since 2015, her own door open. / She had changed two things." (ch 4)

> "On Thursday she had stood in that doorway and looked at the notebook, and then at the pen. At eight Lesca drove to the mairie" (ch 6)

> "In March nobody had stood against him. She tipped out the tin and built the coins into piles of ten along the trestle. / 'Four hundred and twenty, with the door,' she said." (ch 7)

In the first, the new paragraph's "She" is Lesca with Coralie as the live antecedent. In the second, "she" is Coralie and the reason the notebook goes into the safe is left for the reader to assemble. In the third, I cannot tell which woman speaks the total.

The larger version: the two protagonists are told apart by one device, "Maylis" in the odd chapters and "Lesca" in the even, and nobody tells the reader that is the rule. Chapters 5, 13, 23 and 24 open on a bare "she". And from chapter 11 Jeannot calls the employee "Lesca" in dialogue ("Both done, Lesca.", "What are you sat here for, Lesca?"), so the mayor's name is live in the wrong village. The drop from "little Lesca" to "Lesca" is too good to lose. So fix the other end: put the name, or "the mayor", in the first sentence of every chapter without exception, and in the first sentence after every section break where another woman was the last subject.

### 5. The scene's meaning is restated afterwards, and the central rule is stated five times from one mould (ch 11, 16, 18, 20, 24).

> "It was the first thing in Peyrebarthe that had been done because she said so. She had done it with something that was not hers." (ch 11)
> "She had laughed with what was not hers, and it had found her before Maïté was off the stairs." (ch 16)
> "She had said a thing that was not hers to say, in her own kitchen, and it had been across the table before the pencil was down." (ch 18)
> "It did not come by day for what was her own." (ch 20)
> "It did not come for what was her own." (ch 24)

The rule is good and the reader needs it. Five statements in the same words is the narrator checking that the reader got it. Chapters 16 and 18 are also the same scene twice (she uses what is not hers, "She told her mouth to open", "It was on her. It had not come down off anything", timed, "little finger").

The same habit closes scenes that had already landed:

> "She could go there to knock. She could not go there to forget." (ch 18, after "*Nil.*" has done it)
> "It had not got smaller for being said. It was in the kitchen now, with two people who knew its weight." (ch 24)
> "That was what the sash had cost. [...] She had its price now, to the minute." (ch 19)
> "It was why she mended the roof." (ch 15)

What I would do: state the rule once in the narrator's voice, at chapter 16, the first time it bites in public. At 18 show it without the gloss. At 20 and 24 keep the bare wardrobe and the unlocked knees and cut the sentence. Cut the four closers above. In chapter 24 end the paragraph on "She had come to pay it where it was owed."

### 6. Feeling is reported as a list of things not done (whole book).

> "Coralie did not touch her." (ch 20) "She did not take out her pen." (ch 20) "Lesca did not answer." (ch 22, 24) "She did not sit." (ch 23) "She did not push." (ch 3, 9, 15, 18) "She was not kissed." (ch 24)

"did not" appears 168 times, "She did not" 53. Counting had not, was not, could not and the rest there are about 370 negated verbs, one every 150 words. Eighteen of 113 sections end on a negation. Singly these are the book's reticence and many are its best beats (Larrère at the loto: "He did not turn round."). At this rate the reader learns to hear the construction, and in the two scenes that need the most, the bedroom in chapter 20 and Maïté's kitchen in chapter 24, the narrator has nothing left but one more refusal.

What I would do: halve them. Keep a "did not" only where the not-doing is the event of the paragraph. Where it is stage direction ("He did not look at her", "She did not turn round"), cut it or say what the person did.

### 7. The whole village answers back in one sentence (ch 3 to ch 25).

> "'I know what the commune pays.'" (Kévin, ch 3)
> "'I know what it took. I counted it.'" (Marie-Jo, ch 6)
> "'I know what he was paid. He told me.'" (Père Honoré, ch 6)
> "'I know what an employee can't do. I read it to you in your own kitchen.'" (Marie-Jo, ch 7)
> "'I know how many came. I counted them.'" (Marie-Jo, ch 12)
> "'I know the sum. You gave it me in court.'" (Kévin, ch 12)
> "'I know where he is.'" (Larrère, ch 23) "'I know where you were.'" (Coralie, ch 25)

Twenty-one retorts on "I know what/how/where/the". Beside it run "I'm not asking. I'm telling you." (six, across Arlette, Coralie and Jeannot) and "I'm saying it to you. I'm not saying it to the mairie." It is a good comeback and it belongs to one person. Give it to Marie-Jo, who counts, and make the others find their own way of refusing to be told. Kévin's natural move is the shrug ("I looked up. That's all there is to say about it."). Sarthou's is the file. Coralie's is the measurement refused ("Don't give me the measurement.").

### 8. There is a number in almost every sentence, and the numbers that matter drown (whole book; worst in ch 2, 7, 12).

> "She ordered one string of two hundred warm-white lamps, twenty metres, for outdoor use, at thirty-eight euros ninety, with delivery by Friday for nine euros more." (ch 3)

Across the manuscript there is a figure every 27 words. In chapter 7 it is every 18. The administrative tally is the voice and I would not change the voice. But the reader is asked to carry 02:51, nineteen turns, forty-one letters, twenty-six letters, ten minutes against twenty-five, and to feel "Forty-one" as a blow. They are carrying those through a crowd.

Worse, three numbers are used for everything. "Eleven" appears 77 times: eleven ponies, eleven wreaths, eleven plates, eleven nights of film, eleven panels, eleven barrows, eleven kilometres, eleven thousand euros, the confession took eleven minutes, the medal item takes eleven minutes, she stands at the stone eleven minutes, Sarthou had it eleven minutes before her, the council rises at eleven minutes past eight. "Nine" 82 times. "Forty" and its compounds 97. I could not tell which of these were meant to chime and which were habit, and a motif the reader cannot tell from a habit is not working.

What I would do: decide which numbers the book is about and protect them. Change the incidental elevens, nines and forties to anything else. Cut prices and measurements that no one acts on.

### 9. Four audible moulds, and one change of narrator (whole book; ch 6, ch 23).

- **"looked at A, and at B."** "looked at" 111 times; the paired form about 29. "He looked at the label, and at her." (5) "She looked at him, and then at the clipboard." (7) "He considered the stone, and the mairie, and her hands." (8) "He considered the fifty-four bags, and the gendarme, and her." (19) "He looked at the string, and at the cut turf, and at how she held the spade." (25). It is the book's stand-in for a reaction. Keep one in three.
- **"as she would have".** Ten, plus two "as he would have": "as she would have watched a man walk on a frozen pond" (13), "as she would have noticed a tooth gone" (17), "as she would have gone down a till that would not balance" (18), "as she would have read it off a chart" (20), "as she would have given it to a doctor" (25). Each is good. Coralie gets the same one twice in five chapters.
- **"Then" to open a sentence:** 128 times. With no semicolon and no dash anywhere in the manuscript and 1,083 uses of ", and", the prose has one way of joining two things and one way of moving on. Sentence length averages between 8.5 and 11.5 words in every chapter from 3 onwards. It is even, and after 40,000 words even is flat.
- **Chapter openers.** Eighteen of 25 begin "On Monday / On Friday / On Saturday". Four begin "At nine minutes to three". The dateline gives the day and withholds the woman, which is the wrong way round (fault 4).
- **Voice changes.** (a) Chapter 6 slips into the present: "The minutes of that meeting record six votes for the plots. They also record that during the vote the mayor counted her fingers and found five." Nowhere else does the chronicler stand in the reader's time. (b) Chapter 23 changes hands: timestamps on their own lines, verbless fragments ("Three minutes out. Two to get him up a ladder. Three back."), and two similes of a kind the book has not used, "The five words lay across the sum like a hand" and "the clay moved under her palm like the flank of an animal". The speed is right and the timestamps are earned by 24 chapters of clock. The two "like" similes are from another, softer novel; the book's own similes are working ones (a sack, a trailer, a till). Cut both. (c) The narrator enters other heads only when a joke wants it: "He had seen mayors." (20) "She had been told a great many things at six in the morning, at the home, by people who believed them." (13). I would keep these, but they make the narrator's silence about Coralie and Larrère elsewhere look like a choice, so be sure it is one.

### 10. Usage and consistency (list).

- **"deliberation"** (8 uses: "by a deliberation she had signed", "no deliberation covered it"). In English it means discussion. Here it means a resolution voted by the council. A reader without French reads the wrong word every time. Use "resolution", or gloss it once in chapter 1.
- **"a hand of water"** (9 uses, first in ch 1: "I had a hand of water at the tonne"). Never explained. It carries Jeannot's whole grievance. One clause on first use: a hand's depth.
- **"the code"**, "prevented", "the brigade", "the men in green", "through the chair": each is left to context. "Prevented" is handled perfectly (ch 12 makes the gloss the joke). Do the same for the others or cut them.
- **phone / telephone.** Maylis's mobile is "the phone" in chapters 3, 9, 11 and 15 and "the telephone" from 19 on ("Her telephone was under the pillow"). Pick one.
- **headlights / headlamps.** "Headlights" twice in chapter 1, "headlamps" nine times after. The reflector scenes depend on the word. Make chapter 1 match.
- **Maire / maire.** "Monsieur le Maire" capitalised four times (ch 1, 2, 22); "Madame le maire" lower case 21 times. If the capital marks the prefecture's formality, chapter 1's narration ("addressed to Monsieur le Maire") is not the prefecture.
- **Ch 8, a dropped line.** "'I'm saying it to you. I'm not saying it to the mairie.' 'Tell Madame le maire the fête committee wants the hall keys on the fifth, for the sixty tables.'" Two speeches by the same speaker closed and reopened on one line with nothing between. A beat has fallen out.
- **Ch 13.** "The council had had it before it each March for three years". Three "had/it" in six words.
- **Interrupted speech** is printed with no mark at all: "on the night of the'" (10), "did you'" (20), "like your father never'" (23). Chapter 10 explains it in the next sentence. The other two read as typesetting errors on first sight. Any consistent mark would do.
- **The calendar slips a day, by my count.** The book matches the two Decembers by date: 6 December is twenty-six under the line in both (ch 15, ch 16), and chapter 16 says the mayor will be given the other river "a day at a time, to the centimetre". The 2014 flood is "the night of 12 to 13 December" (ch 10) after nineteen days of rain (ch 19). The 2026 flood comes on the night of Friday 11 to Saturday 12, which is the eighteenth day by chapter 17's count ("fifteen days out of fifteen" on Tuesday 8), and in chapter 22, on the 11th, the mayor takes "ninety by now" from her file, where ninety is the figure for the 12th. The weekday matches and the date does not. In a book that asks the reader to trust its arithmetic, check this one.
- **The same exposition twice.** "From November to March glass eels came up the Adour on the night tides" opens the eel paragraph in chapter 3 and again in chapter 8. The echo is the method, but this is information, not image, and the second time it is a re-read.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The first sentences of chapters 1 and 2**, and the two that follow them: "Rain was falling on the tiles." against "The tiles over her were dry and made no sound." The whole design in two lines, with nothing explained.
2. **The caller's cadence and where it ends up.** "'Fifty-three,' he said. 'Five, three.'" (7), Daguerre's "Here", "Kernels went onto cards the length of the hall, with a sound like rain starting on a tin roof", and then chapter 23's last line, "'Forty-one,' Coralie said." Not a word of that chain.
3. **The secretary and letter forty-one** (ch 13), from "I've come to take back a letter" to "That's the mairie". The funniest page in the book, and it is all rule.
4. **Larrère's window and Larrère's timer.** "He lowered his window a few centimetres, on account of the rain" (1) to "Then he put the window down as far as it went. She had not seen that done in eighteen years." (17); and the timer ringing on "did you" (20). Two running jokes that turn into plot without being pointed at.
5. **Coralie in chapter 20**, from "'Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one.'" to "'Because you'd have told me.'", and the last lines of chapters 24 and 25: "'That back gutter's coming over again,' she said. 'Look at it before you go.'" and the man in waders stepping over the string.
