# Critic: Reader

Read `manuscript/01.md` to `25.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

Verdict: below the bar as a whole. Chapters 1 to 13 read at 5 to 6. Chapters 19 to 24 read at 9. The reader the bar names does not reach chapter 19.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 6.** The danger has a date only on chapter 14 of 25: "That village had not been spared December 2014. It had not had it yet." Until then the worst that can happen is "forty euros a month you'll not have the trouble of."
- **Tension: 7.** 9 when it is this: "It rang once. Nobody answered it, and she did not wait for the second ring." 4 when it is this, for the sixth time: "It came at 02:51, and Maylis let it take her. The phone buzzed at four. She walked the yard in the rain, and at twenty past she lay down on her back."
- **Progression: 6.** I am told most things twice. Ch 10: "Guy Larrère stood at the prefect's left in the mayor's sash and clapped with the tips of his fingers." Ch 11: "Guy Larrère stood at his left in the sash and clapped with the tips of his fingers."
- **Comedy: 6.** I smiled often and laughed twice. The best is procedure meeting a person: "There's nothing here that lets me un-receive it. There's no column."
- **Characters: 7.** The villagers are alive. Coralie is the one I would miss: "I'm not on any of them." The two leads are kept behind glass for nine chapters.
- **Switch between dark and comic: 8.** After the drowning told entire, nine rings, then: "'Who's dead?' said Jeannot Castagnet." Clean every time it is tried.
- **Prose: 7.** Plain and exact: "She pushed harder and so did it, and the pain went round the rib from her spine to her breastbone, and she saw that of the two of them she would tire first." Loaded with figures (132 in chapter 1 alone) and with rituals repeated word for word.
- **Ending: 8.** "'That back gutter's coming over again,' she said. 'Look at it before you go.'" It costs: a death either way, and the mayor never learns what her five words did.
- **Clarity: 5.** The rule the whole temptation rests on is one line in a notebook: "*When the other one gets up and I am still sitting, I go with her.*" I cannot say what happens to the other one.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes, and I nearly stopped at chapter 5. **Press it on a friend?** Not on the friend the bar names. On one patient friend, with "give it until chapter 8".

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below.**

- Crouch has the premise, the danger and the rule in the reader's hands within three chapters, and the reader learns each with the hero. Here I have the premise at the first line of chapter 2, then wait seven chapters for the two women to catch up with me, and fourteen for the danger.
- Crouch makes me afraid for the marriage by showing it warm first. Here the couple is already a shopping pad with boxes when I meet it. I learn what there is to lose in chapter 13 and feel it in chapter 20.
- Level with The Good Place on reversals that send me back: chapter 14 (the flood has not happened there yet), chapter 19 (she rang once), chapter 24 ("He rang you from here"). Above Crouch on the ending: the warning from the one who has lived it makes things no better, only different, and the man was in a dry cellar.

**What I did not understand.** What becomes of the woman whose bed is taken. Where the Port, the dyke, the gate and the meadows lie in relation to each other. Why the rain over there copies 2014 to the centimetre. What the endless hall was, until chapter 23 showed me.

**Where I skimmed.** Chapter 3 from the lights to the ponies. The way into the box from the third time on. The medal in 11, the lease in 13, the cameras in 15. Every committee vote with a tally.

**Where I could not stop.** The turn from 1 into 2. The end of 8. Chapter 13 from the weal on. Chapter 14. Everything from 19 to 24.

**What I will remember in a month.** The thing with its back to her. The same sentence opening chapter 2. *left* and *right*. The crooked reflector going out. "It rang once." Monsieur Daguerre calling "Here". Nineteen sandbags given to it to count. "Forty-one." The gutter.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

1. **I know the answer seven chapters before they do, and the clock starts at chapter 14.** Ch 3: "So it could not be her past and would not be her future, and she remembered it all the same." Ch 5: "*I had told them, and they gave me the mairie.*" Ch 7: "*My job gone.*" Chapter 2 told me there is a mayor. Each of these is a wrong guess at a puzzle I have solved, and "*There are two of us. She is the mayor.*" comes on chapter 9. Cut the wrong guesses. Let the signature in chapter 5 be proof enough and put the cards straight after it. Move the discovery of chapter 14 to about chapter 8, so that a known date and a known death run under seventeen chapters and not eleven.

2. **Almost everything is told twice.** The medal, ch 10 and again ch 11, quoted above. The box, ch 6: "It was a forearm's length from her face, grey steel, with two lamps on it side by side"; ch 9: "a steel panel a forearm's length from her face, with two lamps on it side by side." The lease in 2 and 4, then again in 13. The cameras in 8, then 15. The loto in 7, then 8. Keep a second telling only where it changes the first: the box joke "pointed the other way", the reflector. Give every other one a line.

3. **The rule of the chair is never set out, so the temptation has no price.** Ch 17: "*I mean to stay in the chair. That is where the sash is.*" Ch 16: "She would not need five words. She would put down the handle and walk the track." If one stays, where is the other? Sharing the body, shut out, left in the chair? Neither woman asks. Each weighs what she would leave and never what she would take. Use the nine breaths in 16 and the visit in 17 to show it: one of them tries to move a hand. Then have each say what staying does to the other.

4. **Maylis refuses in three sentences.** Ch 23: "She did not sit. She had written on the back of a form that she meant to. She looked at what was in the seat, and at the lamps, and kept her feet." Eleven chapters built "*I want that.*" The mayor gets a page in 22 and it is the best reasoning in the book. Give Maylis hers, with a reason I can see.

5. **I cannot draw the place, and the sum depends on the drawing.** Ch 1 raises the gate to drain: "the meadows drained only on the ebb". Ch 5 raises it to flood: "Raised then, the gate would give the river three hundred hectares to go into and take it off the dyke". That is the only explanation, mid-committee. I never learn why the Port stays dry when the meadows fill. The mayor is on a roof at the end of chapter 2 looking down at all of it. Lay it out there, once, top to bottom. Bring "It is a door in a bank. It is not a tap" forward from chapter 10.

6. **Nothing warm to be afraid for, and nobody's inside, for nine chapters.** Ch 1: "'What do you want, Maylis?' 'Nothing. Go back to sleep.'" The first feeling stated outright is in chapter 10: "She found that she was crying, flat on her back, without any sound." Put one scene in the first three chapters where the two of them are good together, so the landing costs something when it comes.

7. **Three things I waited for are not given.** Larrère is last seen "at the back, under the fourth tree" in one village and not at all in the other after he rings off; the man who gave the order from that kitchen is never faced. The winch that stops at nineteen is loaded three times ("And if it stops with the gate down and the river over the line?") and ends "She had not turned it a centimetre." Coralie's answer is "a door, and did not listen for which." Keep the door. Give Larrère one wordless beat in each village. Let the winch stick once more, when it no longer matters.

8. **The book asks its largest question and declines it.** Ch 18: "*24 November.*" with no heading. Ch 22: "She gave it none now." Ch 16: "a day at a time, to the centimetre." Did the link bring the rain? Is the mayor warning the other one of something she carried over? Let her say what she fears, once, to the priest on the telephone in 18. She need not be right.

9. **"It", "the chair", and "she".** Ch 16: "The motion had one fault. It had fetched her back that morning after nine breaths." Ch 12 opens "the mayor of Peyrebarthe woke in the chair", and I took it for the box. Chapters 5, 13, 19 and 24 open on "she" or on the commune, and I find out which woman by the weather. Name her in each first sentence. Mend every "it" that follows another noun. Once Arlette has named the thing in chapter 4, use the name.

10. **Figures and office words bury the ones that matter.** Ch 1: "The three notes could be consulted in the register of deliberations at the mairie during opening hours." Mairie, loto, commune, deliberation, brigade and prefecture are never glossed. 02:51, nineteen turns, forty houses, forty-two beds and ten minutes against twenty-five have to be found among euros, vote tallies and years. Halve the figures in chapters 1 to 6. Gloss each office word once, in passing, as "tonne" is.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. The first sentence of chapter 1 returning as the first sentence of chapter 2, with "The tiles over her were dry and made no sound."
2. The night in three tellings (*She drove into him*; *She got to the tonne too late*; "It rang once"), and the nine rings and "Who's dead?" that close chapter 19.
3. Coralie in chapter 20: "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." and "Because you'd have told me."
4. The empty chair, from "That's yours. It's paid." through the hall with no end to "Forty-one", with Daguerre's "Here" and the floor "as dry as a kitchen."
5. Maïté: the kiss wiped off twice, "He rang you from here," and the gutter.
