# Critic's report: Thriller

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `25.md`, in order, in full. 57,188 words.

Verdict against the bar: below it for thirteen chapters, level with it from 19 to 23. A reader who came for Crouch does not reach the part that is as good as Crouch.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 6.** Lethal and personal once stated, but stated at chapter 14 of 25, after 31,541 words (55% of the book): "That village had not been spared December 2014. It had not had it yet." Until that sentence the stakes in the wet village are a dark tree, a complaint about a hand of water and a job that ends in January.
- **Tension: 5.** The book's ritual is printed more often than its threat. The minute 02:51 appears twenty times in fifteen chapters and fingers are counted thirty times in eighteen. The loto, the one public scene of the first half with a threat in it, ends: "Jeannot Castagnet stood up in his waders with a card in each hand and shouted for the ham."
- **Progression: 5.** Each discovery is made twice, and the reader has it first. "*There are two of us. She is the mayor.*" is written in chapter 9. The reader was told so by the first sentence of chapter 2.
- **Comedy: 7.** Procedure as a wall: "There's nothing here that lets me un-receive it. There's no column."
- **Characters: 8.** Coralie is the best thing in the book: "I thought I could have the one without the other, if I kept a landing between."
- **The switch between dark and comic: 7.** Clean where the joke is made to carry the dread. "'Nineteen,' Madame Etcheto said. 'That's few. It counts quickly.'" Then, of the man who has called "Here" at every bag: "Nobody called."
- **Prose: 8.** "Then the water came up the door to it and put it out."
- **The ending: 7.** "'Forty-one,' Coralie said." The count is exact and the last three chapter endings are right. The bill goes to the wrong people (faults 6 and 7).
- **Clarity: 5.** The crossing has no stated limit, a date is flagged twice and never explained ("*24 November.*", "she gave it no heading", "She gave it none now"), and the village's verdict on Maylis arrives as a riddle: "They said he had the key and went for a walk."

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** I would have stopped during chapter 7 and, had I got to chapter 8, not stopped again.

**Would I press it on a friend?** Not on the friend this bar describes. I would have to say "give it until chapter 8", and that sentence is the report.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below**, for these reasons.

1. **When the premise starts to hurt.** In *Dark Matter* the hero is taken in the first chapter and is living in the wrong world by the third, and the question "will he get home to her" runs from there to the end. In *Wayward Pines* the man cannot leave the town by the end of the first act. Here the reader has the premise by chapter 2, the two women have it at chapters 8 to 10, and the deadline arrives at chapter 14. Crouch's premise and his clock start together. This book's are twelve chapters apart.
2. **Where a chapter stops.** A Crouch chapter stops on the turn, usually in one line with white space after it. These chapters put the turn in the middle and walk on to a quiet beat. Chapter 10 has "*She went down the track and knocked. He is alive there because of it.*" and ends "then she wrote in the register that the sitting had risen at 20:11."
3. **How long he stays in the room.** Crouch plays his set pieces at full length. This book's two nights at the gate take about 900 words (chapter 19, the first night, received as memory) and 1,695 words (chapter 23, the second, live). That is under 5% of the book for the two events the other 95% is about.

**Level or above** in one place: the run of reversals in 19 to 24. The minute on the telephone screen, Coralie's twelve years with the call log, and the kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice each send the reader back over earlier chapters, which is what *The Good Place* does with its first-season turn. Chapter 20 puts a marriage on the fulcrum of the plot as well as Crouch does. It gets there without his method, which is to make the reader love the marriage first. Both couples here start cold, so what the reader feels for them through the first half is regret, and fear only from chapter 17.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The clock starts at chapter 14.**
Chapter 14: "That village had not been spared December 2014. It had not had it yet."
The reader watches a gauge for thirteen chapters with no way to read it. Chapter 5: "The river stood a metre forty below the line." Nothing tells us that figure is on a schedule. The book has chosen one surprise in chapter 14 over twelve chapters of suspense, and the reader it wants leaves before the surprise.
*Do:* give the reader the 2014 calendar in chapter 2. Lesca has it to hand: the flood file, the eleven cast plates with their line and date, the committee's agenda. One paragraph: it began to rain on 24 November 2014 and did not stop; a metre under on the third; ninety over on the twelfth. Chapter 1 has just said "It rained all Tuesday, straight down" on Tuesday 24 November. From then on every reading on Maylis's gauge is a countdown the reader can do and she cannot, and the lights, the loto and the road signs play over it. Lesca stays blind until 14, as now. Her mistake in chapter 10 then stops being a detour and becomes the thing the reader wants to shout at.

**2. The night the book is built for is 1,695 words long, and its death is offstage.**
Chapter 23: "She was not gone long. She came back by herself, wet to the collar, and took the next chair by its handles without a word to anyone."
From the breach to the end of the chapter is 534 words. The rescue is one paragraph. Nobody we are with goes down the dark corridor. Twenty chapters have said "forty houses and a care home", and when the bank goes the reader gets a street lamp count and a summary.
*Do:* triple it. Keep every timestamp to 03:11 as it stands. After it, stay in the water: the road, Sarthou's open door, the yard, the chairs, the dyke-end corridor going dark, the door of twelve. Let Maylis be the one who cannot go down it because she has a chair in her hands. Keep "Forty-one" as the last word.

**3. Both women decide to take the other's life on the same night, and nothing comes of it.**
Chapter 16: "That the mayor remain seated." Chapter 17: "*I mean to stay in the chair. That is where the sash is.*"
This is the engine of *Dark Matter*: another you wants your life. It is built by chapter 17 and dismantled in private. Maylis drops her wish in chapter 19 on learning a price. Lesca drops hers in chapter 22 by arithmetic. Neither knows the other meant it, neither is endangered by it, and the "woman in a bed with her eyes open who did not wake" is promised three times and never found.
*Do:* let one of them succeed for part of the night. Lesca holds the lamp, and it is Lesca in the van when the telephone lights, with the handle forty metres off and the thing she has wanted for twelve years in her hands. She gives it back there, under pressure, in the other woman's body, and not from the edge of a chair. Across the landing Coralie finds what she said she would find and rings Bayonne. The choice becomes an event.

**4. Chapters end after their turn.**
Chapter 16 contains the motion above and ends: "'Carried,' Lesca said, 'by six votes to one.'" Chapter 1 has a woman's name on a gravestone and ends with a mayor who "went in to sign his post." Chapters 7, 10, 12, 14, 15 and 18 do the same.
The cause is one habit: a chapter opens on what she woke with and then runs the day.
*Do:* invert it. Run the day first and end on the night and what it delivers. The hand-off to the other woman's chapter then happens across the break, on the turn. No scene needs rewriting, only moving.

**5. Everything is staged twice, and the reader has always seen it once already.**
Chapter 11, of the medal ceremony that chapter 10 has just played in full: "It was eleven minutes long, and she had all of it."
Chapter 8 opens by re-telling the loto of chapter 7. The lease is told in 2 and 4 and received in 13. The cameras are told in 8 and received in 15. The endless hall closes 15 and opens 16. The two hundred euros are in 3 and again in 12. The reader spends chapters 3 to 9 waiting for Maylis to learn what chapter 2 said.
*Do:* one rule. An event is staged once, in the village where it costs most. The other woman gets a line of it and the wrong thing she does with it. That frees about 4,000 words, which fault 2 needs. It also brings the cards forward: the room and *right* / *left* belong at chapter 5 or 6, not 8 and 9.

**6. The sender never gets the bill.**
Chapter 24: "Nothing had come after she stood up. She did not know whether five words had got across, or what a woman in a van had done with them."
Lesca's five words cost sixteen minutes, forty houses and the woman who, in her own village, sits "at the end nearest the stone". The reader knows it as she reads her speech. She never does. She is spared the one fact that would make her atonement cost something.
*Do:* one last delivery before the link closes. She wakes on Saturday with a hall, three rows of blankets and the last chair empty, and a number. Then she walks to the stone and looks along the front row.

**7. The man who gave the order is never faced.**
Chapter 24: "Guy Larrère was at the back, under the fourth tree."
He rang from the drowned man's kitchen and said, in chapter 23, "He's dry as long as you do what you're told." That is the book's best twist and it produces no scene. In the dry village Lesca learns it and goes home past a gutter. In the wet village he is not seen again after a bank he ordered held has gone into forty houses. Chapter 20 even leaves the question loaded: "When you stood on that platform, before you laid a hand on the winch, did you". The timer cuts him off and nobody ever finishes it.
*Do:* two short scenes. In the wet village, dawn, the Port road, his window all the way down and Sarthou holding the telephone. In the dry one, the timer's last use: she lets it ring, and asks him from whose telephone.

**8. The crossing has no stated limit, and the date that might explain the flood is dropped.**
Chapter 16: "To stay gone she would have to keep it off her, and in twelve years she had not learned how that was done."
A reader cannot fear a mechanism they cannot state. If Lesca stays, is Maylis put out of her own body, or do they share it? The text does not say, so the threat in fault 3 has no shape. And "*24 November.*" is written at the foot of a page in chapter 18 and recalled in 22, both times with no heading. If the rain began over there because the thing found a second woman to sit on, that is the largest stake in the book and it is never said. If it did not, the line is a tease.
*Do:* state the rule once, as a finding, in the form the notebook already uses. And give 24 November its heading or cut both mentions.

**9. On the night, the keyholder lies down and sets her alarm.**
Chapter 21: "She laid the telephone where she could reach it lying down, and set its alarm for four. Then she put the seat back as far as it went".
The river is eighty-six over, the bank is coming through, a man has just gone under the ground, and she schedules her four o'clock. She gave up wanting the chair in chapter 19. The plot needs her in the room at 02:51 to receive five words, and that need is the only reason on the page.
*Do:* have her fight sleep and lose. The mower in chapter 11 has already shown what a fortnight of four-hour nights does to her. It is more frightening, and it is not her decision.

**10. The caller's name is on one woman's screen and not the other's.**
Chapter 23: "Under the time it said Castagnet, house." Chapter 24: "'He rang you from here.'" / "'Who did?'"
Maylis reads in a second what Lesca has not known in twelve years, on the same call from the same telephone. A reader who enjoys the twist will go back and check it.
*Do:* one line in chapter 19. In 2014 she lifted it in the dark without looking, or it was the landline by the bed.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The hour.** "The screen said 02:51." (chapter 19), and the first sentence of the book that it re-reads. Also the nine rings that follow and "'Who's dead?'".
2. **The two cards.** *right* in chapter 8, *left* in chapter 9, and "*2. My hand. Not my word.*" This is the first moment of fear in the book and the device is clean.
3. **Coralie's speech in chapter 20.** "Either you opened it for forty houses, and I live with the mayor. Or you opened it because I was in that building. Then he's mine as well". Every word, including "Don't say it now."
4. **The arithmetic of chapter 23.** The timestamps, "She meant to do both.", "She had given it ten minutes. It had held sixteen.", Jeannot's "Open it now", and the last word.
5. **The plants that pay.** The kitchen clock ("Behind his voice a clock was ticking, close, of the kind that hangs in a kitchen."), the crooked reflector and "Nobody had given her this one.", the buckled suitcase, and the last images of 24 and 25: the gutter, and the man in waders getting down into the grave.
