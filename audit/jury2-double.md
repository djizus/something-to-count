# The double: a literary report

## 1. Where it belongs

This is a provincial novel of the double, in which the life not lived arrives to demand a body, a lover and a second attempt at an irreversible choice. Its nearest neighbours are Saramago’s *The Double*, Dostoevsky’s *The Double* and Atkinson’s *Life After Life*: erotic substitution, humiliation before a community, and foreknowledge that cannot make action innocent.

## 2. Against those books

**Saramago, *The Double*.** Saramago turns an image glimpsed in a film into a rival whose identical appearance permits sexual impersonation; the exchanges make bodies, names and partners dangerously transferable. This novel makes that danger comparably intimate through Coralie’s professional knowledge of touch: “I felt both.” One hand holds her while the wrist withdraws. Recognition becomes something a lover feels before she understands. It does less well at sustaining uncertainty: Saramago’s argumentative narrator keeps reopening the premise, whereas this narrative repeatedly explains what its evidence means. Its own achievement is Coralie’s refusal: “I don’t get into a bed over somebody who can’t speak.” The desired woman judges the substitution rather than serving as its prize. This is kinship, not derivation.

**Dostoevsky, *The Double*.** Golyadkin’s socially fluent counterpart occupies the place his original cannot secure; humiliations at work make duplication a crisis of public standing. Lesca likewise possesses the authority Maylis cannot exercise: “Then it’s carried, or it’s a tie, and in a tie the chair decides.” The novel matches the older book’s capacity to make power bodily humiliating: Maylis raises her wounded palm to apologise for her own insubordinate mouth. But Dostoevsky’s broken repetitions make the narration itself inhabit Golyadkin’s bewilderment. Here the two women remain unusually legible, their characteristic evasions neatly labelled. What is new is reciprocity: the confident double envies the subordinate’s shared bed, and Maylis enjoys a square that calls her mayor. Neither is simply the successful or authentic self. The bureaucratic comedy recalls Dostoevsky without copying him.

**Atkinson, *Life After Life*.** Atkinson rewrites scenes across Ursula’s lives; altered timing and accumulated dread make an ordinary action carry the pressure of earlier deaths. This novel achieves comparable pressure economically when Maylis lets Jeannot’s phone ring nine times after learning that Lesca allowed one. A repeated gesture acquires a different moral duration. It has less imaginative breadth: its two careers and households often function as deliberately balanced opposites, where Atkinson’s variations admit whole contingent social worlds. Its distinctive refusal is that rescuing the previously dead man kills somebody else. “She wanted it not to be her” destroys the comforting account of Maylis as the improved version. There is no further life available to erase Madame Etcheto. Again, no specific derivation: the shared device produces a different ethical result.

## 3. What is its own

The combination of municipal maintenance, shared bodily control and a dead village playing bingo is distinctive. Inguma’s leaf-covered steel body joins a local night visitor to the experimental room without making either vocabulary wholly explain the other. The bodily contest can be ludicrous and coercive simultaneously: a hand grips a stair post against its own legs while a lover waits above.

Most distinctive is the distribution of responsibility through working objects. The key, the timer, the shopping pad and the nursing list belong to occupations before they acquire moral force. A duck hunter rescued against his will subsequently carries residents out of the building endangered by his rescue. His final letter records the facts without becoming gratitude, and the second shovel provides help without absolution. Those are more original than the two lamps.

## 5. Five faults, worst first

1. **The supernatural can perform the work of conscience.** “This isn’t your kitchen” announces the room where false explanations cannot be spoken. This compelling device also lets an external mechanism identify an evasion before either woman must expose it unaided. I would make those failed utterances less consistently diagnostic and preserve more doubt about what a refusal to speak means. The eventual confession should exceed what the room can certify.

2. **The prose too often interprets an image immediately after giving it.** “She wanted it not to be her” is devastating; “That was Either, with the river in it” later supplies another definitive gloss. Such formulations accumulate around already intelligible actions. I would retain the first admission and cut several subsequent explanations, especially where a hand, a crossed-out line or a departing pillow already carries the judgment.

3. **The weather connection is easier to establish than to believe.** “I kept her out for two nights, and the road dried” becomes the basis for a vast moral attribution. The text acknowledges the small sample, but competing explanations rapidly lose force. I would give the connection one substantial contradictory observation that remains troublesome, so the women must act under uncertainty rather than treating a correlation as settled knowledge.

4. **Supporting people can become their repeated verbal business.** “One. It’s 5 in the morning. Two.” Jeannot’s enumerations are funny, but join Larrère’s gaps, Josiane’s adultery theory and Coralie’s boxes in a conspicuously organised ensemble. I would remove some recurrences and spend that space on an unpatterned exchange. Kévin’s “I wanted six” shows how much a person gains when his established habit breaks.

5. **The haunting ends too obediently after the lesson.** “‘I won’t.’ It came out whole.” The lamps extinguish, Inguma leaves, and the wardrobe is empty: an elegant but almost certified release. I would leave one ordinary manifestation unresolved. The grave and second shovel already provide an ending; they do not need a supernatural receipt confirming that the women have finished learning.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

- **The double’s compromised tenderness.** Lesca really wants Jeannot alive and Coralie beside her; those desires do not excuse taking Maylis’s body.
- **The evacuation’s patient labour.** The repeated journeys with wheelchairs make forty-two cease to be a convenient number in a moral sum.
- **The ending’s refusal of repair through romance.** Coralie leaves for a future she wanted before the disaster. Jeannot helps dig the grave of the woman whose death his survival cost.

## 7. The verdict

It belongs on the double’s shelf by subject and by several accomplished scenes; it does not yet stand there as an equal whole. I would not put this version forward for a literary prize. Its decisive limitation is the wish to explain and certify what its best actions already leave a reader painfully able to judge.
