# The literary speculative novel: a jury read

## 1. Where it belongs

This is a compact literary speculative novel about municipal work, divided identity and the evasions by which people survive their own decisions. Its nearest neighbours are *Never Let Me Go*, *Life After Life* and *The Anomaly*, though its village comedy and attention to physical labour give it a different social world.

## 2. Against those books

**Kazuo Ishiguro, *Never Let Me Go*.** Ishiguro makes institutional euphemism part of Kathy's ordinary language; the reader gradually hears what her account protects her from acknowledging. Here, “No one came to tell him” performs comparable work: a true sentence shelters Lesca from the question of what she knew. The annual speech, the cut-off questions and the timer make that evasion public and repeatable. This novel does institutional self-protection as well as Ishiguro does, in miniature. It does worse at sustaining the distance between what a character says and what a reader understands: its narrator frequently supplies the interpretation. Its own achievement is to bring care work and manual work into the same moral reckoning. Coralie's “I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them” makes professional experience the basis of an ethics of possession.

**Kate Atkinson, *Life After Life*.** Atkinson's repeated lives alter our feeling for familiar rooms, relationships and historical contingencies. This novel achieves comparable force with a smaller change: “Pillows: two. At home she had one.” The alternate life becomes immediately inhabitable, and losing intimacy weighs against gaining office. It offers less breadth and less unpredictability than Atkinson; these two lives increasingly organise themselves around one exemplary night. What it adds is a struggle between contemporaneous selves who can injure, desire and bargain with each other. “The chair votes once” turns the speculative premise into a local political fact. The resemblance is structural, not derivative: there is no borrowed sequence or recognisable imitation of Atkinson's voice.

**Hervé Le Tellier, *The Anomaly*.** Le Tellier's duplicates become legible through contrasting biographies, encounters and competing public explanations. Here, “You're looking at her” launches a more sustained encounter: the duplicate occupies the very mouth needed to refuse her. The novel matches Le Tellier's ability to make an impossible situation socially funny. It does worse at allowing its premise to produce divergent forms of thought; nearly everything returns to choice, counting or withheld speech. Its distinctive advance is bodily consent. Maylis locking her injured hand on the stair post carries a violence and vulnerability that a meeting between separately embodied duplicates cannot. The double is inherited material, but this treatment is not derivative of *The Anomaly*.

**Ted Chiang, “Story of Your Life”.** Chiang makes foreknowledge change the form and emotional meaning of narration. Here the stopped watch and recurring minute change the meaning of an initially ordinary phone call: “She didn't wait for the second ring.” Both works make knowledge costly without treating it as a puzzle prize. Chiang's integration of speculative thought and narrative form is more complete; this novel usually explains its discoveries in dialogue or lists. Its own question concerns responsibility after an attempted rescue fails. The second night cannot erase the first, and the other Castagnet cannot substitute for the dead man.

## 3. What is its own

I have not encountered this particular conjunction of a sleep-paralysis visitor, a university's steel room and municipal authority. Inguma carries rivets beneath its leaves; sending it to count the gravel removed from a mother's field joins the supernatural to an actual history of dispossession.

The dead playing village bingo are especially good. They scarcely attend to the living woman's confession. Their indifference prevents the afterlife from becoming a tribunal arranged for her benefit.

Most distinctive is the distributed responsibility. Lesca brings the river; Maylis leaves the gate; Coralie returns Etcheto to her room; Larrère withholds action. The saved man finally helps dig the grave of the woman whose death accompanies his survival. Nobody's account exhausts what happened.

## 5. Five faults, worst first

1. **The narration sometimes settles the judgment it has earned the reader's right to make.** “She wanted it not to be her” states Maylis's motive at the decisive fork. The preceding thought about the river's doing already exposes that evasion. Cut the explanatory sentence and audit the other passages that interpret a gesture immediately after showing it. Lesca's uncertainty about why she opened the gate demonstrates the richer alternative.

2. **The speculative room adjudicates sincerity too conveniently.** “It gives me somebody to blame, and it won't come out” makes blocked speech certify an evasive explanation. Repeated failures on *Either*, *tide* and *get* begin to feel like compulsory moral corrections. Reduce those interventions; let the selves hear a plausible defence and dispute it. Keep the room's physical strangeness without asking it to settle every account of motive.

3. **Too many people finish their thoughts with the novel's governing insight.** “It's four when you're the one counting” is excellent Darrigrand, but it joins a crowded company of precise statements about numbers, choices and measurements. Give some supporting encounters less thematic finish: a distraction, an irrelevant grievance, a memory nobody can use. Coralie and Jeannot have distinct voices; the village needs more room beyond their shared moral vocabulary.

4. **The approach to the flood over-rehearses its arithmetic.** “The trapdoor was twenty-five minutes away ... The bank had ten.” Repeated route descriptions and timings make the dilemma clear but reduce discovery before the actual emergency. Condense duplicate rehearsals. Preserve the evacuation's patient-by-patient labour, where duration acquires human weight.

5. **The closing pattern is almost too fully accounted for.** “One lamp went out. Then the other” closes the speculative encounter alongside the office, relationship, numbered letter and grave. Keep the consequences, but loosen some exact verbal returns. The final shared digging can bear more of the closure without every earlier object receiving a concluding use.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

- The unequal hands. Possession becomes a bodily struggle rather than an abstract identity problem.
- Coralie's independent ambition and her refusal to make caregiving an obligation to stay.
- Etcheto's death and the unreconciled work afterwards. A successful rescue cannot purchase absolution.

## 7. The verdict

Yes, it would stand on that shelf. I would put it forward for prize consideration and argue for it at the longlist stage; its control of interpretation would make me hesitate over a shortlist. What earns the nomination is the second night's refusal to redeem the first. What limits it is the frequency with which the prose tells us exactly how to judge that refusal.
