# The uncanny and the folk tale

## 1. Where it belongs

This is a village folk tale about possession, public responsibility and the harm concealed inside a wish to do good. It stands nearest to Marcel Aymé's "The Man Who Walked Through Walls", Maupassant's "The Horla" and Shirley Jackson's *The Haunting of Hill House*, with Robert Aickman's "The Hospice" close behind its care-home doors.

## 2. Against those books

**Aymé, "The Man Who Walked Through Walls".** Aymé gives Dutilleul an impossible bodily freedom, then follows it through office revenge, burglary and adultery until ordinary medication leaves him imprisoned in a wall. This novel matches his ability to make the impossible operate among jobs, petty authorities and practical objects: "A meeting needs somebody in the chair. Since neither of us is in it, I'll chair from here." It is less economical; its repeated explanations turn Aymé's swift comic consequence into a long negotiation. Its own achievement is divided agency: one hand administers, the other resists, and their struggle has injuries and questions of consent attached. It is not derivative of Aymé. The administrative comedy belongs to the tradition; the bodily conflict is its own.

**Maupassant, "The Horla".** Maupassant lets the narrator's experiments with water, milk and his reflection accumulate evidence without settling whether an invisible master exists outside his mind. Here the opening weight, immobility and familiar smell achieve comparable intimacy: "It smelled of cold steel and old tobacco, in a house where nobody had ever smoked." The cracked rib gives the terror an unusually convincing physical cause and cost. But corroboration by other characters, followed by a working account of the crossings, reduces the uncertainty much sooner. "You're the water" eventually becomes an explanation everyone relevant can use. What Maupassant does not offer is a community whose routines both conceal the supernatural and provide ways to oppose it. The chest-sitting visitor is a shared folkloric inheritance, not evidence of derivation from "The Horla".

**Jackson, *The Haunting of Hill House*.** Jackson makes Eleanor's desire to belong inseparable from the house's invitation; access to a home becomes a danger. Lesca's desire has that force: "That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it." The frightening thing is recognisable love becoming entitlement. Jackson gives Eleanor a more unstable inner language, in which attraction and terror contaminate perception. Lesca repeatedly states her motives in orderly dialogue, leaving less for the reader to discover. This novel adds something Jackson's does not: the desired household comes with a river that endangers other households, and the woman being desired refuses entry on grounds learned through care work. No derivation; the resemblance concerns the emotional engine.

**Aickman, "The Hospice".** Aickman's traveller enters an institution whose meals, hospitality and unexplained customs make care increasingly coercive. This novel is equally good at putting dread inside ordinary procedure: "Staff to wait. Staff not to join in." The dead playing bingo offer its strongest Aickman-like estrangement. "He put a grain on his second card" answers a confession without answering it. Aickman allows institutional customs to remain bewildering; here the instruction, count and consequence are usually decipherable. Yet the evacuation achieves something different and substantial: institutional care becomes actual labour, chair by chair, while authority fails. The affinity is real, but neither the institution nor the afterlife feels borrowed.

## 3. What is its own

I have not met this combination elsewhere: an inherited counting charm used to prolong possession; a supernatural visitor whose steel knocks against the experimental chamber; a gravedigger confronted by dead neighbours who remain busy with their cards. The coat, key, scar and pillow carry differences between lives without requiring a panorama of alternate history.

The best invention is the second catastrophe. Saving the man does not vindicate Maylis. Her thought, "If she went on, whatever the river did behind her was the river's doing", exposes the wish to escape authorship of harm. Madame Etcheto's death gives that evasion a person and a room. The later shared grave-digging offers help without turning Jeannot's survival into acquittal.

## 5. Five faults, worst first

1. **The dialogue sometimes supplies the reading after the scenes have earned it.** "That was Either, with the river in it." Coralie's sentence closes a moral comparison readers can make themselves, and compresses a desperate rescue into Maylis's habitual evasion. Cut this gloss and others like it; keep "I know what you picked. I was the other one."

2. **The plot arranges collective paralysis too efficiently.** "A vote was a vote." Petitioners accept defeat, neighbouring workers cannot come, and evacuation waits for a siren. Each obstacle is plausible; their accumulation makes the solitary dilemma feel constructed. Let one petitioner attempt practical help and fail through the conditions already present. That would test the village's helplessness rather than merely announce it.

3. **Some villagers have too few departures from their identifying behaviour.** "It's adultery whichever she's after, and it's in that house." Josiane repeatedly converts everything into the same accusation. Give her one consequential response that exceeds this formula, preferably by replacing a repeated gossip exchange. Jeannot's eventual lifting and digging show how much such a departure can do.

4. **The scientific premise borrows significance it never fully earns.** "What we want to know is whether there is another you, somewhere, who sees the other one." The experiment establishes the two lamps, then largely leaves the story to folklore. Compress its introductory explanation and strengthen the already present sensory connection between Inguma and the room. More scientific exposition would weaken the tale; the chamber needs imaginative necessity.

5. **The ceremony repeats information at the expense of its immediate pressure.** "The first sheet was about the rain." Then come the river and Castagnet, facts we have repeatedly followed. Shorten the summaries before the changed sentence. Preserve the confession travelling through the crowd and the awkward continuation of the programme: those details make this scene live.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

- The dead's indifference to confession. Their game continues; nobody exists merely to forgive Lesca.
- Coralie's professional knowledge and independent desire. Her refusal to enter an occupied body is the novel's clearest ethical action.
- The final assistance without absolution: Jeannot gets the second shovel, and the grave still has to be dug.

## 7. The verdict

Yes, it would stand on that shelf, especially beside Aymé. It has less psychological openness than Jackson and less unresolved strangeness than Aickman, but its practical world and costly ending earn the company. I would put it forward for a prize judged by readers of those books. The deciding strength is its refusal to make either saving Jeannot or confessing his death settle the account.
