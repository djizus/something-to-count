# The comic village: literary report

## 1. Where it belongs

This is a village tragicomedy in which practical work, gossip and municipal authority govern a supernatural encounter with the life not lived. Its nearest neighbours are Pagnol's *Jean de Florette* and *Manon of the Springs*, Fitzgerald's *The Bookshop*, and Mills's *The Restraint of Beasts*: small communities make disastrous decisions through ordinary interests, then dispute who should carry the consequences.

## 2. Against those books

**Pagnol, *Jean de Florette* and *Manon of the Springs*.** The novel matches Pagnol's conversion of water management into moral action. A sticking handle, a flooded track and a locked trapdoor matter as much as the concealed spring does in Pagnol; nobody can solve the ethical problem without doing the physical job. Jeannot's "Nobody tells me what's under my floorboards and then tells me how to vote" makes pride obstruct survival convincingly. It does worse at giving culpability a long social history: César and Ugolin's kinship, ambitions and investment in the land accumulate beyond the immediate catastrophe, whereas Larrère's interests arrive chiefly when the plot needs an obstruction. Its own contribution is the paired disaster. Saving the hunter kills someone else; neither possible life supplies the innocent answer. The water, concealment and collective responsibility establish kinship, but the novel is not derivative of Pagnol.

**Fitzgerald, *The Bookshop*.** Fitzgerald makes local power operate through ostensibly proper procedures, especially Mrs Gamart's campaign to displace Florence Green. This novel is equally good at showing authority hiding inside courtesy: "Then it should have gone last Monday" defeats an urgent request without raising a voice. It also respects skilled work, particularly Coralie arranging blankets to open towards the door. It does worse at leaving social meanings unstated. Fitzgerald's exchanges allow silences and unequal understanding to persist; here the narration frequently supplies the interpretation immediately. What Fitzgerald does not have is the physical rival inhabiting the supposedly unassertive woman's body. Coralie's attraction to the woman who answers makes the institutional comedy painfully intimate. There is no derivative plot or borrowed voice.

**Mills, *The Restraint of Beasts*.** Mills turns fencing, deadlines and obedience to supervisors into a deadpan account of people accepting increasingly dreadful arrangements. This novel matches his ability to let an ordinary working phrase disclose that acceptance: "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station." Both make compliance active and dangerous. Mills sustains greater uncertainty about what the narrator understands; this novel repeatedly names its ethical lesson and gives its central evasive word a conspicuous symbolic career. Its distinct achievement is giving two versions of the same worker different powers, then demonstrating that command can be as evasive as acquiescence. The resemblance is structural, not imitation.

**Chevallier, *Clochemerle*.** The public urinal turns civic improvement into a conflict among political, religious and sexual interests. Here the floodgate similarly gathers incompatible private uses into one municipal object. The association chaired from a running car is as good a comic arrangement as anything the village could invent: "The president's on the other side of it." Chevallier offers a broader, less disciplined crowd, with appetites that exceed the central quarrel. Peyrebarthe's inhabitants too often speak in variations of the same concise, counting idiom. This novel does something different by allowing the comic object to become a genuine instrument of death, without dropping its petty disputes. It is not derivative of *Clochemerle*.

## 3. What is its own

I have not read this particular combination elsewhere: the alternative self brings her flood into the village she hopes to save, while her competence makes her attractive to the partner she is attempting to appropriate. Coralie distinguishes the women through opposed movements in one wrist; professional care becomes a way of recognising a person.

The dead play the same bingo as the living, with the same unsociable concentration. They offer no consoling revelation. Lesca tries to refuse death in the language of an official apology, and her father does not look up.

Most distinctive is letter forty-two. A lifelong complainant finally records another person's predicament, yet insists, "I was in no danger". Helping dig the grave does not require him to accept the account that makes his rescue necessary.

## 5. Five faults, worst first

1. **The people share too much verbal machinery.** "One. It's 5 in the morning. Two." Jeannot's counting is funny, but timers, lists, numbered complaints, votes and measurements dominate nearly everyone's expressive life. Give a few supporting figures speech and concerns that escape that pattern. Keep Jeannot's enumeration; loosen the surrounding uniformity.

2. **The narration interprets before the reader needs help.** "It had always come when she needed it. It had never been true, and it wasn't true now." The pad and the interrupted word have already exposed Maylis's evasion. Cut explanatory repetitions after gestures and dialogue, especially around *Either*, and preserve the places where thought reveals something behaviour cannot.

3. **The catastrophe is timed too visibly for its parallel.** "Maylis had given the bank ten minutes, and it had held for sixteen." The break at exactly 3:11 makes the opposing deaths neatly coincide, but the clocks, travel estimates and ring counts sometimes make catastrophe resemble a worked exercise. Retain the decisive time; reduce intermediate confirmations and let bodily uncertainty govern more of the rescue.

4. **The closed supernatural encounter is more orderly than the human aftermath.** "It came out whole." Together with the lamps going out and Inguma's departure, Lesca's successful refusal appears to switch off the mechanism. Keep the final refusal, but remove one confirming signal so that speech does not seem to cure what the village still cannot settle.

5. **Some subordinate lives are introduced chiefly to explain resistance.** "Nobody else in this village has ever opened one before I knocked." Larrère acquires loneliness late, immediately before the fatal refusal. Seed his attachment to Maïté earlier through an unguarded action, rather than relying on a declaration when the reader is deciding how to judge him.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

- Coralie's knowledge of hands, and her right to want a working life beyond this relationship.
- The village's comedy continuing after catastrophe, especially the failed oven impersonation and the letter that refuses gratitude.
- The last shared task. Jeannot takes the second shovel; the book ends with work that neither restores the dead nor settles the argument.

## 7. The verdict

Yes, it could stand on that shelf, most comfortably beside Fitzgerald and Mills. I would put it forward for a prize judged by readers of these books, while arguing for revision of its explanatory passages and its supporting voices. The deciding achievement is that its comic village survives the moral climax: the people continue to misread, accuse and help one another, and the ending gives them no common verdict.
