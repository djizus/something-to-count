# Literary jury report

## 1. Where it belongs

This is a literary speculative novel about two versions of a municipal worker, the people endangered by their choices, and the uses of public service as a defence against intimacy. It stands nearest to *Never Let Me Go*, *The Anomaly* and *Life After Life*, with a rural comic particularity those books do not share.

## 2. Against those books

### Ishiguro, *Never Let Me Go*

Ishiguro makes institutional horror enter through familiar routines: school collections, exchanges, donations, the consoling rumour of deferrals. This novel can match his ability to make care physically intimate without making it consoling. After Coralie decides to leave, she straps Maylis's rib: "Her hand stayed on it a little longer than a bandage needs" (17). Affection survives the decision; it does not reverse it.

It does worse at giving readers discoveries the characters cannot adequately formulate. Kathy's memories expose more than her explanations admit. Here Coralie often states the moral significance of a scene almost completely: "I know what you picked. I was the other one" (17). The sentence hurts, but leaves less for rereading to uncover.

Its own departure is active responsibility. These women can refuse orders, misuse authority and change another person's survival. Their suffering includes what they have done, whereas Kathy's accommodation grows within a fate imposed on her. It is not derivative of Ishiguro.

### Le Tellier, *The Anomaly*

Le Tellier gives duplicated passengers different desires and practical claims on the lives they discover already occupied. This novel handles that collision at least as vividly through one contested body. "The chair votes once, Madame le maire" (6) turns two raised hands into a comic, political and bodily fact at once.

It does worse at accommodating varied consciousnesses. Le Tellier changes forms and registers as he moves between lives; here even the lovers' quarrels frequently proceed as questions, evidence and decisions. The two Maylises are distinguishable, but the surrounding voices share too much argumentative efficiency.

What it adds is a double whose crossing brings destructive weather. The coveted alternative life can only be entered at a cost to its inhabitants. That makes replacement an ecological and civic wrong, beyond the competing claims of duplicates. The premise is not derivative of *The Anomaly*; the resemblance lies in the ethical consequences of duplication.

### Atkinson, *Life After Life*

Atkinson repeatedly makes Ursula's survival depend on accumulated foreknowledge, bodily apprehension and another attempt at a familiar danger. This novel equals her in making a changed action generate a changed loss. "Lesca was gone. The water kept coming" (14) prevents the removal of the intruder from becoming an instant cure. Saving Jeannot does not save Madame Etcheto.

It does worse at allowing alternative lives the breadth of lived time. Atkinson's repetitions contain childhood, family, desire and historical accident. Lesca's twelve years arrive mainly as consequences of the flood: office, cameras, estrangement, commemoration. They explain her well but seldom surprise us independently of that explanation.

Its distinct achievement is allowing the second chance to belong to two opposed people at once. Lesca wants another woman's future; Maylis wants control of her own body. "It's a whole life with one hand. I won't pay that" (9) makes the price concrete. It is not derivative of Atkinson.

## 3. What is its own

I have not read this particular combination elsewhere: a gravedigger negotiating possession through municipal procedure; a Basque night visitor counted away with the materials of local labour; an afterlife that preserves the disappointing prizes and absorbed indifference of village bingo.

The finest invention is care as a means of recognising identity. Coralie distinguishes the women through a wrist resisting her fingers, before she has an explanation for the double. The different versions of Peyrebarthe also distribute power convincingly: the mayor's mother loses her pony field to the gravel lake; the former poacher wears the municipal coat after prison. An alternative life has an economy, including other people's losses.

## 5. Five faults, worst first

1. **Characters explain the book's judgments too fluently.** "It's four when you're the one counting ... When it's your house it's one" (11) is sharp, but joins many speeches that perfectly diagnose the speaker's predicament. Keep this one; remove some comparable explanations from the domestic confrontations. Let an unanswered question or an inconvenient action carry more of the judgment.

2. **"Either" sometimes makes Maylis's paralysis feel prescribed.** At the decisive daylight opportunity she says "Either" (12), despite knowing what she wants and having begun to lift the gate. Her lifelong submission is credible; this exact verbal relapse is too convenient. Make the retreat more individual through her response to Jeannot's presence and Larrère's watching car. The fatal missed opportunity should remain.

3. **The obstacles accumulate into an excessively complete enclosure.** "They've got their coats, but I've told them they can't" (12) follows blackmail, an adverse vote and evacuation restrictions. Each obstruction is plausible within the book; together they make the solitary crisis look arranged. Loosen one barrier and let help fail through a particular person's imperfect decision rather than another comprehensive prohibition.

4. **The material vocabulary becomes too uniform.** Watching Coralie pack, Maylis thinks, "She took more than that to the dump in a morning" (17). Labour rightly governs Maylis's perceptions, but distances, loads, counts and job estimates explain nearly every emotional event. Cut this comparison and a few similar glosses. Preserve her working knowledge while allowing moments she cannot measure.

5. **The aftermath occasionally annotates its own refusal of exoneration.** "The arm is the excuse you've written for me. It isn't mine" (17) explains what "At 3 I never tried the handle" already establishes. Keep the latter admission and Jeannot's help. Removing the gloss would leave the ending's moral disagreement alive between them.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

- Coralie's independent appetite for the Bayonne floor, and her refusal to enter a bed over a woman who cannot speak.
- The rescue's repeated wheelchair journeys while Maylis longs to abandon them for Coralie. Duty becomes difficult bodily work.
- The final second shovel. Jeannot remains troublesome, ungrateful and willing to dig.

## 7. The verdict

Yes, it would stand on that shelf, though its prose and range of consciousness fall below the strongest books there. I would put it forward for a prize discussion, with reservations about a shortlist. What earns the nomination is the second rescue's compromised motive and irreversible death: the novel makes its sympathetic worker answer for wanting innocence more than she wanted to decide.
