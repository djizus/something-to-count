# Tradition 3: the uncanny and the folk tale

## 1. Where it belongs

This is a village tale of possession and responsibility, in which a nightmare borrows the machinery of municipal work and an alternative life brings its weather with it. It stands nearest to Marcel Aymé's "The Man Who Walked Through Walls", Maupassant's "The Horla", and Shirley Jackson's *The Haunting of Hill House*. Its Basque counting charm belongs to the daily life of the care home as naturally as the medication chart.

## 2. Against those books

**Aymé, "The Man Who Walked Through Walls".** Dutilleul's impossible gift first takes effect within an ordinary employee's humiliations, then releases ambitions that exceed his modest appearance. This novel equals Aymé at making the fantastic speak an occupational language: in the hall of the dead, Lesca announces, "You can put that in the minutes as apologies received" (13). It does worse at compression. Aymé lets each escalation change the situation; here the same timer, shopping boxes and evasive answers receive repeated demonstrations. What it adds is a sustained examination of bodily consent. Coralie's "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak" (8) makes possession consequential for the person whose bed is being appropriated. That is more searching than Dutilleul's romantic adventure.

**Maupassant, "The Horla".** Maupassant's diary turns nights, bodily sensations and little experiments into an increasingly uncertain account of an unseen occupier. This novel is comparably exact about the body under siege: "It smelled of cold steel and old tobacco, in a house where nobody had ever smoked" (1). The smell gives the impossible an intimate history. It does worse at keeping perception dangerous. The other village becomes established fact relatively early, and competing explanations soon become excuses we can identify. It adds something Maupassant's isolated narrator cannot supply: another person can distinguish two wills through touch. "I felt both" (4), Coralie says of the holding hand and withdrawing wrist. That evidence also exposes what her relationship lacks.

**Jackson, *The Haunting of Hill House*.** Jackson makes Eleanor's hunger for belonging inseparable from the house's invitation. Her scene of holding a hand in darkness, then discovering that the presumed companion cannot have supplied it, makes intimacy itself frightening. Here the hand is equally effective, and Lesca's desire for "a bed with two pillows" (13) gives the invasion a recognisable temptation. Jackson does better at allowing desire to contaminate narration without being fully named. This novel often states its psychological meaning after the gesture has already conveyed it. Its distinctive achievement is to make the desired home resist the invader through a nurse's professional understanding of consent, while the supernatural threat expands into a public flood.

The affinities are substantial, but I would call it derivative of none of these books. The combination of shared flesh, imported river and civic liability develops its own consequences.

## 3. What is its own

Inguma's overlapping leaves conceal riveted steel. The nightmare seems partly made from the experimental room, yet the closing sound merely suggests their kinship: "like one part of the room touching another" (17). That suggestion is stronger than an explanation would be.

The dead play ordinary village bingo, complete with unsuccessful claims and a duck wrapped in paper. Jean continues marking his cards while Lesca confesses beside him. Death grants her no special hearing.

Most memorable is a gravedigger possessed by the mayor she might have become, with authority distributed unevenly between her hands. The river she hopes to prevent arrives with the woman offering prevention. Folklore survives through a care plan that expressly forbids staff participation. These inventions belong together because the book makes work, care and evasion physical.

## 5. Five faults, worst first

1. **The plot too carefully arranges a solitary chooser.** "She wasn't going to have him turn the handle that would send him to prison" (12). Kévin has expressly accepted that risk, yet Maylis decides for him and sends him elsewhere. Combined with the refused staff loan and evacuation restrictions, this makes solitude feel imposed to preserve the moral experiment. Let her ask him, and make her refusal of his help expose her desire to own the decision. The problem should arise unmistakably from her character.

2. **The counting charm acquires precisely the power the climax needs.** "Then you'll take her home" (13). Earlier counting postpones Inguma, and Maylis cannot recall it when stranded; here a short count becomes a directed expulsion. Folk magic can be irregular, but the new instruction makes this escape feel convenient. Establish that possibility through an earlier, modest use, or make the climax work entirely through the postponement already demonstrated.

3. **Interpretation follows strong images too often.** "It's a whole life with one hand" (9). The stair-post struggle has already made that prospect visible. Cut explanations of the hand, pillow and boxes wherever the preceding action supplies their meaning. Jackson's advantage lies partly in leaving the reader inside the sensation longer.

4. **Too many speakers turn experience into pointed corrections.** "Don't give me the measurement" (17) echoes "Don't give me the minutes" (14). This suits Coralie, but joins the book's pervasive counting, specifications and corrected formulations until the village can sound like one argumentative intelligence. Keep her precision; give supporting characters more speech that wanders, misfires or concerns something outside the governing motifs.

5. **The aftermath completes too many accounts.** "The motion is carried" (16). Confession, medal surrender, gutter repair, council removal and both domestic reckonings supply successive settlements. Shorten the ceremonial and council aftermath, keeping Maïté's withheld cheek and Coralie's departure. The grave already gives responsibility a continuing task.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

- The comic administration of Inguma, especially Madame Etcheto's authority and the staff instruction not to join in.
- Coralie's touch as both skilled care and an independent judgment about whose body she is touching.
- The final digging, including Jeannot's refusal to thank Maylis or accept that he needed rescuing.

## 7. The verdict

Yes, it could stand on that shelf, particularly beside Aymé. It has not achieved Jackson's freedom from explanation or Maupassant's sustained uncertainty, but its inventions have enough force and coherence to survive those comparisons. I would put it forward for a literary prize. The decisive merit is that saving Jeannot neither repairs Maylis's relationship nor cancels Madame Etcheto's death; the last shared labour offers company without acquittal.
