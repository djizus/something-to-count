# The comic village

## 1. Where it belongs

This is a tragic village comedy in which municipal work, gossip and household habits become the means of confronting guilt. Its nearest neighbours are Pagnol's *Jean de Florette* and *Manon of the Springs*, Fitzgerald's *The Bookshop*, and Mills's *The Restraint of Beasts*. The supernatural doubling gives a familiar provincial community a second opportunity to do harm, rather than an escape from its first one.

## 2. Against those books

**Pagnol, *Jean de Florette* and *Manon of the Springs*.** Water here has Pagnol's material authority: who controls it controls other people's lives, and ordinary villagers help catastrophe happen. Jeannot's "Nobody tells me what's under my floorboards and then tells me how to vote" makes wounded pride obstruct his own safety. The novel matches Pagnol's ability to make a local quarrel fatal. It has less breadth: agricultural livelihoods and generations of belonging are sketched around one approaching night, whereas Pagnol makes the land sustain a whole history of desire and dispossession. Its own achievement is giving the person who operates the water two incompatible lives, and making rescue in one life produce another victim. It is not derivative of Pagnol; the shared water politics lead to a different moral difficulty.

**Fitzgerald, *The Bookshop*.** Florence Green's business meets opposition through apparently respectable local arrangements; Peyrebarthe similarly turns permission into a weapon. The neighbouring village's workers are willing, but "Then it isn't one yet" cancels Maylis's request because her mayor has not made it. This matches Fitzgerald's precision about how power makes an ordinary person helpless. Fitzgerald allows more to remain unsaid; here the secretary explains the form, signatures, insurance and forthcoming merger in one conversation. The double adds something Fitzgerald does not attempt: Maylis can inhabit authority and discover that its apparent freedom includes somebody else's enforced deference, embodied in Kévin's cap. The resemblance is a kinship of observation, not derivation.

**Mills, *The Restraint of Beasts*.** Mills makes fencing work, employment and habitual obedience carry the reader into grotesque violence. This novel is equally convincing when a gate is a job before it is a moral symbol: "Every job on that bank had taken her longer than the time she gave it." Its labour has weight, fatigue and accumulated knowledge. It is less willing than Mills to leave absurdity unexplained; the repeated vocabulary of choosing supplies instructions for reading the characters. What it adds is intimate care. A hand can hold a tool, resist possession, betray desire or measure another person's survival. That continuity between paid work and affection is its own. It is not a borrowing of Mills's deadpan machinery.

**Chevallier, *Clochemerle*.** Chevallier's public urinal turns local infrastructure into a struggle over prestige, morality and political allegiance. The floodgate performs a comparable service. The association meeting chaired through a car window deserves that company: "We can't shut the door. The president's on the other side of it." The physical joke explains the distribution of power. Chevallier provides a more populous, unruly comic society; Peyrebarthe's secondary villagers often arrive with a recognisable verbal routine and a task in the plot. Its distinctive gain is that the routines survive disaster but cease to be harmless. Coralie cannot finish the oven-door impersonation after the flood. This is not derivative of *Clochemerle*, though its municipal satire belongs squarely in that lineage.

## 3. What is its own

I have not read this particular conjunction elsewhere: a gravedigger sharing bodily control with her mayoral alternative, while the village reads their struggle as adultery. Nor this afterlife, where the dead play the same bingo as the living and remain too occupied to offer absolution. The best invention is the register of practical objects. A shopping pad becomes a pulse chart and a rescue plan; a complaint becomes testimony without becoming thanks. These objects acquire meaning through use, and remain recognisable things people need.

## 4. Scores

The scale used here is 5 competent, 7 good, 8 fit for the named shelf, 9 a defining book on it.

- **Originality: 8/10.** "Maylis had crossed from one village to the other, and Josiane had still got there first." The double cannot outrun provincial interpretation.
- **Central idea and its reach: 8/10.** "Then it wasn't Inguma that came for her ... It was you." The second life acquires a death of its own; revision cannot erase responsibility.
- **Prose: 7/10.** "The seats could be cleaned. The engine was finished." Concrete, economical distinctions carry the loss. Repeated explanatory sentences occasionally reduce that economy to a manner.
- **People: 8/10.** "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much." Kévin's dream exercise becomes a wish that the rescue was unreal. He exceeds his initial function as helpful poacher.
- **Comedy: 8/10.** "It isn't settled, and you're not to worry about it." Larrère's reassurance becomes funnier with each discovered reason to worry; Coralie's performance makes it shared pleasure.
- **Ending: 8/10.** "It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you." Jeannot offers an account and labour without absolving Maylis. The friendship remains difficult enough to believe.
- **Remembered in a year: 8/10.** "She heard the passage, then the front door, then the car door shutting on a pillow." I expect to remember that departure, the empty forty-second chair and the two shovels.

## 5. Five faults, worst first

1. **Indecision sometimes becomes an implausibly absolute disability.** "I can't push, and I can't give it its work." Maylis can prepare a complex rescue plan, yet cannot choose a counting task already demonstrated to protect her. Her failure makes the fatal encounter possible, so it needs stronger dramatic grounds. Show what she fears losing by keeping Lesca away, or revise the failed instruction so that pressure interrupts a choice she actually makes.

2. **The narration settles a moral question the scene could leave alive.** "If she went on, whatever the river did behind her was the river's doing. She wanted it not to be her." This makes her rescue primarily an evasion before the reader can weigh its mixture of courage, fear and self-deception. Cut the final explanatory sentence and let the turn towards Jeannot, followed by the broken bank, bear the accusation.

3. **The choice motif becomes too neatly prosecutorial.** "You said both. I know that word. I've cooked it." It is a sharp line, but gathering dinner decisions, possession and an attempted double rescue under one verbal fault simplifies their differences. Keep Coralie's immediate grievance, "I was the other one," and lose the cooking interpretation here. The reader already knows the household history.

4. **Procedure occasionally sounds assembled to close every exit.** "The agreement between the two villages starts on 1 January, and before that our men aren't insured on your side." This arrives alongside the withheld warning, resignation threat and refusal to evacuate. Keep the obstruction, but shorten its explanation and give the willing workers some presence beyond voices behind a hand over a telephone. The institution will feel more real if its members have room to act imperfectly.

5. **The supporting villagers can be too consistently available as comic cues.** "It's the eighth this year. I keep count." Even the priest joins the counting motif at the last grave. Let him stop after agreeing that Madame Etcheto's words will do. An occasional person who does not echo the book's vocabulary would make the village larger than its design.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

- The physical competence: the nineteenth turn, the flooded dip, wheelchairs that pull sideways, bags held properly open.
- Coralie's independent desire for Bayonne. Her vocation gives her departure a life beyond Maylis's punishment.
- Jeannot's final refusal to thank, followed by his taking the second shovel. Keep both actions.

## 7. The verdict

Yes, it can stand on that shelf, though Fitzgerald's restraint and Pagnol's social breadth expose its limitations. I would put it forward for a prize read by that jury, without calling it a likely winner. The deciding strength is its ending: responsibility leaves people with work to do together, while forgiveness and domestic restoration remain unavailable.
