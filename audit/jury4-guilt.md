# Tradition 4: guilt, choice and confession

## 1. Where it belongs

This is a comic moral novel in which the life not lived becomes a means of exporting guilt, then a second occasion for incurring it. Its nearest neighbours are *The Fall*, *The Remains of the Day* and *The Visit*: an evaded drowning, professional language used to conceal personal desire, and a community's arithmetic around one death. The fantastic machinery belongs to those questions rather than displacing them.

## 2. Against those books

### Camus, *The Fall*

As well: the novel finds a precise physical measure for an omission. “She didn't wait for the second ring” (10) is as consequential as Clamence's failure to turn back towards the woman's cry: a tiny act becomes a whole subsequent life. Worse: Clamence makes confession itself an exercise of power, drawing his listener into judgement and complicity. Here the eventual admission receives comparatively firm narrative endorsement. Its own contribution is the official sentence “No one came to tell him” (15): literally true, publicly repeated, and used to answer a question about knowledge. Confession must undo a civic record, not merely expose private vanity.

### Ishiguro, *The Remains of the Day*

As well: occupation supplies the language in which intimacy is evaded. “There's a key, and a handle, and Larrère, and a van. And then there's me, as a distance” (9) exposes Maylis's substitution of competent service for an answer to another person. Stevens's professional recollections similarly disclose what his account cannot acknowledge. Worse: Ishiguro lets hindsight, qualification and apparently irrelevant memories gradually betray Stevens; this narrator sometimes tells us exactly what an action means. Its own achievement is Coralie's independent ambition: “I posted it today, and I posted it for me” (8). She can desire the more responsive double and still refuse her, then leave for a life chosen before the disaster.

### Conrad, *Lord Jim*

As well: a short decision acquires a bodily afterlife. The scar, clenched hand and repeated trial make guilt something performed rather than merely discussed. “The arm is the excuse you've written for me. It isn't mine” (17) rejects an exonerating account as firmly as Jim resists being reduced to an ordinary explanation of his jump. Worse: Conrad's competing witnesses and Marlow's uncertainties make moral knowledge itself unstable; here we largely receive the decisive events directly. Its own distinction is refusing death as the final payment. Lesca must return, speak, surrender office and stack chairs. Maylis must live beside the man she saved while digging the grave of the woman she did not.

### Dürrenmatt, *The Visit*

As well: civic procedure makes complicity funny before it becomes dreadful. “Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station” (6) turns grammar into responsibility; the timer repeatedly prevents the question the town must hear. Worse: Dürrenmatt lets the townspeople's credit purchases betray a shared decision before they admit it. This village's obstruction is more concentrated in one president, his paperwork and his blackmail. Its own contribution is that communal routines also sustain people: bingo becomes evacuation seating; the poacher's van carries residents; the supposedly expendable hunter carries an old man. The community cannot be dismissed as uniformly corrupt.

The novel is not derivative of any of these. *The Fall* supplies the closest moral resemblance, but neither the doubled agency nor the two unequal outcomes reproduces Clamence's method.

## 3. What is its own

I have not encountered this combination of a shared body, responsibility divided between its hands, a municipal floodgate and a leaf-covered night visitor wearing the steel of an experimental room. The funniest invention is also the ethical one: an appetite entered on a shopping pad belongs to the same problem as an order to inundate fields. Most distinctive is the refusal of the apparent rescue narrative. One woman opens the gate and kills a man; her counterpart saves him and leaves another person to drown. Neither life can repair the other retrospectively.

## 5. Five faults, worst first

1. **The fantastic world sometimes certifies the moral verdict.** Lesca's “It's the ti—” cannot finish in the steel room (9); her final “I won't” comes with “It came out whole” (17). That contrast gives honesty an external test, narrowing the difficult uncertainty about motives. I would remove the conspicuous speech blockage and leave the promise to be judged through conduct. Keep the room and the crossing.

2. **The emergency has too many convenient barriers to help.** “They've got their coats, but I've told them they can't” (12) adds insurance to signatures, colour warnings, blackmail and obedience. Their accumulation makes the dilemma feel arranged. I would remove one administrative refusal and let an available offer expose what Maylis herself will accept, delay or decline.

3. **The narrator occasionally closes an interpretation the scene has earned.** “She wanted it not to be her” (14) supplies the verdict on Maylis's preference for letting the river act. This is plausible, but it excludes mixed courage, fear and desperation at precisely the richest moment. I would cut that sentence and trust the steps towards the gate followed by the turn away.

4. **Madame Etcheto's death is more intricately prepared as a pattern than as the loss of a particular life.** “Twelve. One, two” (15) brings her room number into the dead's game with formidable neatness. Her stubbornness and dress given to Jeannot's mother help, but the pattern still dominates. I would give one existing exchange a concern beyond Inguma, her room and her coming death, rather than add another portent.

5. **Repeated explanations sometimes reduce a motif to its meaning.** “I know that word. I've cooked it” (17) makes the dinner-choice correspondence explicit after the gate and trapdoor have already demonstrated it. I would cut that explanation, retaining “I was the other one” and the failed oven impersonation. Their collision carries the connection without spelling it out.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

- The single unanswered ring, and the difference between knowing, warning and opening.
- Coralie's refusal to enter another woman's bed over someone who cannot speak, followed by her decision to leave for herself.
- The ordinary labour after public disgrace: chairs, gutter, bandage, grave. Repair continues without cancelling the death.

## 7. The verdict

Yes, it stands on this shelf as a smaller, less stylistically expansive neighbour. I would put it forward for a literary prize, without calling it a likely winner. The deciding achievement is that saving Jeannot does not absolve Maylis: confession cannot buy back the dead woman, the lost office or Coralie. The final second shovel offers company in work, not a certificate of innocence.
