# Tradition 5: the literary speculative novel a prize jury reads

## 1. Where it belongs

This is a comic village novel whose speculative device makes responsibility bodily: two versions of a municipal worker compete for one body, one lover and the right to decide who gets wet. Its nearest shelves are Hervé Le Tellier's *The Anomaly*, Kate Atkinson's *Life After Life* and Kazuo Ishiguro's *Never Let Me Go*: alternative lives, consequential repetition and the language people use to live beside what they have done.

## 2. Against those books

**Le Tellier, *The Anomaly*.** This novel is as good at finding social comedy inside an impossible encounter. “Then it's carried, or it's a tie, and in a tie the chair decides” (4) gives the double an entire political temperament through a procedural joke. It has less variety of voice and less intellectual range than Le Tellier's ensemble. Its particular achievement is making duplication an invasion: the contested wrist, mouth and stair post turn the abstract question of which self deserves a life into an immediate question of consent. Coralie's “I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak” (8) gives that question a convincing professional and intimate vocabulary. It is not derivative of *The Anomaly*; duplication is shared material, while the dramatic machinery and social world are different.

**Atkinson, *Life After Life*.** Like Atkinson, it makes repetition alter the meaning of a familiar action, rather than merely supply another outcome. One phone ring becomes nine; a rescue becomes an abandonment. “Maylis had given the bank ten minutes, and it had held for sixteen” (14) is particularly good: reality exceeds the calculation without becoming merciful. It does worse at supplying a life outside the repeated catastrophe. Atkinson's successive childhoods, households and wartime lives make recurrence inhabit a whole historical world; here almost everything points towards the gate. Its own contribution is the opposed ethical failure of two simultaneous selves: acting can kill, but withholding action cannot keep one's hands clean. It is not derivative of Atkinson's recurrence structure.

**Ishiguro, *Never Let Me Go*.** Its best institutional language deserves the comparison. “No one came to tell him” (15) is factually true, socially usable and morally evasive. The novel makes a public ceremony expose the concealment carried by six ordinary words. It is less subtle than Ishiguro about what characters cannot admit: explanations often follow the discovery. Kathy's retrospective narration makes evasion part of the reader's experience; this narrator more often names it. What this novel adds is resistance through practical labour and a lover who wants a particular hospital floor, rather than merely a place in the protagonist's redemption. Coralie's “I posted it today, and I posted it for me” (8) matters. There is no derivative voice or borrowed institutional allegory.

**Ted Chiang, “Anxiety Is the Dizziness of Freedom.”** Chiang's communication between divergent branches makes alternative selves a problem of responsibility rather than proof that one's character was inevitable. This novel takes that problem equally seriously: “She wanted it not to be her” (14) defeats the comforting distinction between saving someone and causing harm. It does worse at integrating the scientific premise into the discovery. Chiang's prisms remain intelligible parts of his characters' world; the university experiment here largely hands over to folklore. What this novel does that Chiang's story does not is embody divided agency in a struggling pair of hands and extend its consequences into municipal work, flood maintenance and communal remembrance. The resemblance is conceptual, not derivative.

## 3. What is its own

The combination feels original: a gravedigger's occupational arithmetic, an intrusive alternative mayor, Basque nightmare folklore and a flood whose arrival accompanies the visitor. Above all, the afterlife is village bingo. The dead neither pronounce judgement nor grant absolution: Castagnet keeps marking his cards while Lesca confesses. The ordinary institution survives death and ignores the protagonist's need for an audience.

The comedy also becomes an instrument of intimacy. Coralie's oven impersonations first sustain the couple; in the last chapter she cannot finish one. A damaged rib interrupts laughter before it interrupts rescue. These are connections between bodily vulnerability and companionship that I have not encountered in this particular form elsewhere.

## 5. Five faults, worst first

1. **The argument sometimes explains its own evidence.** “I know that word. I've cooked it” (17) translates “Both” back into the established dinner-choice lesson after the flood has complicated that lesson enormously. Cut this gloss. Keep Coralie's accusation and Maylis's admission; readers can make the connection without being steered towards a single moral formula.

2. **The obstruction of practical alternatives feels heavily arranged.** “They say they'd come ... but I've told them they can't” (12) adds insurance and a future municipal agreement to the many barriers already preventing help. Each is plausible; their accumulation makes the crisis feel prepared for the ethical test. Compress this refusal and trim repeated official prohibitions. Let fewer obstacles carry more weight.

3. **The experiment promises more scientific substance than the book supplies.** “What we want to know is whether there is another you, somewhere, who sees the other one” (1) almost announces the subsequent plot. The final contact between Inguma's steel and the room's frame offers an association, not an account of that research. Make the opening researchers' claim less comprehensive; preserve the strange room without advertising an investigation the novel will not pursue.

4. **Some secondary people remain wonderfully efficient functions.** “It's adultery whichever she's after” (5) is excellent Josiane, but her readings of events repeatedly deliver the same social mechanism. Keep her sharpest interventions and cut weaker repetitions. The chorus needs less predictable recurrence, not another subplot.

5. **Physical clarity occasionally becomes over-instruction.** “The hand on the brake was the weaker, and it had got there first” (13) explains a contest the surrounding action already demonstrates. Trim these explanatory bridges across the possession scenes. Preserve the concrete movements and the distinction between the hands; reduce the accompanying account of why each movement wins.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

- The dead Castagnet's indifference to confession. His second card is more unsettling than a verdict would be.
- Coralie's independent ambition, her refusal to cross the landing over an unwilling person, and her eventual departure.
- The last shared grave-digging: Jeannot offers labour without gratitude, pardon or a rewritten account of the night.

## 7. The verdict

Yes, it would stand on that shelf. I would put it forward for a literary prize, though its occasionally insistent explanation would enter the jury discussion. The deciding achievement is that the alternative life cannot redeem the original one: the second rescue produces another death, and the final companionship consists of helping to dig its grave.
