# Literary report: the uncanny and the folk tale

## 1. Where it belongs

This is a village supernatural tale in which a counting charm opens onto possession, public guilt and the cost of wanting another life. Its nearest neighbours are Marcel Aymé’s “The Man Who Walked Through Walls,” Kafka’s *The Metamorphosis* and Shirley Jackson’s *The Haunting of Hill House*: the impossible becomes domestic fact, and domestic fact becomes terrible.

## 2. Against those books

**Aymé, “The Man Who Walked Through Walls.”** It matches Aymé’s ability to put an impossible bodily power among ordinary jobs, officials and humiliations without changing narrative temperature. “The secretary bent down and put the next letter on the floor beside Lesca’s hand, and the hand signed it” (6): excellent, because neither the institution nor the hand sees a reason to stop. Aymé gets more acceleration from fewer encounters; this novel sometimes rehearses its administrative jokes after they have established the village. Its difference is substantial: supernatural escape produces an account owed to people who cannot escape. The surviving hunter helps dig the dead woman’s grave. This is kinship, not derivation.

**Kafka, *The Metamorphosis*.** The novel is comparably good at making bodily estrangement a problem of work and dependence. “It’s a whole life with one hand. I won’t pay that” (9) makes dispossession material before making it symbolic. But Kafka lets the family’s changing behaviour disclose a horror larger than any stated explanation; here speakers sometimes supply the interpretation immediately. What this novel adds is an argument between two versions of an ordinary worker, both implicated and neither reducible to victim or monster. It tests incompatible actions rather than making bodily exclusion the whole catastrophe. It is not derivative of Kafka.

**Jackson, *The Haunting of Hill House*.** It does as well at making the promise of belonging dangerous. Lesca’s motion, “take the other one’s bed, and Coralie Mounet in it” (3), joins longing to appropriation with frightening economy. Coralie’s refusal gives that temptation bodily resistance: “I don’t get into a bed over somebody who can’t speak” (8). Jackson sustains greater uncertainty between a haunting and a consciousness constructing its desired home; this book eventually tells us which visitor brings which water. Its own achievement is to give the desired woman a profession, ambitions and a right to refuse both claimants. She is more than the destination of somebody else’s hunger. No derivation.

**Maupassant, “The Horla.”** The bedside intruder and commandeered body are the clearest resemblance. “An answer went past Lesca on its way to the mouth” (3) is an excellent rendering of divided agency: thought becomes something with traffic and priority. The opening’s pressure, tobacco smell and leaf fragments achieve the intimacy of Maupassant’s invasion. The novel does less with uncertainty about the perceiver’s sanity; its double soon becomes a separately observed person. It does something the diary tale does not: distribute the consequences of possession across a community and make testimony matter. A shared nightmare tradition does not make it derivative of “The Horla.”

## 3. What is its own

The dead play village bingo without becoming either wise ancestors or a welcoming audience. A confession cannot interrupt their numbers: “He put a grain on his second card” (13). That indifference is more disturbing than a tribunal would be.

Inguma’s counting work belongs to the same world as trailers of leaves, roadside cutting, gravel extraction and care plans. The charm survives through elderly women and nursing practice; its misuse is a mistake about labour, including the difference between tractor speed and walking. The creature’s steel beneath its leaves keeps the experimental room and inherited tale touching without making either explain the other.

Most distinctive is the exchange of casualties without moral compensation. One Castagnet lives; one Etcheto dies. Neither arithmetic nor confessing makes the two deaths equivalent. The ending’s second shovel offers company in work, not acquittal.

## 5. Five faults, worst first

1. **The supernatural sometimes receives its interpretation twice.** “When she comes over into me she brings her river with her” (9) supplies the diagnosis after the dry days, resumed rain and “You’re the water” have made it available. Keep the evidence and the decision to expel Lesca; compress the explanatory speech. The action must be intelligible, but the reader needs some work left to do.

2. **Maylis’s inability to choose occasionally becomes an imposed verbal mechanism.** “Either” at the daylight gate (12), after her attempted opening, costs her the clearest opportunity to avert disaster. Coming after the failed bedtime charm, it risks making catastrophe follow a catchword rather than a person. Give the daylight retreat its particular fear in her own terms; remove one earlier failure on the same word.

3. **Some institutional barriers appear assembled to guarantee solitary ordeal.** “On orange nobody leaves the building” (12) sits beside the rejected loan of workers, the vote, blackmail and orders to wait. Kafka’s institutions make their subjects’ entire existence precarious; these sometimes look arranged to close each practical exit. Cut one redundant obstruction and let the remaining refusals carry the pressure.

4. **Physical danger is planted too explicitly.** “He said his mouth tasted of coins. He coughed, and it came out pink on the sheet” (7) supplies the exact symptoms subsequently reproduced. The long warning briefly makes Coralie an advance narrator of the coming injury. Shorten it; keep the pain and emerging symptoms in Maylis’s present experience.

5. **Maïté’s desire remains more concealed than developed.** “That’s winter things. In the— It’s blankets” (4) makes the packed suitcase a clue, paid by her eventual departure. Her privacy is persuasive, but the lover and departing wife receives less independent inward weight than Coralie. Replace one clue with a moment in the existing kitchen scene where Maïté wants something that is neither concealment nor escape.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

- The dead’s absorption in their cards, especially their refusal to reward confession with attention.
- Coralie’s skilled hands and independent ambition: they establish both recognition and consent.
- The final shared digging, with Jeannot’s refusal to call his letter thanks. The work continues after the verdicts.

## 7. The verdict

Yes, it stands on that shelf, particularly beside Aymé’s stories of impossible powers and ordinary appetites. I would put it forward for a prize judged by readers of these books. The deciding achievement is that saving the man does not redeem the choice: the drowned woman remains a person owed a grave, and the rescued man takes the other shovel.
