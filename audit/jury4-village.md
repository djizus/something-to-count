# The comic village: literary report

## 1. Where it belongs

This is a comic village novel in which a fantastic doubling exposes the damage hidden inside ordinary competence, public procedure and private affection. Its nearest neighbours are Chevallier's *Clochemerle*, Pagnol's *Jean de Florette* and Fitzgerald's *The Bookshop*: a community makes its own disasters, then decides whose name to put on them.

## 2. Against those books

**Chevallier, *Clochemerle*.** Chevallier makes a public urinal reveal the village's political, sexual and religious divisions; here a floodgate gathers competing interests just as economically. Larrère chairs an association meeting from his car: “We can't shut the door,” because “The president's on the other side of it.” This is excellent comedy of authority occupying actual space. The novel has less social abundance than Chevallier: the café repeatedly produces the same sexual explanation, and several villagers remain their office or habitual remark. Its distinctive gain is the intimacy of that public machinery. Coralie impersonates the mayor through the oven door; village satire becomes something a couple shares, then can no longer share. It is not derivative of *Clochemerle*.

**Pagnol, *Jean de Florette* and *Manon of the Springs*.** Pagnol makes control of water, neighbourly silence and ownership inseparable; this novel is comparably good at turning geography into moral pressure. “The trapdoor was twenty-five minutes away”; “The bank had ten.” Nobody needs an abstract argument about competing obligations. It does worse at making the community's knowledge feel uneven and organically accumulated: its inhabitants are often assembled to witness precisely the next necessary humiliation. Pagnol's landscape and agricultural hopes have more independent life. What this novel adds is a genuinely divided rescue: one woman must confront another version of herself, and saving the threatened man does not save her innocence. The kinship is strong, but the plot is not a borrowing from Pagnol.

**Fitzgerald, *The Bookshop*.** Fitzgerald's polite local obstruction destroys Florence Green's practical enterprise. Here the neighbouring town's staff have their coats ready, but “Then it isn't one yet”: a request does not exist until the proper mayor makes it. That conversion of everyday speech into exclusion is worthy of Fitzgerald. Her novel gives secondary figures more room to resist interpretation; this one more often tells us exactly what a refusal means. Its own achievement is to make the competent protagonist culpable too. “I know what you picked. I was the other one” prevents Coralie from becoming merely the loyal companion of a person wronged by institutions. Again, affinity rather than derivation.

**Magnus Mills, *The Restraint of Beasts*.** Mills lets practical work and obedience develop into absurd menace without changing the workers' matter-of-fact register. The secretary placing a letter beside the mayor lying on the floor, so the surviving hand can sign it, achieves that composure. This novel is less willing to leave absurdity unexplained: “Inguma was doing the roadsides on foot” supplies the answer to its own joke. It surpasses Mills in emotional range, particularly when workplace routines become the means of preserving vulnerable bodies. Its fantastic creature also belongs physically to the work: leaves over riveted steel. That image is its own.

## 3. What is its own

The two versions of one municipal worker fighting over a body through its hands: a lover recognises the silent original through the wrist while the more persuasive double speaks. The dead's bingo hall, which retains handbags, cheating and the duck prize rather than becoming solemn. The flood travelling with a woman's wish to inhabit an unspoiled life. Most of all, Kévin wanting a sixth finger during the evacuation: a comic exercise in recognising dreams becomes the wish that an unbearable event were unreal. I have not read that particular transformation elsewhere.

## 5. Five faults, worst first

1. **The village's obedience sometimes serves the plot too conveniently.** “A vote was a vote” dismisses the nine petitioners who wanted the gate opened. Their withdrawal helps manufacture the solitary dilemma. Give one petitioner a concrete, costly reason for withdrawing and another an unsuccessful action. The problem is collective behaviour reduced to a sentence, not the absence of more helpers.

2. **Madame Etcheto risks becoming the payment for someone else's moral education.** “Twelve. One, two.” Then: “Somebody pulled a chair in to a table.” Coming before Lesca's return from the dead, this invites a substitution reading the novel neither establishes nor resists. Etcheto's stubbornness has life, but her loss is chiefly carried by other people's reckonings. Give her one earlier exchange with an interest beyond Inguma and room twelve; retain the terrible restraint of the discovery.

3. **The prose sometimes states the significance it has already earned.** “She wanted it not to be her” follows the explicit distinction between killing by opening the gate and letting the river kill. Keep the ugly reasoning, which matters, but cut the concluding gloss. Apply that cut wherever an action is followed by its interpretation.

4. **Different occupations too often speak in the same polished rebuttal.** “Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.” Beside Coralie's “It's soup. It doesn't need a vote,” the same comic construction travels too freely. Keep the occupational vocabularies; let some exchanges be less finished, especially outside the central couple.

5. **Some returns announce their own transformation.** “It's not a complaint”; “It's the first one that isn't.” Letter forty-two already changes the meaning of Jeannot's correspondence through its contents and his arrival. Cut the second announcement and let the changed act register. Several motifs could survive with one fewer explanation or reprise.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

- Comedy grounded in physical work: windows, towels, sandbags, chairs and the stubborn handle.
- Coralie's independent desire for Bayonne, and her refusal to make either affection or competent care mean consent.
- The unequal aftermaths: confession costs Lesca her office; Maylis saves Jeannot and loses Coralie. Neither woman receives a clean moral balance sheet.

## 7. The verdict

Yes, it would stand on this shelf, and yes, I would put it forward for a prize judged by readers of these books. Its civic world needs a little more freedom from plot necessity, but its comedy survives contact with death, and its ending understands that useful work can resume before forgiveness arrives.
