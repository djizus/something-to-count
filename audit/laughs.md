|---|---|---|
| 1, 6, 7, 10 and throughout | "we thought you were still in the box" | I am told the village has found this funny for twelve years. I never did, not once. The variants ("She's checking the box gave them all back", ch. 5; "So the box gave them all back", ch. 6; "All back?", ch. 7) got one small smile in ch. 5 and nothing after. ## Three. Two registers or one

Mostly one, and it is the book's best quality. The same flat, procedural voice tells the jokes and the deaths, so the two do not have to be joined. "The mairie had two months in which to answer, and the sender drowned on the fourth day" (ch. 2) is one sentence and it is both. So is the minute's silence on a kitchen timer, the widow's "He'd have sent the mairie a registered letter about it", Sarthou asking whether his kitchen is under the bank, the letter that cannot be un-received by a woman who has him down as dead, and the chrysanthemums. In those places the comedy is the dark part, seen from the counter side.

It breaks into two in one place, and it is a big one: the nights. Every scene of the weight and the steel room is written with no comedy at all. They are careful, slow, repeated in near-identical wording ("The weight came at 02:51, and she let it take her"), and nobody in them says anything a person would say. Whenever the book goes into the box it becomes a different, graver book, and whenever it comes out to the café or the mairie counter it becomes the funny one again. I felt the gear change each time. The only night material that is funny is what people say about it in daylight (the minuted finger count, "minutes her nights", "People on the forum would pay for that", Coralie's "They didn't write it down this neatly").

A second, smaller split runs between the two women. The worker's chapters are the comic ones: the loto, the letters, the mower, the chrysanthemums. The mayor's chapters are colder, and their comedy is nearly all Larrère being timed. By the middle of the book I expected odd chapters to be warm and even chapters to be hard, and I was mostly right. Ch. 12 is the exception and is the mayor's best chapter for it.

Bluntly: this is a wry book, not a funny one. Almost everything is one dry line that closes a scene. Nothing gets out of hand. The only sequence that builds across several beats and several people, each making it worse, is the chrysanthemums in ch. 15, and it is the one place I laughed more than once in a row. The counter scene in ch. 13 is the other. Both come late. For the first twelve chapters the comedy is a tone of voice, pleasant and steady, leaning on five or six running gags that are worn out by the halfway mark.
