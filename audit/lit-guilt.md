# The literary read: guilt, choice and confession

## 1. Where it belongs

A short moral fable in comic-village dress. A woman who once drowned one man to save forty houses is given the night again, in another village and another woman's body, and finds that she is the flood. It stands nearest to Camus's *The Fall*, Dürrenmatt's *The Visit* and Conrad's *Lord Jim*, with Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day* behind Maylis.

## 2. Against those books

**Camus, *The Fall*.** Clamence ends by begging the girl to jump again so that he can save them both, then adds that fortunately it is too late. This novel grants the wish and reaches Camus's answer by its own road. Asked what she is crossing for, Lesca tries "to get him out" and it will not come: "'To stay,' she said. 'That's the one that comes out.'" Her public sentence, "No one came to tell him", true in every word and false in sum, is a judge-penitent's sentence, and as good as his. It betters Camus in one thing: the confession costs. Clamence confesses to trap his listener. Lesca's takes the sash and the medal and buys nothing on the landing. It is worse where Camus is greatest. He does bad faith with a voice, and the reader has to catch it. Here a steel room catches it for us.

**Dürrenmatt, *The Visit* and *The Pledge*.** As well as he does it: the village as a chorus that is wrong at full confidence ("She wants water"), and a ceremony whose form swallows the event: "Madame Castagnet will lay the flowers." The dry Port voting her out, "Their hands were no slower than the rest", is pure Güllen. From *The Pledge* it takes the sound plan beaten by a ditch: "Every job on that bank had taken her longer than the time she gave it." Worse: Güllen is guilty and the play says of what. Peyrebarthe lived twelve years on a drowned man and is never asked what it owes, and its second flood arrives by magic, where Dürrenmatt's arrives by cheque. What it has that he lacks is warmth. His townsmen are a mechanism. Coralie is not.

**Conrad, *Lord Jim*.** Jim says, "I had jumped… it seems." Lesca has the sum, but "all she could remember was turning the handle." It is the same hole where a motive should be, and the novel leaves it open between forty houses and one car in a yard: "That sounded no more true, and no less." The other village is Patusan, the second chance that carries the old harm to a new place. Worse: Marlow circles the jump until it cannot be exhausted, and this night is told once, as a report. What Conrad does not have is the witness who refuses to know: "Because you'd have told me."

**Ishiguro, *The Remains of the Day*.** "Either" is Stevens's "dignity", and Coralie waits to be asked as Miss Kenton waits. The shopping pad with its boxes is an object Ishiguro would envy. Worse: Ishiguro never glosses Stevens. This narrator does: "What she wanted was for Coralie to stay awake… so Maylis didn't ask."

It is derivative of none of them. It owes Camus the premise and Dürrenmatt the square, and repays both.

## 3. What is its own

- **Both branches are played and both are convicted.** Lesca turned the handle and drowned a man. Maylis refuses it, "She wanted it not to be her", and a woman of ninety-four drowns in room twelve. One was decorated for it and the other is told she "went for a walk". I have not read the dilemma run twice with no verdict.
- **Conscience as a hand.** "The owner keeps her right hand." Guilt in this tradition is interior. Here it grips a stair post and a handbrake.
- **The dead at bingo**, and a death reported as a call: "'Twelve. One, two.' Somebody pulled a chair in to a table."
- **A demon as bailiff**, bought off with counting, and written into a care plan: "Staff to wait. Staff not to join in."
- **Bad faith drafted as a council motion**: "That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it."
- **The penitent as the catastrophe**: "You're the water."
- **A rescue that earns no thanks**: "It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you."

## 4. Scores

- **Originality: 8.** "The one it cannot take home dies in that bed. After that she plays bingo with the rest of the dead."
- **The central idea and how far it is taken: 8.** Taken to both ends: "If she turned that handle, Jeannot drowned and her hand had done it. If she went on, whatever the river did behind her was the river's doing."
- **The prose: 7.** At its best it is exact and deadpan: "The loudspeaker on the bank road said 'drowned him' after the square had finished hearing it." It has one pitch, and it explains.
- **The people: 7.** Coralie is an 8: "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." The village is a 6.
- **The comedy: 8.** "Castagnet, Opened. It reads as if he was opened." I smiled on most pages, including the gravest.
- **What the ending does: 8.** It refuses absolution and hands out work: "Somebody has to stack the chairs."
- **Whether I will remember it in a year: 8.** The chair pulled in at the bingo table. I will not remember the rules.

## 5. Five faults, worst first

1. **It explains its own moral.** "Twelve years ago she had let his phone ring once and then done what she had come to do. Holding this wheel was the same thing." Again in the last chapter: "At the gate I thought I could do better than you… I drove past the handle to be the one who did." Cut every line where narrator or character draws the parallel between the two nights. Reduce the last steel-room scene to "Forty-one", "Don't come again" and "I won't."

2. **The rulebook.** "So that's a fourth point, and I didn't have it on my list. Standing up in this room, a woman can't say a thing she knows isn't true." By chapter 4 the reader holds the chair, the seat, the right hand, the counting, the pictures, the fingers and the truth rule. A book about one act should not need a manual. Keep the word that sticks and strike its announcement as a rule. Thin the first four chapters until the night at the gate stands clear of the mechanism.

3. **The second flood has a magical cause.** "The rain came when Lesca came." The novel raises the human cause, a mayor sitting on the money and a handle stuck at nineteen turns, and then has the room refuse it. That lightens Larrère and puts Maylis's choice under fate. The human-sized guilt is that Lesca knew from the 6th and came anyway, and it gets one line: "I can't stand here and say I won't." Give it a scene.

4. **The village speaks in tics, and the victim is one of them.** "One. It's Jeannot. Two. Jean's for my gravestone." Dürrenmatt's Ill becomes a man before he dies. Dead Jean is two jackets and a stopped watch, and live Jeannot is a numbered list until the cemetery. Give him one scene off his routine before the night, and ration "I'll be brief".

5. **Thriller furniture, and glosses in mouths.** "03:08. Six hundred metres to go. She ran." And one mayor telling another: "The prefecture at Pau, the State's office over all these villages, sends you a yellow warning." Keep the two times that carry meaning, 2:51 and 3:11. Cut every explanation no speaker would give.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

1. The unanswered motive, and Coralie's reason for never asking: "I wanted you in the house, and I thought I could have you without it, if I kept a landing between us."
2. The confession held inside the ceremony: the late loudspeaker, the flowers laid on schedule, the medal handed back across the tape, the chairs.
3. Comedy in the gravest rooms: "Here," from Monsieur Daguerre, and twelve called in the hall of the dead.

## 7. The verdict

Chapters 11 and 13 to 16 would stand on that shelf today, beside *The Pledge* and *The Visit*, not beside *The Fall*. The whole book does not stand there yet, because it trusts its machinery and its glosses more than its reader. I would put it forward for a prize. A jury that knows these books will grant it the idea, the comedy and the confession scene, and will divide over the rulebook. Cut the explaining and I would argue for it.
