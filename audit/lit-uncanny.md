# The literary read: the uncanny and the folk tale

## 1. Where it belongs

A folk tale told in a town-hall register. A Basque night demon ferries a village gravedigger into the self who once chose, and the book treats the haunting as municipal business. It stands nearest Marcel Aymé (the *Passe-muraille* stories, *La Vouivre*), Maupassant's "The Horla" and Bulgakov. Shirley Jackson's villages are the fourth neighbour.

## 2. Against those books

**Aymé, "The Man Who Walked Through Walls".** The same method: one impossible fact, received by clerks. Dutilleul puts his head through the wall to torment his office chief. Lesca chairs a meeting inside a dream: "'All those in favour.' 'There are two of us,' Maylis said. 'Then it's carried, or it's a tie, and in a tie the chair decides.' 'There's nobody in the chair.'" That is Aymé's level. Worse: Aymé has a narrator, amused and audible in every sentence. This novel has one such moment ("Call her Lesca") and then the narrator goes silent, so the characters must carry all the wit. What Aymé does not do: make the joke pay a moral bill. His clerk ends stuck in a wall, which is a punchline. This ends with a drowned woman of 94 and "You had the key."

**Maupassant, "The Horla".** The weight on the chest, the paralysis, the experiment that proves the visitor is real. Maupassant's carafe is Bordenave's pad with its cross and Coralie's three columns, and the test is as good: "A tide comes in twice a day. It doesn't take Thursday off." Worse, and it matters: the Horla frightens because nobody knows its rules. Inguma keeps office hours, and by chapter 4 its rules are read out in numbered points. I was never afraid of it. What Maupassant does not do: turn the visitor into the remedy. "I've been fighting it since the rib, and half the time it was doing my job."

**Bulgakov, *The Master and Margarita*.** Bingo night is the Variety Theatre at village scale. The supernatural crosses a public room and the room files it under adultery: "'She wants water,' one of the Lassalle sisters said." Lesca is also a small Pilate, awake at the same minute for twelve years over a man she let die. As good in the set piece. Smaller everywhere else, with no second register to rise into. Its own: Pilate is released. Lesca confesses and is told, "Somebody has to stack the chairs."

**Jackson, "The Lottery" and *We Have Always Lived in the Castle*.** The village that decides who pays, then turns: "'Late,' the dealer said... 'Had the key and went for a walk, did you?'" Jackson's menace is missing. Her mercy is here: Marie-Jo's "Just the one. Sit down on it" is the food left on the Blackwood doorstep. What Jackson never does: let the scapegoat be guilty.

Derivative? Of none of these. The charm is the one the collectors recorded, as I know it, with God and the Virgin cut out; the invention is to take its stalling clause as paid labour. The borrowed part comes from outside this tradition. The university box that splits the world is stock speculative furniture.

## 3. What is its own

- Folk belief written into a care plan: "Staff to wait. Staff not to join in."
- The village dead at bingo, with corn for counters, and a death reported only as sound: "'Twelve. One, two.' Somebody pulled a chair in to a table."
- The ghost is the survivor. The woman who saved forty houses brings her river with her: "You're the water."
- A room where a lie sticks in the mouth like the handle at nineteen turns: "It's the ti—"
- A marriage told on a shopping pad: "'I don't cook Either,' Coralie said. 'There's no recipe for it.'"
- A boy who counts his fingers hoping the flood is a dream: "I wanted six, Maylis."
- A dilemma run down both tracks, with neither woman let off.
- Everyone counts: leaves, pulses, letters, turns, heads. The demon's task is the whole village's habit.

## 4. Scores

- **Originality: 8.** "She reported him. She said he had made it lose its count, and it came at 1 instead of 3."
- **The central idea and how far it is taken: 7.5.** Taken to its end: "I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me." Held back because the hinge, that the visitor causes the flood, is declared and never felt.
- **The prose: 6.5.** Exact and object-led at its best: "She took more than that to the dump in a morning." But it has one register for a shopping list and for the hall of the dead.
- **The people: 8.** "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them."
- **The comedy: 8.** "Castagnet, Opened. It reads as if he was opened."
- **What the ending does: 8.** "It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you."
- **Memory in a year: 8.** "The window went down. It went all the way down, into the door."

## 5. Five faults

1. **The uncanny is audited until it stops being uncanny.** "'The owner keeps her right hand. I couldn't get it off you. Last point. Inguma takes the visitor home when it has finished counting.'" Aickman gives no rules. A folk tale gives three. I count ten here: the right hand, the shared memory, the seat, the truth room, the cooling body, the count, the words, the place at the table, the rain, the minute. Cut Lesca's list to what the reader has already watched happen. Leave the rain as Coralie's chart and Lesca's failed lie, never as a law.

2. **The narrator explains the scene just shown.** "Twelve years ago she had let his phone ring once and then done what she had come to do. Holding this wheel was the same thing." And: "Maylis had let it ring nine times, and Jeannot was alive." The reader had both. Go through chapters 12 to 14 and strike every sentence that states an equivalence.

3. **Inguma has no will.** "It took her home." It is a ferry with leaves on. Those leaves cover "grey steel underneath, with a row of rivets", which promises that the folk demon and the laboratory box are one thing. The book never collects on it. Give Inguma one act outside its timetable, or one late sentence that lets the reader see the box under the leaves. Do not show its face.

4. **The chair is an exposition pipe.** "Maylis knew why, from the chair. Lesca had put cameras on the gates and sent the film to the gendarmes, and Kévin had done six months in prison, for eels." Lesca's village (the lake, the four houses, the plastic bag) arrives mostly this way and is thinner than Maylis's. Let Maylis meet the cap in Kévin's hands without the reason, and learn it later from somebody's mouth.

5. **Every object pays out.** The timer, the suitcase, the reflector, the kitchen clock, the gutter, the duck, the oven door three times. "'I said that house,' she said. 'I only had the one name wrong.'" It satisfies and it is airless. Jackson and Aickman leave a door open. Leave two things unredeemed. The climax is tidy in the same way, by the clock ("02:59", "03:03", "03:06"). Keep 2:51 and 3:11 and drop half the rest.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

- Coralie and the pad. She asks the only question that matters, and she leaves.
- "Twelve. One, two." and the hall of the dead, exactly as quiet as it is.
- The refusal to acquit. Jeannot's letter "doesn't thank you", and Maylis answers: "The arm is the excuse you've written for me. It isn't mine."

## 7. The verdict

It stands on the shelf at the Aymé end, beside *La Vouivre* and Atxaga's *Obabakoak*: the comic fantastic with a village and a conscience. It does not stand beside "The Horla" or Aickman, because nothing in it is left dark.

I would put it forward for a prize and expect a longlist. A jury that knows these books will admire the construction and the people. It will also say that the sentences serve where Clarke's and Jackson's sing, and that the book explains what it has already shown.
