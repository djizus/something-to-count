# The literary read: the comic village

## 1. Where it belongs

A village comedy of paperwork with a folk demon and a double inside it, which becomes in its last third a book about who pays for a decision. It stands nearest to Pagnol's *Manon of the Springs* (water, a silence, a confession in the square), Fitzgerald's *The Bookshop* (a quiet woman, a patron with a folder, a ghost kept as a household fact, no consolation) and Aymé's *La Vouivre* (a village that handles the supernatural as council business). Its café is Chevallier's, on a smaller budget.

## 2. Against those books

**Pagnol, *Jean de Florette* and *Manon of the Springs*.** The moral skeleton is Pagnol's: a man dies over water because someone who knew kept quiet. Larrère ringing from the Castagnet kitchen is the village that knew where the spring was. The novel matches Pagnol in the square: "She watched her sentence travel back through the square and down the road, row by row, at walking pace." It does worse on the antagonist. Papet has a desire, a past and a letter that breaks him. Larrère has a car window. What it does that Pagnol does not: it puts the guilt in the heroine, runs the night twice, and lets the woman who refuses to choose kill someone too. It is indebted to Pagnol. It is not derivative of him.

**Fitzgerald, *The Bookshop*.** The same length, temper and refusal. Larrère's undated resignation is Mrs Gamart's Act of Parliament. Maïté boarding the bus with too few coins is Florence Green leaving Hardborough. Inguma is lived with as the rapper is. But Fitzgerald's narrator thinks. She will stop to tell you a kind heart is not much use for self-preservation. Here almost no sentence looks up from the job. In return this book has what Fitzgerald withholds, a marriage, and it is better than she is on manual work.

**Chevallier, *Clochemerle*.** The misreading chorus is Chevallier's, and it pays off: "A woman takes flowers to the wife because she's after the husband or because she's after the wife", closed twelve chapters later with "I only had the one name wrong." But Clochemerle is populous and carnal, and told by a man who enjoys his own digressions. This café has four voices and no bodies. Chevallier's comedy comes from appetite. This one comes from procedure, and there it is his equal: "Castagnet, Opened. It reads as if he was opened."

**Aymé, *La Vouivre*; Spark, *Memento Mori*.** Aymé allows one impossible fact, explains nothing, and lets officials cope. "Staff to wait. Staff not to join in" is pure Aymé. So is a demon that counts the roadsides on foot when the estimate was made at tractor speed. But Aymé never issues a rulebook, and this novel issues six rules, as agenda items. The bingo of the dead is Spark's brisk afterlife with corn on the cards, and "You can put that in the minutes as apologies received" would not shame her.

Smaller debts: the deadpan of labour ("It was a morning's work for two arms. She had one") is Magnus Mills, used for conscience instead of menace. The second shovel is a Guareschi ending. "Somebody has to stack the chairs" is the whole of Barbara Pym in six words.

## 3. What is its own

- Two wills in one body, played as slapstick and as consent. Coffee runs over a bar because two women are fighting for one jaw. A nurse settles who is present by taking a pulse: "That's her. In this hand."
- A demon kept off by giving it clerical work, and a woman who cannot do even that. Asked what it should count, she says "Either".
- Indecision as a physical fact that moves the plot. "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station."
- A memorial that lacks a verb. The council debates it three times. When the verb comes it is *Left*, the word on the heroine's own card.
- A marriage kept on a shopping pad, with boxes.
- The arithmetic of the end. Nobody is rescued for free: "fetching a man out of a dry hole."

## 4. Scores

- **Originality: 8.** "She said he had made it lose its count, and it came at 1 instead of 3."
- **The central idea and how far it is taken: 8.** It goes past the comfortable stop. "Nobody emptied hers."
- **The prose: 6.** Exact and clean, in one register, and it explains: "Maylis knew what Lesca wanted. Maylis had lain still for the village's sake. It was going to cost her Coralie."
- **The people: 7.** Coralie, Jeannot, Maïté and Madame Etcheto are 8s. Larrère is a 5. "I don't cook Either. There's no recipe for it."
- **The comedy: 7.** I smiled in most chapters. "Eleven years I've been timed like an egg."
- **What the ending does: 8.** "She heard the passage, then the front door, then the car door shutting on a pillow."
- **Memory in a year: 8.** The dead at bingo, a petition pushed through a car window, a hand locked on a stair post.

## 5. Five faults

1. **Too many machines, with a manual for each.** A university box, a Basque demon, lucid dreaming, a room where a lie will not come out, a right hand that stays with its owner, rain that follows a visitor. "There are three points, and I'll say them once." Then: "So that's a fourth point, and I didn't have it on my list." The truth rule is the worst, because it does the moral work the people should do. Coralie's chart already catches the same lie, better: "A tide comes in twice a day. It doesn't take Thursday off." Cut the truth rule. Show each other rule once, in action, and never recite it.

2. **No narrator, except once, and badly.** "Call her Lesca." A voice arrives to do the admin and is never heard again. This tradition lives on that voice: Chevallier's, Fitzgerald's, Spark's. Remove the intrusion, or give the narration five or six sentences of its own judgment across the book. "Maylis had crossed from one village to the other, and Josiane had still got there first" shows it can be done.

3. **The moral is stated, then stated again.** At the dip the narration says "She wanted it not to be her." Three chapters later she tells Kévin "I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me." The second is spoken, costs her something, and is enough. Cut the first, and let her turn her back on the lamp without the gloss.

4. **Larrère is a catchphrase.** "I'll be brief" opens almost every scene he has, and Coralie's oven door does him better than he does himself. His one unbroken speech, with the timer pinned, shows a man. Give the Larrère of Maylis's village one scene that is not spoken through glass, and let him want Maïté out loud.

5. **Tics run past their yield, and a thriller's clock in a village.** Jeannot's "One. Two.", "Here", the hand's width, the pulse readings; and in chapter 14 a timestamp every few lines: "02:59." "03:03." "03:06." The village novel keeps church time. Halve the countings, and keep two minutes only, 2:51 and 3:11.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

1. **Coralie, and that she leaves.** "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them."
2. **The paper.** The stone's verb, the timer, the care plan, letter 42, the form Hontaube will post on Monday. The same objects carry the grief: the list on its cord, the timer turned back to nothing.
3. **The unforgiving sum.** Forty-one, the empty chair, and a rescued man who will not be grateful: "I was in no danger, and I'll say so to anyone."

## 7. The verdict

Yes, low on the shelf. It can stand beside *The Bookshop*, which is its size and its temper. It cannot yet stand beside *Clochemerle* or Pagnol, who have a narrator and a villain. I would put it forward for a prize judged by readers of those books, expecting a longlist and a quarrel on the jury. Those for it will cite chapter 14 and the last page. Those against will cite the rulebook and the absent voice. What decides it in the book's favour is that the second woman, the one we were promised would do better, fails as well, and nobody on the page lets her off.
