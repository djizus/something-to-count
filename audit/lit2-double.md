# The literary read: the double

## 1. Where it belongs

A novel of the life not lived, told as village farce and ending as a moral reckoning: two versions of one woman, split by a choice at a floodgate, share a body for eighteen days. It stands nearest to Henry James's "The Jolly Corner", Saramago's *The Double* and Conrad's "The Secret Sharer", with Le Tellier's *The Anomaly* behind them. Its plot skeleton, I have to say, is also that of a thriller, Blake Crouch's *Dark Matter*.

## 2. Against those books

**James, "The Jolly Corner".** Brydon meets the self he would have been, powerful and ruined, with two fingers gone from one hand; Alice Staverton says she could have liked that one too. This novel has the same three: the mayor with the scarred right palm, the self who stayed small, and the woman who says "I'd have this one again." It does James's pity as well as he does. It does his dread worse, because nothing here is withheld. What James does not do: his other self is seen once and faints away. This one eats soup, chairs a meeting and is refused on a landing.

**Saramago, *The Double*.** The mayor's motion, "That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it", is António Claro's demand for a night with Maria da Paz. Saramago carries the substitution through and the women are deceived. Here the woman takes the wrist and knows: "That's her. In this hand." Then she refuses the better offer: "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." That is an advance on Saramago, and on every Amphitryon since Plautus, where Alcmene is always fooled. What it does worse is everything at sentence level. Saramago's narrator is a mind. This book has no narrator, only a recorder.

**Conrad, "The Secret Sharer".** Leggatt saved a ship and killed a man doing it; the captain hides him and risks everything to let him go. Lesca saved forty houses and drowned one man. Maylis harbours her, then puts her out, and loses anyway. Conrad's captain is rewarded for his loyalty to the double. This book refuses that: "You're the water."

**Dostoevsky, *The Double*.** The café scene, where the bolder self takes over the timid one's mouth in public ("She's got a mayor in her"), is Golyadkin junior at the office, and nearly as funny. But Dostoevsky never lets you settle whether the double is there. Here Coralie's rational account ("He asked for horses, though. You asked for me.") lasts one page, and chapter 3 hands the double her own point of view.

**Derivative?** Of *Dark Matter* in outline only: a steel box, a self who chose otherwise, who comes for the other's partner. Crouch's double is a thief and the hero wins his wife back. Here the double is the penitent, the partner judges both, and nobody wins. The debt is real and should be known; the book is not Crouch's. Against Atkinson's *Life After Life* it is the sterner book: Ursula gets to try again until it comes right, and here the second try kills someone else.

## 3. What is its own

- The double's guilt arriving as weather. She crosses, and her flood crosses with her.
- A sleep-paralysis demon as ferryman and bailiff, delayed by being given things to count, and late because the count was estimated "at the speed of the village tractor" while it "was doing the roadsides on foot".
- The afterlife as bingo in the village hall: "Twelve. One, two." Then: "Somebody pulled a chair in to a table."
- Lies that will not come out in the steel room ("It's the ti—"), sticking like the handle at nineteen turns.
- Two selves fighting with a hand each over a handbrake: "The one on the brake was the weaker, and it had got there first."
- The choice run twice, opposite ways, and wrong both times. The decisive one cannot know why she chose. The other says "Both", and is told: "That was Either, with the river in it."

## 4. Scores

- **Originality: 8.** "You'll count the fingers of my right hand. Then you'll take her home."
- **Central idea, and how far it is taken: 8.** "She wanted it not to be her." The idea reaches its end there. "You're the water" does not: it rests on two dry days and is never tested again.
- **Prose: 6.5.** At its best, "She watched her sentence travel back through the square and down the road, row by row, at walking pace." At its usual, "She had cracked a rib on Monday, and that was why she was sleeping on her back in the first place."
- **The people: 7.5.** Coralie is a 9: "I've held hands for a living since I was twenty, so I know when a hand is holding on, and I know when it wants to be let go." The village is an 8. The two Maylises are a 6.5.
- **Comedy: 8.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station."
- **What the ending does: 8.** "'What do you want, Maylis?' 'Madame Etcheto's being buried here on Tuesday. I want to dig the grave myself.'"
- **Memory in a year: 8.** "'Lesca, Maylis,' Lesca said. 'Surname first.'"

## 5. Five faults

1. **One voice for two selves.** The Maylis chapters and the Lesca chapters are written in the same short declarative sentence, and it explains itself: "Her left hand came across and closed over Coralie's fingers. Maylis hadn't told it to." Then, two lines on: "Maylis hadn't said it." The books on this shelf are voices. Let Lesca's chapters think in items, motions and minutes down to the syntax, keep Maylis in turns, trailers and metres, and cut every clause that accounts for what the reader has just seen.

2. **Too much machinery.** The tradition runs on one unexplained fact. This has a physics box and a demon, a counting rule, a fixed minute, a falling pulse, a hand apiece, leaking memories, words that stick and a rain chart. Chapters 6 to 9 are spent operating them: "She worked out that the count would take it one working day: eight hours from 6, when Lesca woke up." Drop one system entirely (the pulse countdown or the rain chart) and shorten the day-swap.

3. **The clocks set on the principals never strike.** Coralie describes a man dying of a rib through the lung; Maylis coughs and "It was pink." She then runs a kilometre, wades a flood and digs a grave. The cold body and the lost hand are likewise threatened and withdrawn. The price is paid by a woman of ninety-four. Either make Maylis's body pay or cut the lecture that promises it.

4. **Maylis is a word.** "Either" is said a dozen times and stands in for a temperament. Golyadkin hesitates in a hundred shapes. Keep four: the pad, the gendarme, the demon, the platform. Give her one want that is not Coralie's question.

5. **The ledger balances to the last line.** In three chapters the oven door, the pad, the key, the suitcase, the verb, the timer, the cards, the five fingers and letter 42 each take a curtain call. "'I flew,' he said" is one too many. Leave two of them open.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

1. Coralie's two fingers on the wrist: the lover who is not fooled, prefers the double, and refuses her.
2. The hall of the dead, and the number twelve called in it.
3. The platform at 5 in daylight, with the man above ground and the handle fitted: "'Either,' Maylis said. 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said." The catastrophe happens there, by default, and the end keeps faith with it: "I was in no danger, and I'll say so to anyone."

## 7. The verdict

Yes, it would stand on that shelf, at the lower end: beside Le Tellier and Atkinson, a step below James, Saramago and Conrad. It earns the place by design, by its people and by its comedy, not by its sentences. I would put it forward for a prize judged by readers who know those books. I would expect it to be argued over, remembered longer than most of the list, and beaten by a book with a voice.
