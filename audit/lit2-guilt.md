# The literary read: guilt, choice and confession

## 1. Where it belongs

A short moral fable about one unforgivable decision, made twice by the same woman in two versions of a village, told as village farce. It stands nearest to Camus's *The Fall*, Conrad's *Lord Jim* and Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day*, with Dürrenmatt's *The Pledge* close behind.

## 2. Against those books

**Camus, *The Fall*.** Clamence heard a woman go into the Seine and walked on. His book ends by asking for her to jump again so that he might save them both, then: too late, "Fortunately!" This novel grants the wish Camus withholds, and shows what it costs. That is its best idea.

- As well: the true sentence that lies. Lesca's "No one came to tell him" is as good a piece of bad faith as anything Clamence says.
- Worse: Clamence's confession is a new way of ruling, and Camus knows it. Lesca's works like medicine. She says "I opened the gate, and I drowned him", waits for the weight, and "nothing came down on her".
- Its own: the aftermath. "The loudspeaker on the bank road said 'drowned him' after the square had finished hearing it." Then: "Somebody has to stack the chairs." Camus has no village to hear him.

It is not derivative of *The Fall*. No sentence sounds like Camus.

**Conrad, *Lord Jim*.** Jim jumps, cannot say why ("I had jumped… It seems"), and takes a second chance in Patusan that kills someone else.

- As well: the blank at the centre of the act. "She tried to remember what she had been thinking when she made it, but all she could remember was turning the handle." Lesca's crossing is Patusan, and Madame Etcheto is its Dain Waris.
- Worse: Conrad has Marlow, a mind turning the act over for 300 pages. This novel has no voice that thinks. It has objects.
- Its own: it splits Jim in two. One woman acted and killed; the other refuses to act, "She wanted it not to be her", and someone dies anyway. Conrad never tests the omission.

**Ishiguro, *The Remains of the Day*.** Stevens cannot say what he wants, and Miss Kenton leaves.

- As well: Coralie's reason for the landing is on Ishiguro's level. "If I'd asked, I'd have had the answer in the house with us, at every meal."
- Worse: Stevens's evasions come from a whole life, his father and Lord Darlington. Maylis's "Either" comes from nowhere. It is a tic with a shopping pad attached.
- Its own: Coralie is no Miss Kenton. She asks outright, repeatedly, and she is funny. "I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it."

**Dürrenmatt, *The Pledge* and *The Visit*.** In *The Pledge* a perfect plan is beaten by an accident on a road. Here the van sinks in the dip, and the prophet gets Friday the 4th wrong by thirty centimetres. In *The Visit* a town votes its way to a death in the language of procedure, as this one does: "A vote was a vote."

- As well: procedure as comedy and as alibi.
- Worse: Dürrenmatt indicts the town. This novel lets it off lightly.
- Its own: its warmth. Dürrenmatt has no Coralie.

## 3. What is its own

- The second chance arrives as the catastrophe. "You're the water."
- Guilt as a domestic distance, measured: four steps across a landing, kept for eleven years by a woman who chose not to know.
- The undecidable motive, forty houses or one car in a yard, known to the partner and left unasked.
- A first stated want that is a penance. "I want to dig the grave myself."
- The dead at bingo, who do not look up. "He put a grain on his second card."
- Arithmetic refused by the man it was done to. "It's four when you're the one counting. When it's your house it's one, and they knock it down."
- A trolley problem conducted by registered letter, kitchen timer and car window.

## 5. Five faults, worst first

**1. The apparatus does the conscience's work.** The steel room is a lie detector: false sentences stick in the throat. "'I w—' Lesca's fist closed. 'I can't stand here and say I won't.'" And at the end: "It came out whole. They both heard it." Ishiguro makes the reader catch the lie. Here the room catches it for us, six times. I would keep it twice, for "the tide" and for "I won't", and elsewhere let her lie fluently and trust us to hear it.

**2. One death has too many authors, and the facts are left vague where only the motive should be.** Madame Etcheto dies of Lesca's river, Larrère's order, Jeannot's vote, the prefecture's orange, Coralie not waking her, and Maylis's walk. "'Then it wasn't Inguma that came for her,' Madame Etcheto said. 'It was you.'" Yet when Lesca leaves, "The water kept coming", and the proof of her guilt is two dry days. Conrad gives Jim one jump. Decide whether the crossing brings the flood, and say it once so the reader cannot argue. Then let "You had the key" stand alone against it. The real hinge is 5 p.m. on the platform, handle fitted, Jeannot above ground, "Either". Give that moment more room than the dip.

**3. The two-in-one-body wrestling is run too often.** Stair post, kitchen, bingo bar, handbrake. "They had one nose between them, so now neither of them could breathe." It is funny, but by the climax the moral contest has become arm-wrestling: "It was one hand against the other." Cut one of the middle bouts so the handbrake arrives fresh.

**4. Nothing is wasted, and the carpentry shows.** Every object returns on schedule: the timer, the pad, the oven door, the suitcase, the chrysanthemums, the number. "'Twelve. That's my door,' Madame Etcheto said" is planted three times before the dead call it. A reader of this shelf starts counting the pay-offs. Let two things fail to come back, and give Maylis one scene that shows where "Either" came from.

**5. The one man who knew is dismissed with a punchline.** Larrère twice sat in Castagnet's kitchen knowing where Castagnet was. He is also the only person who ever asks the question: "When you stood on that platform, did you—". He exits on "'I'll be brief,' he said", and silence. Dürrenmatt would have made him speak. Let him finish that sentence once, untimed, and let it be a fair question.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

1. The refusal to say which woman chose rightly. Both pay, and the village's verdict on each is unfair in a different way.
2. Coralie in both houses: her questions, her charts, and her not asking.
3. The flat reporting of the worst moments. The boot sole under the water, the mud at the corner of the mouth, "Forty-one."

## 7. The verdict

It would stand on that shelf, at the Dürrenmatt end, beside *The Pledge*, not beside *Lord Jim*. It has a real idea in this tradition, takes it to an honest conclusion, and is funnier than anything on the shelf. It stands lower than Camus, Conrad and Ishiguro because they put a mind on the page and this book puts hands, props and a demon there.

I would put it forward for a prize judged by readers who know those books. I would expect a longlist, and a shortlist if the lie-detecting and the wrestling were thinned and the fact of who brought the water were settled.
