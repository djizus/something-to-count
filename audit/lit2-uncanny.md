# The literary read: the uncanny and the folk tale

## 1. Where it belongs

A village fable in which a Basque night-hag carries a roadmender between two versions of her village, with a municipal farce played over a drowning. It stands nearest to Marcel Aymé (the marvel administered by clerks), to Maupassant's "The Horla" (the weight on the chest, the stolen will) and to *Piranesi* (two selves in one body, a keeper of the dead, a hall). Shirley Jackson's village is a fourth, more distant neighbour.

## 2. Against those books

**Aymé, "The Man Who Walked Through Walls" and *La Vouivre*.** Dutilleul finds he can pass through walls and goes to a doctor, who prescribes powders. This novel has the same reflex and has it as well as Aymé: 'It's in the plan now. Staff to wait. Staff not to join in.' Summoned to her seat among the dead, the mayor answers, 'You can put that in the minutes as apologies received.' What it does worse: Aymé's narrator is a wit, and the joke is in his sentences. Here the narration is neutral and all the wit sits in the dialogue. What it does that Aymé does not: it carries a grief the whole length of a novel and lets the comedy stop for it. It is in debt to Aymé's method. It is not derivative of any one story.

**Maupassant, "The Horla".** The Horla's narrator wakes with something kneeling on his chest, then watches his own will being used by another. This novel is exact about the body in the same way: 'At 2:54 the little finger of her right hand moved against the blanket. The rest of the hand followed.' It does worse on the one thing "The Horla" is made of, which is doubt. Maupassant's man never knows whether he is haunted or mad. Here the question is closed by chapter 4, at a meeting with an agenda. What it does that Maupassant does not: the possessor gets her own chapters and her own claim, and the hag is given work. 'I've been fighting it since the rib, and half the time it was doing my job.'

**Clarke, *Piranesi*.** Both books have a gentle keeper of the dead who shares a skull with a harder self, both have tides, and both end with neither self the winner. Clarke's House is inexhaustible and her narrator's awe is the book. This novel's other place is a steel shed used as a committee room, and its one great hall is seen for a page and a half. It has what *Piranesi* lacks: a community, a lover who keeps a chart, and jokes.

**Jackson, "The Lottery".** The village as a chorus that keeps score is here, and well done: the café pad with its cross, and later the dealer's 'Had the key and went for a walk, did you?' Jackson's village is malign under its small talk. This one only gossips, so the book has no menace from that side.

## 3. What is its own

- The creature: 'overlapping brown leaves, and where the leaves parted she could see grey steel underneath, with a row of rivets.' The tale's demon and the laboratory's box are one object, and it always turns its back.
- The old charm made into an economy. Each person pays Inguma in what she has a great many of: leaves, roadsides, gravel loads 'in a book', corn. The sum is done 'at the speed of the village tractor', and the thing does it on foot.
- A spell that fails on a flaw of character. She cannot choose what to give it to count, says 'Either', and it sits on her.
- A room where an untrue sentence sticks in the mouth 'the way the handle stuck at nineteen turns'.
- The dead at bingo, called surname first, with a duck on the edge of the stage.
- One body shared limb by limb, with the self holding out in the right hand.

I have not read these elsewhere.

## 4. Scores

- **Originality: 7.** 'It hit the floor like a full sack dropped off a trailer.' The crossing-to-undo-a-disaster premise is borrowed from another shelf; the hag is new.
- **The central idea and how far it is taken: 8.** 'She wanted it not to be her.' The woman who chose and the woman who cannot are both left unacquitted, with one death each.
- **The prose: 6.** 'Lesca had the rest: the legs, the left hand and the mouth.' Exact, clear, and at one pace throughout.
- **The people: 7.** Coralie: 'I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it.' She is an 8. The heroine is thinner than her lover.
- **The comedy: 7.** 'Castagnet, Opened. It reads as if he was opened.' I smiled often, less often the third time a routine came round.
- **What the ending does: 7.** 'She watched her sentence travel back through the square and down the road, row by row, at walking pace.' The confession is an 8. The last chapter is a 6.
- **Whether I will remember it in a year: 7.** ''Twelve. One, two.' Somebody pulled a chair in to a table.' I will remember that, the leaves over the rivets and the car window. I will not remember a paragraph.

## 5. Five faults, worst first

1. **The narration has no voice.** 'She had built that patch herself in April, one wheelbarrow at a time. At 2:55 she gave it ten more minutes.' Every book on this shelf is a way of speaking before it is a story. This one counts: turns, centimetres, pulses, minutes. I would let the narrator see things that are not measurements and are not used later, and let a sentence run long when the river does.

2. **Nothing is left to be afraid of.** '*What. Since when. How do you know.*' The book audits its own ghost in three columns. Coralie's explanation by sleep paralysis lasts one page. I would keep that reading alive until the bingo hall, cut the chart, and let 'Lesca was gone. The water kept coming' stand without anyone confirming afterwards whose river it was.

3. **Two machines for one marvel.** 'What we want to know is whether there is another you, somewhere, who sees the other one.' A tale allows one impossible thing. The university's experiment invites questions the book then declines: 'I don't know why it comes.' I would cut the explanation at the door and keep only the barn, the chair, two lamps and a card. Madame Etcheto's mother is theory enough.

4. **Every object pays, and every person has a tag.** ''One,' Jeannot said. 'Those are my boots.'' 'I'll be brief.' 'Here.' The last chapter cashes the flight, the suitcase, the chair, the oven door, the cards and letter forty-two in turn, and the two nights match to the minute: 2:51 and 3:11 in both villages. A reader feels the ledger. I would break two symmetries and leave three things in the village that never come back.

5. **The limb traffic.** 'She got the little finger up, then the one next to it.' Chapters 5, 8, 9 and 13 are largely an account of who holds the jaw. It is the book's own device and it is overworked. I would cut a third of it and keep the stair post, the handbrake and the two hands going up to vote.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

1. Inguma exactly as it is: silent, back turned, the sound of a sack, obedient to the count. No face and no speech.
2. 'Either' as joke, flaw and failed spell, and the refusal that follows from it to say which woman was right. Coralie's 'Don't give me the measurement' belongs with this.
3. The hall of the dead and Madame Etcheto: 'Twelve. That's my door.'

## 7. The verdict

Not yet on that shelf. It has three or four inventions that belong there, and a moral design that does not flinch, written in sentences that belong one shelf down. Its uncanny is a mechanism the reader fully understands, where Maupassant, Jackson and Clarke leave something that cannot be understood.

I would put it forward for a longlist, on chapters 2, 9 and 13 to 15, and I would expect it to stop there. Judges who have read those books will admire the hag and the bingo, and then ask where the voice is.
