# The literary read: the comic village

## 1. Where it belongs

A municipal farce with a drowned man under it. A village on the Adour is run through a car window, a kitchen timer and a shopping pad, and a woman who never chooses meets the self who chose once and killed a neighbour. It stands nearest to Pagnol's water books, Guareschi's *Don Camillo* and Fitzgerald's *The Bookshop*, with Chevallier behind the council scenes and Magnus Mills behind the voice.

## 2. Against those books

**Pagnol, *Jean de Florette* and *Manon of the Springs*.** The same crime: a village's silence about water. "No one came to tell him" is Pagnol's sin in six words, true and a lie, and the public unsaying of it in the square equals Manon's scene at the fountain: "The loudspeaker on the bank road said 'drowned him' after the square had finished hearing it." Worse: Pagnol has hills, seasons, thirst. This village has rain and a date. What Pagnol does not do: let the reader watch the same night go the other way and cost a different life.

**Guareschi, *Don Camillo*.** Guareschi also has a flood on a river bank, a mayor, and a supernatural party spoken to as a colleague. Inguma sent off to count roadsides "on foot" is as matter-of-fact as the Christ over the altar, and Maylis and Lesca are one conscience in two officials, like the priest and Peppone. Worse: Guareschi's episodes are free; this plot is a clock. Better: Guareschi always forgives. Here Jeannot says, "It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you. I was in no danger."

**Fitzgerald, *The Bookshop*.** The same temperature of defeat by paperwork: a resignation signed with a gap for the date, and Hontaube's "Then it should have gone last Monday." Fitzgerald's poltergeist is also taken as plumbing. Worse, and it matters: Fitzgerald puts a narrating intelligence in every paragraph. This book has none; it only reports behaviour. Its own: Fitzgerald's heroine is done to. Maylis does it.

**Chevallier, *Clochemerle*.** The stone that needs a verb is the urinal debate in miniature and as good: "Castagnet, Opened. It reads as if he was opened." But Chevallier gives thirty people bodies, trades and appetites. Here most villagers are one habit each.

**Mills, *The Restraint of Beasts*.** The job-sheet deadpan is Mills's: "She read it the way she read a job sheet, for what the job would need." Mills never explains a joke. This book sometimes does.

It is derivative of none of them. The half-a-body slapstick has a film ancestor in *All of Me*; the dilemma is the trolley problem moved to a flood bank. Neither is hidden, neither is the point.

## 3. What is its own

- The double as a municipal office: "That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it." A motion, amended in the same ink.
- A folk demon on piecework, miscalculated "at the speed of the village tractor".
- The dead at bingo in the village hall, one man with "his arm around his card so that his neighbour couldn't copy it".
- "Either" as a character flaw that becomes a shopping-pad joke, then a catastrophe on a platform, then a verdict: "That was Either, with the river in it."
- A monument with two names and no verb.
- A rescue of a man who was dry, at the price of a woman who was not.

## 4. Scores

- **Originality: 7.** "A meeting needs somebody in the chair. Since neither of us is in it, I'll chair from here."
- **The central idea and how far it is taken: 8.** It does not flinch from its own arithmetic: "She wanted it not to be her."
- **The prose: 7.** Exact with objects, almost never wrong, never once a voice. At its best: "She watched her sentence travel back through the square and down the road, row by row, at walking pace."
- **The people: 7.** Coralie and Jeannot are 8; the chorus is 6. "I don't cook Either. There's no recipe for it."
- **The comedy: 7.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station."
- **What the ending does: 8.** It refuses thanks, keeps the death, and still ends on two shovels: "Then he stepped over the string in his rubber boots and began to dig."
- **Whether I will remember it in a year: 7.** The window gap, the stone, and "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much."

## 5. Five faults

1. **The mechanism takes the page from the village.** A steel room with two lamps, a demon, counting tasks, finger-counting, a room where false sentences stick, and a ledger of limbs: "Lesca had the rest: the legs, the left hand and the mouth." Aymé and Guareschi allow one impossible thing and then look at people. Chapters 5 to 9 wrestle the same body four times (stairs, bar, landing, kitchen table), before the van. I would keep the bar, where it is funny in public, cut one of the others, and state each rule once.

2. **Nobody is telling it.** The tradition is a tradition of voice: Chevallier's wine-soaked asides, Spark's cold eye, Fitzgerald's one sentence of judgement per page. Here the weather report is "On Sunday 6 December it was raining," and the village has no light, no food beyond soup and duck, no faces. The single physical portrait is Jeannot's neck, "red from the weather and it needed a barber", and it is the best thing in its chapter. I would give the narrator twenty such sentences across the book, on the square, the Port and the fields.

3. **The people say the theme.** "So for twelve years I've had two answers. Either you opened it for forty houses, and I live with the mayor. Or you opened it because I was in that building." Coralie has already said everything with "2:51. I've known that for twelve years." Pym would stop there. Cut her speech to a third and let the phone do it.

4. **The narrator nudges the joke.** "Maylis had never smiled at Jeannot Castagnet in her life, and the whole café was watching her do it." "Nobody in Peyrebarthe had ever heard Maylis's mouth forbid anything." The café's faces have already told us. Delete these wherever a villager reacts in the next line.

5. **Everything is paid off.** Pad, pillow, key, timer, suitcase, verb, cards, letter forty-two, the oven door, the fingers: each returns for its last turn. Most returns are good; together they read as built. The weakest is the neatest: "Each of them put her card in. Lesca closed it and laid it back under the panel." And Maïté's verb, "Left", rhymes with the card marked *left* in a way only English allows a French village. Leave two things unreturned. I would lose the envelope ceremony and end the steel room on "Neither of them sat down."

## 6. Three things it must not lose

1. **The road "Either" travels**, from "Eggs are what Either gets in this house" to Jeannot's "Then it's shut. That's how the vote left it." A running gag that becomes the disaster is the hardest thing in comic writing and it is done here.
2. **The refusal to console.** The cellar was dry, the letter does not thank her, Coralie takes the pillow, and the count stops at "Forty-one." Any softening here turns it into Guareschi on a weak day.
3. **The comedy of municipal objects**: the window that "went all the way down, into the door", the timer, the clipboard boy asking "whether the prefect might have the medal back, until things are clearer", and Marie-Jo's "Somebody has to stack the chairs."

## 7. The verdict

It would stand on that shelf at the near end, beside Mills and the *Don Camillo* books, not beside Fitzgerald, Spark or Pagnol. The bingo night, the flood night, the square and the grave belong there without apology. Chapters 5 to 9 do not yet.

I would put it forward. I would expect readers who know those books to shortlist it for its ending and its moral nerve, and to hold it back from winning for the reasons in faults 1 and 2: too many rules, and no one's voice. Of the two, the rules are quicker to fix and the voice is what a jury would remember.
