# The literary read: the double

## 1. Where it belongs

A comic village novel of the double, in which the other self is neither rival nor devil but the one who has already chosen: a mayor with a drowned man on her conscience crosses into the body of the roadmender she would have been, twelve years before the same flood. It stands nearest to Saramago's *The Double* (the claim on the other's bed), Le Tellier's *The Anomaly* (two copies offset in time, one knowing what the other will learn) and Henry James's "The Jolly Corner" (the unlived self, known by a damaged hand, and the woman who would take either). Stevenson supplies the shared body; Atkinson is the book it argues with.

## 2. Against those books

**Saramago, *The Double*.** The same move: the double demands the other's bed. "*That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*" Saramago's women are deceived until a ring mark on a finger gives the man away, and that is the weak place in his book. Here the woman reads the hand first. Coralie takes the wrist of a body with two tenants: "In this hand. I've had sixteen years of her saying nothing, and I know it when I've got hold of it." Then she refuses the better lover: "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." That is Alcmena undeceived, which neither Saramago nor Kleist's *Amphitryon* gives us. As good: the comedy of the small official. Worse: Saramago's sentence thinks while it runs, and he explains nothing; this prose reports, and explains twice. Not derivative.

**Le Tellier, *The Anomaly*.** The engine is his: the later copy carries the earlier copy's future. He spreads it over eleven passengers and it thins into pastiche. This novel stakes everything on one pair and goes further into what the foreknowledge is worth, which is nothing: the forewarned night goes wrong differently. Its negotiation equals his best: "'Then it's carried, or it's a tie, and in a tie the chair decides.' 'There's nobody in the chair.'" Worse: Le Tellier commits to his hypothesis and plays with it. Here the laboratory is a prop, "A lot of steel for one chair", standing beside a folk demon. The premise is in his debt. The book is not derivative of his.

**Stevenson, *Jekyll and Hyde*.** Jekyll wakes and knows who he is by the hand on the bedclothes. This novel makes its whole physical drama from that, as well as Stevenson and funnier: one tenant has the left hand and the mouth, the other the right, and they fight over a handbrake. "It was one hand against the other. The one on the brake was the weaker, and it had got there first." It also ends in a full statement of the case, but made aloud to a square, and nobody dies of it: "Somebody has to stack the chairs." What Stevenson has and this lacks is dread. After chapter 1 Inguma is never frightening. It becomes staff.

**Atkinson, *Life After Life*.** Atkinson lets Ursula practise until she gets it right. Here the night is run twice with opposite choices and neither is right: forty houses dry and a man drowned, or the man fetched "out of a dry hole" and an old woman drowned in her bed. "If she turned that handle, Jeannot drowned and her hand had done it. If she went on, whatever the river did behind her was the river's doing. She wanted it not to be her." Atkinson has no sentence as hard as the last. She has a world wider than one street, and prose with more than one speed.

Against Dostoevsky: his bolder Golyadkin takes the office and the room's goodwill, and the terror is that nobody finds it odd. Here everybody finds it odd, and decides it is adultery.

## 3. What is its own

- The double as weather. The guilty self brings her flood with her: "You're the water." And the hall's answer: "She wants water."
- A possession misread by a village chorus as an affair: "A woman takes flowers to the wife because she's after the husband or because she's after the wife."
- One woman split into first name and surname, and mended in three words: "Good night, Maylis."
- A room where a false sentence sticks in the mouth: "It gives me somebody to blame, and it won't come out."
- The dead at bingo with grains of corn, and a death told as a call: "'Twelve. One, two.' Somebody pulled a chair in to a table."
- Death declined in committee language: "You can put that in the minutes as apologies received."
- The saved man who will not be grateful: "I was in no danger, and I'll say so to anyone."

## 4. Scores

- **Originality: 8.** "'She wants water,' one of the Lassalle sisters said."
- **The central idea and how far it is taken: 8.** All the way to the bill: "I know what you picked. I was the other one."
- **The prose: 7.** Exact, and never more than exact: "It had left a line on the wallpaper a metre above the pillow."
- **The people: 7.** Coralie and Jeannot are better than that; the two halves of the heroine are nearer a diagram. "It's a hole in a field. I know what it is. But I light the stove and lock the trapdoor, and nobody can get at me till it's light."
- **The comedy: 8.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station."
- **What the ending does: 8.** It rewards nobody and still ends on a second shovel. "It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you."
- **Remembered in a year: 8.** "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much."

## 5. Five faults

1. **The catastrophe turns on the catchword.** At 5 in daylight, the handle fitted and the man above ground: "'Either,' Maylis said. 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said." The word has appeared some twenty times by then, so the hinge reads as motif, and the reader is shut out of her head at the one moment that needs it. Halve its uses before chapter 12, and give the platform four lines of what she is afraid of.
2. **Two machines, and the rules are handed over.** A laboratory and a demon do one job, and an old woman states the plot: "Some nights it takes the sleeper away with it and leaves her body in the bed. Before morning it takes her home. The one it cannot take home dies in that bed." Keep the room, because the last page needs it. Let Madame Etcheto stop a sentence early, and let the fatal rule be found out.
3. **Nothing is left over.** Every object pays: the kitchen clock, the suitcase, the reflector, the number twelve. Coralie plants a case history, "He said his mouth tasted of coins", and a page on "Her mouth tasted of coins." The planner's hand shows. Take out three plants and let those things arrive unannounced.
4. **One register, and a tic.** The bandage and the hall of the dead are written at the same pitch, and time is always priced in chores: "Twenty-five minutes was a trailer of leaves to the dump and back." By "She took more than that to the dump in a morning" it is a mannerism. Keep four of these. Allow the prose one long sentence when the bank goes.
5. **Chapter 16 settles three accounts in four pages,** and the middle one is an unmasking: "You stood in my kitchen that night and rang her from my phone, with his van gone down to the fields". A comic turn is asked to carry melodrama, and Lesca's guilt gets a partner just when she has claimed it alone. Give the chapter room, or cut the gate scene to Larrère's silence.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

1. Coralie's two fingers on the wrist, and her refusal on the landing.
2. The refusal to vindicate either choice, down to Jeannot's letter.
3. The procedural voice at the edge of death, and the chorus that gets it wrong: the apologies, the bingo call, "She wants water."

## 7. The verdict

Yes, on the lower end of that shelf, beside Saramago's and Le Tellier's books and below Stevenson's and Dostoevsky's. Chapters 8, 13 and 14 would hold anywhere on it. I would put it forward for a prize, and expect to defend it to readers of those books against two charges: the machinery, and the tidiness. What it has that they will not have read is a second chance that redeems nothing, told as farce.
