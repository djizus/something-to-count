# The literary read: the speculative novel a prize jury reads

## 1. Where it belongs

A village novel with one speculative premise: an experiment in 2014 split a woman in two, and twelve years later the one who opened a floodgate and drowned a man crosses into the one who never chose anything. It stands nearest to Ted Chiang's "Anxiety Is the Dizziness of Freedom", Le Tellier's *The Anomaly* and Atkinson's *Life After Life*, and it is written in the withheld, plain register of Ishiguro's *Never Let Me Go* and *Klara and the Sun*.

## 2. Against those books

**Chiang, "Anxiety Is the Dizziness of Freedom".** This is the debt a jury will name first. Chiang's prism lights a red or a blue LED and the world forks; the branches talk; within weeks their weather differs. Here: "One of these two lamps will come on... whether there is another you, somewhere, who sees the other one", and "We had a dry winter here in 2014." The premise is derivative of Chiang. What it does with the premise is not. Chiang argues his idea to a conclusion (character is what holds across branches). This novel argues nothing and stages one night twice. It is weaker as thought and much stronger as people: Chiang has no Coralie. And Chiang's branches cannot hurt each other; this one's can: "You're the water."

**Le Tellier, *The Anomaly*.** The same situation: two of one person, one partner, and the question of who gets the bed. Le Tellier handles it in cool ensemble pastiche. This novel puts it on a staircase, with a cut hand locked on the post, and lets the partner decide: "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." That is better than anything in Le Tellier's couples. What it does worse: Le Tellier lets the world react; here the wider world never learns anything, which keeps the book small.

**Atkinson, *Life After Life*.** Atkinson replays a life until it comes out right, and each death is wiped. Here both runs stand and both are paid for. The second woman chooses the other way, saves a man who "was in no danger", and an old woman drowns in room twelve. The line that does it: "If she went on, whatever the river did behind her was the river's doing. She wanted it not to be her." Atkinson never lets Ursula choose for so poor a reason. What Atkinson has and this lacks is range of sentence and the long view of a life.

**Ishiguro, *Never Let Me Go* and *Klara and the Sun*.** The register is his: a mind that measures everything by its work ("Twenty-five minutes was a trailer of leaves to the dump and back") and a couple who never ask. "'You never asked.' 'No.' 'Why not?' 'Because you'd have told me.'" That exchange would sit in Ishiguro. The difference is costly. Klara's bargain with the Sun is never confirmed. This book rules a chart in three columns, "*What. Since when. How do you know.*", and proves its magic. Ishiguro also has one rule. This has eight.

## 3. What is its own

- The speculative machine is a folk one. A Basque night-thing is kept busy by giving it something to count: stars, leaves, "the stones the gravel company took out", and at the end "the fingers of my right hand". I have not read that used as a mechanism.
- Council procedure as a moral language: "*That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*"
- The dead play bingo with grains of corn, and the death is announced as a call: "Twelve. One, two." Somebody pulls a chair in.
- "Either". A heroine whose flaw is one word, and a catastrophe that turns on her saying it at 5 in daylight, with the handle fitted.
- The possession shared by limb, written with the definite article.
- A stone with two names and no verb.

## 4. Scores

- **Originality: 7.** The frame is Chiang's. The furniture is its own: "You'll count the corn in this hall. On the cards, and in the dishes, and what's left in the sack."
- **The central idea and how far it is taken: 7.** The choice is run twice and neither run is acquitted. But the idea is never thought about: "I don't know why it comes. That I can say."
- **The prose: 7.** At its best exact: "She watched her sentence travel back through the square and down the road, row by row, at walking pace." It has one gear.
- **The people: 8.** "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much."
- **The comedy: 8.** "Castagnet, Opened. It reads as if he was opened." And: "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station."
- **What the ending does: 8.** Asked once more what she wants, she does not say Coralie: "I want to dig the grave myself."
- **Whether I will remember it in a year: 8.** "the car door shutting on a pillow."

## 5. Five faults, worst first

1. **It proves its magic and has too many rules.** Counting delays the thing; staying seated carries you across; lies stick in the steel room; weather crosses with the traveller; the right hand stays with its owner; memories leak; the unreturned sit with the dead. Then it audits them: "A tide comes in twice a day. It doesn't take Thursday off." A jury that has read Klara will mark this down. Keep "You're the water" as something Maylis believes and the hall mishears. Cut the chart to Bordenave's one cross. Lose the rule about sticking words except for the last "I won't".

2. **Every planted object pays out.** Timer, sash in a plastic bag, forty-two, oven door, five fingers, key in the tray, surname first. The worst case: "'I had a verb,' Maïté said... '*Left*.'" The blank on the stone was the best thing on it. Withhold the verb. Leave the suitcase without the bus. Three unpaid debts would make the paid ones feel like fate and not like bookkeeping.

3. **One sentence for everybody.** A mayor of eleven years, a gravedigger and a care assistant all think in the same short declarative: "So she could cross." "She believed the date." "The pad said 2. She would wait until 2." Lesca's village is the thinner for it; her lake, the four houses and the ponies arrive only in chapter 11. Give Lesca's chapters her own syntax, the minuted, subordinate sentence of someone who has made the same speech twelve times, and bring her lake forward.

4. **The limb-by-limb struggle outstays its welcome.** "Lesca pulled hard at the jaw, and it went from side to side." The stair post and the handbrake earn it. The bingo jaw does not, at that length. Halve it.

5. **Sentences that tell the reader what was just shown.** "Nobody in Peyrebarthe had ever heard Maylis's mouth forbid anything." "Then it wasn't Inguma that came for her... It was you." Cut both, and their cousins.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

1. The platform at 5, in daylight, with the man standing behind her: "'Either,' Maylis said. 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said." The whole book is in that.
2. Coralie: the two answers she refuses to hear ("Don't tell me now"), and that she leaves.
3. No verdict. The saved man says he was dry and digs the grave anyway; the confession in the square is met with "Somebody has to stack the chairs."

## 7. The verdict

Would it stand on that shelf? Yes, at the near end: beside *The Anomaly* and *Sea of Tranquility*, which it out-peoples, and a clear step below *Never Let Me Go* and Chiang, who think harder and explain less. It is an 8 in its people, its comedy and its ending, and a 7 in the three things a jury weighs first: originality, idea and sentence.

Would I put it forward? Yes, for a longlist, and I would argue for it in the room. I would expect to lose the argument at the shortlist to the first judge who has read *Exhalation*, unless the machinery is thinned and the ledger left partly unpaid.
