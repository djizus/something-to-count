# The literary read: the comic village

## 1. Where it belongs

A municipal farce with a death in it. A folk demon and a second self are handled as matters of village procedure, and the procedure is what kills. It stands nearest Pagnol's *Manon of the Springs* for its moral shape, Aymé's *La Vouivre* for its method and Fitzgerald's *The Bookshop* for its temperature, with Chevallier's *Clochemerle* as its comic ancestor.

## 2. Against those books

**Pagnol, *Jean de Florette* and *Manon of the Springs*.** The skeleton is the same: a village's water, a man dead of what nobody said, a public reckoning, and a late fact that moves the guilt. "No one came to tell him" is this book's blocked spring. The confession from the stage is as good as the scene where Pagnol's village breaks its silence: "The loudspeaker on the bank road said 'drowned him' after the square had finished hearing it." It does two things worse. Pagnol gives the Papet a tragedy, and Larrère gets his in one line of another woman's speech: "You stood in my kitchen that night and rang her from my phone." Pagnol's hills are a character, and Peyrebarthe has almost no land. What Pagnol does not do is run the dilemma twice and make the clean-handed answer the worse one: "She wanted it not to be her." This is a debt of shape, not of page.

**Aymé, *La Vouivre* (and "Les Sabines" for the multiplied woman).** This is Aymé's method exactly: one impossible fact, then strict realism about its paperwork. His mayor frets over what a serpent-woman does to his politics, and his village has its gravedigger too. Here the demon enters a care plan: "Staff to wait. Staff not to join in." When the café learns the roadmender has a mayor in her, its one correction is "It takes a small m." Where it falls short: Aymé states his marvel in a sentence and never legislates again, and this book keeps adding rules. What it adds: Aymé's marvels stay marvels, and this one is given a moral job, as a conscience that floods whatever it visits.

**Fitzgerald, *The Bookshop*.** It has the same refusal to reward courage, the same haunting treated as a fault in the building, and the same village closing over. The café's old joke, "Still in the box, were you?", is replaced by a worse one: "Had the key and went for a walk, did you?" That is Hardborough. It is worse in the prose: Fitzgerald's sentences think, and these mostly measure. It differs at the end. Fitzgerald leaves Florence nothing. This book leaves a second shovel, and gets away with it because the letter that comes with it "doesn't thank you".

**Chevallier, *Clochemerle*.** The committee on the stone's missing verb ("Castagnet, Opened. It reads as if he was opened") and an association chaired from a car with its engine running are Clochemerle-grade. Josiane's theory of adultery is wrong all book and then right: "I only had the one name wrong." It is worse in voice: Chevallier has a narrator with opinions and room to digress, this book has neither, and its village is smaller for it. It is better in consequence. In *Clochemerle* the bystander's death is an absurdity. Here a 4–3 vote and the word "Either" drown someone, and the reader feels the causation.

It is derivative of none of them. It also has the deadpan of manual work in Mills's *The Restraint of Beasts* ("Twenty-five minutes was a trailer of leaves to the dump and back") and the unsurprised old of Spark's *Memento Mori*.

## 3. What is its own

- A demon managed by delegation, sent to count a gravel company's output because "each load is weighed, so the loads are in a book."
- The double as a rival office-holder, chairing a meeting in a room with one chair, who turns out to be the disaster she came to prevent: "You're the water."
- The dilemma's second run, where the one man sits in "the driest hole in the village" and rescuing him is a way of not choosing.
- The dead at bingo, with a duck in white paper on the stage.
- A motive left unsettled: "That sounded no more true, and no less."
- A boy who counts five fingers in a flooded yard and says, "I wanted six."

## 4. Scores

- **Originality: 8.** "You'll count the stars. The ones behind the cloud as well."
- **The central idea and how far it is taken: 8.** It is taken to its cost: "I know what you picked. I was the other one."
- **The prose: 7.** It is exact and never musical. Its best effects are plain fact: "A child asked what the lady had done. Somebody told the child."
- **The people: 7.** Coralie is a 9: "I've held hands for a living since I was twenty, so I know when a hand is holding on." Most of the village has one tic each.
- **The comedy: 8.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station."
- **What the ending does: 8.** "It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you."
- **Memory in a year: 8.** "Eleven years I've been timed like an egg."

## 5. Five faults, worst first

1. **The possession turns into logistics.** In chapters 5 to 9 the body is a committee and the prose a list of who holds which part: "Lesca pulled at the jaw and Maylis held it, so all Josiane got was a noise through the nose." I count six rules: it takes you home, a count delays it, you can stay sitting, the body left behind goes cold, untrue words stick in the steel room, the double brings the rain. Aymé needs one. Keep the stair post and the handbrake, where the tug means something. Cut the jaw-wrestling at the bar to a few lines. State each rule once.

2. **It explains its own motifs and pays every one off.** "It had always come when she needed it. It had never been true, and it wasn't true now." Coralie has already said this better, with eggs. Pad, pillow, key, timer, suitcase, reflector, card: each returns on schedule, and the reader starts to hear the gears. Cut the glosses and leave two or three objects unpaid. Pym would let some of them be furniture.

3. **A village with procedures and no place.** "On Sunday 6 December it was raining." Between the Adour and the Basque hills there is hardly a sentence of land, light or food beyond soup and duck. One line shows what Maylis's eye could do: the river "had gone the colour of the sky, and it was very wide." A dozen more, seen as a worker sees, would cost 200 words.

4. **Larrère is a gag asked to carry the guilt.** His folder is plot plumbing ("That's prison, for eels"), and the fact that convicts him arrives second-hand. His one human line is superb: "Nobody else in this village has ever opened one before I knocked." Weight his reasons more and his folder less, and put the reader in a room with him once before chapter 16.

5. **Two origins for one marvel.** A university's steel box and a Basque demon both account for the crossing: "What we want to know is whether there is another you, somewhere, who sees the other one." The last page fuses them, steel knocking on steel "like one part of the room touching another", but lightly and late. Show the rivets again mid-book.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

1. **Coralie.** Two fingers on a wrist, a shopping pad with boxes, a want of her own, and the fact that she goes. "I know that word. I've cooked it."
2. **Procedure as both the joke and the cause.** The window that opens a few centimetres, the timer, the stone without a verb, the form Hontaube will post on Monday. Do not soften the line from these to room twelve.
3. **The refusals.** The motive is never settled, the rescued man "was in no danger, and I'll say so to anyone", and the count stops at "Forty-one." Any consolation beyond the second shovel would sink it.

## 7. The verdict

Yes, it would stand on that shelf, nearer Mills and Guareschi than Fitzgerald and Spark. It has the construction and the humour of the tradition, but not yet a voice at the level of the sentence, or a landscape. What earns its place is rare: a comic village in which the comedy does the damage.

I would put it forward. I would expect a jury to split between those who admire the build and those who can hear it, and I would argue for it on chapter 14 and on Coralie. Its case would be stronger if the middle chapters lost a third of their machinery.
