# The literary read: the double

## 1. Where it belongs

A short novel of the double as the life not lived. One woman was split in 2014 by a lamp in a steel room. One self opened a floodgate and drowned a man; the other never had to choose; twelve years later the first climbs into the second's body. It stands nearest to Saramago's *The Double* (the double who comes for the other's bed), Le Tellier's *The Anomaly* (two copies, one partner, one copy knowing what is coming) and Atkinson's *Life After Life* (the night done again). Its shared body is Stevenson's.

## 2. Against those books

**Saramago, *The Double*.** The same situation: the double decides to take the other's woman. Lesca writes it as a motion: "*That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*" Saramago's Maria da Paz finds out by the mark of a ring, too late, and dies of it. This novel does that part better. Coralie tells the two apart by a pulse, every time, and refuses for a reason of her own: "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." What it does worse is voice. Saramago's narrator is a presence in every line; this narrator has one level tone. What Saramago does not do: the woman here would rather have the double ("I'd have this one again"), which hurts more than being deceived.

**Le Tellier, *The Anomaly*.** His two Joannas, in love with one man, are this book's landing. He is cleverer, wider and sharper about the State: he has Protocol 42, this book has a young man from Pau with a roll of tape. But he never stays with one pair for a whole book, and his copies are three months apart. These two are one decision apart, and each is the other's unpaid bill.

**Atkinson, *Life After Life*.** Ursula pushes Bridget down the stairs to keep the influenza out, and the reader approves: practice makes perfect. This novel refuses that. The forewarned woman does not do better. She does the other thing, for a worse reason: "If she went on, whatever the river did behind her was the river's doing. She wanted it not to be her." Then: "Forty-one." Atkinson has more world, more years and far more sentence. She never makes the second attempt cost a named old woman with a handbag.

**Stevenson, *Jekyll and Hyde*.** Jekyll wakes and sees Hyde's hand on the bedclothes. This novel builds on that hand: "The right hand moved before anything else did. It was Maylis's." At the van in chapter 13 it is as good as Stevenson. In the middle chapters it is worse, because possession turns into a list of who holds the jaw. Stevenson shows the hand once.

It is derivative of none of them. Its nearest debt is to Saramago's situation, and it reverses his outcome. Dostoevsky's meek clerk and bold usurper are here too, but Maylis enjoys the usurper's office: "She liked it. She slowed down to get more of it."

## 3. What is its own

- The double brings her weather. "You're the water." The self who crosses to prevent the flood is the flood. I have read the double as ruin (Golyadkin, William Wilson). I have not read her as rain.
- Counting as the whole moral subject: forty houses against one man, a demon kept off with something to count, fingers counted to prove one is awake, the dead at bingo with grains of corn. "It's four when you're the one counting… When it's your house it's one, and they knock it down."
- The partner as the instrument. Two fingers on a wrist say which woman is in the body.
- The double story played as village farce: a first meeting chaired like a council, a mayor who governs through a gap in a car window, an afterlife in the village hall with a duck in white paper.
- A motive nobody can recover, the woman included: "all she could remember was turning the handle."

## 5. Five faults, worst first

1. **The trap is not airtight at its hinge.** "She wasn't going to have him turn the handle that would send him to prison." One helper at the handle with a phone dissolves the whole dilemma. Forty-two beds against six months for eels is a sum, and this is a book about sums; here one is waved through in a sentence, after Kévin has offered. The café's nine ("A vote was a vote") and the gendarme next door are closed off as thinly. I would make it her flaw, which the book already owns: "You've never once asked me for anything." She does not ask, and she knows she is not asking.

2. **The double has no sentences of her own.** Chapter 3 opens with a mind that itemises, "Pillows: two. At home she had one," and drops it within a page. After that Lesca is narrated in Maylis's sentences. Stevenson gives Jekyll his own document; Le Tellier changes register with each passenger. I would keep the itemising syntax in every Lesca chapter, the square in chapter 15 above all.

3. **The first meeting has no vertigo.** "'Good,' Lesca said. 'You can get up. That saves a week.'" Then: "Lesca told her about the night, in order." Chapter 2 ends on a real chill ("her mouth said"), and chapter 4 spends it on an agenda and a summary the reader gets whole in chapter 10. Poe holds the look; so does James in "The Jolly Corner". I would give Maylis half a page of looking before anyone takes the chair, and cut the summary down to the stone and the date.

4. **The narrator states positions the scene has shown.** "She believed the date. She wanted that gate opened on Friday afternoon as much as Lesca did. What scared her was Monday." There are a dozen of these at scene ends. I would cut them. The one that must stay is the one at the dip.

5. **The motifs return on a timer.** Maylis gives Inguma "Either" in chapter 2 and gives it the same word, in the same beat, in chapter 9. The oven door, the four steps, the pillow and 2:51 each come back once more than they need to, and the reader starts to hear the design. I would cut the chapter 2 instance, so that chapter 9 is the first time the word costs her.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

1. The second woman does no better, neither can say why she did what she did, and the book gives no verdict between them.
2. Coralie: the wrist, the boxes on the shopping pad, "I know that word. I've cooked it."
3. The comedy that goes on inside the flood: "'It's Saturday.' 'Then they'll want the bins out.'" And Sarthou, waist-deep with a baby over his head: "That is for the file."

## 7. The verdict

It would stand on that shelf, as a novella, at the Le Tellier end and not the Saramago end. It stands there for its idea and its people, not for its sentences. Chapters 10 to 14 belong there outright, and the phone scene in chapter 11 is the best thing in it.

I would put it forward. A jury that has read those books will find the premise familiar by chapter 3 and the conclusion unfamiliar by chapter 14, which is the right way round. What decides it is fault 1. If the reader believes she was truly alone at that gate, "She wanted it not to be her" is a line they will carry out of the room.
