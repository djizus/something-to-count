# The literary read: the speculative novel a prize jury reads

## 1. Where it belongs

A many-worlds novel played as village comedy. A woman who cannot choose is visited in her sleep by the self who did choose, and drowned a man, and who brings her flood across with her. It stands nearest to Ted Chiang's "Anxiety Is the Dizziness of Freedom" and Le Tellier's *The Anomaly* for the device, to Atkinson's *Life After Life* for the second run at one night, and to Ishiguro's *Klara and the Sun* for its register.

## 2. Against those books

**Chiang, "Anxiety Is the Dizziness of Freedom".** The steel room with two lamps is Chiang's prism: a light comes on one way or the other, the world splits, and the two selves later make contact. This is the one borrowed part of the book and the least alive. "The woman from the university" asks her question and is never heard of again. Chiang takes the idea further as thought. He asks whether another self's act says anything about you, and he argues it through. This novel does not argue. It does something he doesn't: the other self is weather. "When she comes over into me she brings her river with her." It matches him once, when Lesca tries her two motives aloud: "That sounded no more true, and no less."

**Le Tellier, *The Anomaly*.** The same question, who gets the life when there are two of you, and the same dry comedy of officials. Le Tellier has a planeload and a planet. This has one bed. His doubles sit across a table. These share a body, so a lover can tell them apart by touch: "In this hand. I've had sixteen years of her saying nothing, and I know it when I've got hold of it." Nothing in *The Anomaly* is as good as that. Nothing here has its reach.

**Atkinson, *Life After Life*.** Atkinson's replays improve, because Ursula learns. Here the replay is handed to a different woman and comes out wrong in a new way. One man lives, an old woman drowns in room twelve, and the man says of his letter: "it doesn't thank you. I was in no danger, and I'll say so to anyone." On that one point this is the harder book. On span, on a whole family, on sentences with air in them, Atkinson is far ahead.

**Ishiguro, *Klara and the Sun*.** Klara bargains with the Sun in a barn, by homely rules. Maylis gives a demon "every metre of roadside she had cut in this village since 2008" to count. Both see the world in units of their work. Ishiguro has a voice, and he leaves his miracle uncertain. This book has no narrating voice, and it confirms its demon: the tablecloths lift. What it has that Ishiguro never has is jokes.

It is not derivative of any of the four as a whole. It owes Chiang the lamp.

## 3. What is its own

- The double as the disaster. "You're the water", said across a bar and heard by the village as thirst.
- A demon that clerical work can delay, and that is finally given "the fingers of my right hand". "It was a short count."
- The afterlife as village bingo, with "a duck in white paper, with its feet sticking out", and a dead woman who declines her chair: "You can put that in the minutes as apologies received."
- Possession as a custody dispute over a wrist, a jaw and a handbrake.
- Indecision as a household object: the shopping pad, the boxes, "I don't cook Either."
- A confession made by leaving a typed sheet, after which the next item is the flowers.
- What deciding costs, shown in the decider's own village: a lake, four houses, a boy in prison. "It's four when you're the one counting."

## 4. Scores

- **Originality: 7.** Known parts, a new compound. "You'll count the stones the gravel company took out of it."
- **The central idea and how far it is taken: 8** as drama, lower as thought. "If she turned that handle, Jeannot drowned and her hand had done it. … She wanted it not to be her."
- **The prose: 6.** Exact and clean, in one register. At its best: "The loudspeaker on the bank road said 'drowned him' after the square had finished hearing it."
- **The people: 7.** Coralie alone is an 8. "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them."
- **The comedy: 8.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station."
- **What the ending does: 8.** It refuses to let the second attempt redeem the first. "'Forty-one,' Coralie said."
- **Memory in a year: 7.** I will keep the hall of the dead and the kitchen timer. I will have lost who held which hand.

## 5. Five faults

1. **The narration has no voice, and it explains.** The talk is alive and the sentences between it are an inventory. They also gloss what a scene has just shown: "Maylis had written *Either* on the shopping pad on Tuesday, when she wanted the duck." "It was the smallest thing that had happened to her since Friday." Every book on this shelf is carried by a way of seeing in the narration itself. I would cut each sentence that tells me what I have understood, and let the narration take, once a chapter, the liberty it takes in the square, where a sentence is watched travelling "row by row, at walking pace".

2. **Nothing is left over.** 2:51, 3:11, nineteen turns, four steps, the reflector, the kitchen clock, the brown suitcase: every object is a plant and every plant is paid. "'Twelve. That's my door,' Madame Etcheto said", and twelve is duly called. One such stroke is fate. Twenty are clockwork, and a juror starts to watch the mechanism instead of the people. I would leave three or four unpaid, and give Peyrebarthe one page that has nothing to do with the gate.

3. **The accounting of limbs.** From chapter 5 to chapter 9 the reader tracks organs. "Lesca had the rest: the legs, the left hand and the mouth." It is ingenious and it gets in front of the feeling. The wrist under Coralie's fingers does all of this work by itself. I would allow one contested part of the body in a scene and trust the reader with the rest.

4. **The lamp.** The physics is thin where it is Chiang's and strong where it is the old woman's. "Somewhere was another Peyrebarthe. So she could cross" is the weakest inference in the book. I would shrink the experiment to the card and the envelope and let the demon carry the crossing. It already does.

5. **The two women are an argument.** One is a word, one is a manner. "Either" arrives a dozen times, and each time I know it is coming. Maylis is most a person in the few lines where she wants something for herself: "She liked it. She slowed down to get more of it." I would give her more of those, and Lesca one hour that is not procedure.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

1. **Coralie.** The pad, the pulse chart, the landing, and "Keep asking me things." She is the reason the book is a novel and not a problem.
2. **Procedure as both the comedy and the conscience.** The timer, "That the mayor remain seated", the hall of the dead taking apologies, and "Somebody has to stack the chairs."
3. **The sum that will not close.** Forty-one. A rescued man who was dry. A motive that is "no more true, and no less". The book never says who was right. Do not let it.

## 7. The verdict

It would stand on the shelf beside *The Anomaly* and *Sea of Tranquility*: as well built as either, warmer than both, and with an ending better than either has. It would not stand beside *Never Let Me Go* or *Life After Life*. Those books have a voice and a width this one has chosen not to have.

Would I put it forward? Yes, for a longlist, and I would argue for it on the comedy, on Coralie and on the last three chapters. I would not expect it to win. Readers who know Ishiguro will ask whose voice the narration is, and at present it is nobody's.
