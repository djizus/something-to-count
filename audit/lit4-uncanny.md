# The literary read: the uncanny and the folk tale

## 1. Where it belongs

A village fable in which a folk demon is given office hours. The Basque night-weight, Inguma, carries a road-mender into the life of her other self, a mayor who once opened a floodgate and drowned a man. It stands nearest to Marcel Aymé (the stories, and *La Vouivre* more than "The Man Who Walked Through Walls"), to Maupassant's "The Horla" for its first page, and to Shirley Jackson for its village. Among recent books its neighbours are Max Porter's *Lanny* and Susanna Clarke's *Piranesi*.

## 2. Against those books

**Aymé.** In *La Vouivre* a mayor has to square a serpent-girl with his anticlerical politics, and in "The Man Who Walked Through Walls" a clerk of the Registration Ministry treats a miracle as a matter for his office. This novel does that as well as Aymé does. Its best moment is pure Aymé: Lesca, seated among the dead, declines by procedure. 'The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies… You can put that in the minutes as apologies received.' What it does worse: Aymé has a narrator, dry and amused, who owns every sentence. This book has none. It reports. What it does that Aymé does not: it makes the marvel cost a life, and makes the visitor herself the disaster. Aymé's wall only traps his clerk.

**"The Horla."** The first page equals it. 'It hit the floor like a full sack dropped off a trailer.' Maupassant then spends his whole story on whether the man is mad. This novel spends one breakfast on it (Coralie's 'The doctor calls it sleep paralysis') and moves on. So it never frightens again, and by chapter 6 the demon is a late employee: 'It was five hours late.' That is a choice, and a funny one, but the dread of page one is not paid back until the thing lands on the van roof. What it does that Maupassant does not: the old charm works. Inguma can be sent to count. As I remember it from Barandiaran, the stars, the grass and the sand are the recorded formula, and the novel extends it honestly to leaves, gravel, corn and five fingers.

**Jackson.** The café is a Jackson village, a chorus that is wrong about everything and still passes sentence. Josiane's 'I said it was in that house. I only had the one name wrong' and the dealer's 'Had the key and went for a walk, did you?' would sit in "The Summer People". It is warmer than Jackson, and so less disturbing.

***Piranesi* and *Lanny*.** Like Piranesi, Maylis is a keeper who counts and who knows the date of a flood nobody will believe. Clarke gives that mind a voice. Maylis's counting is glimpsed and never sung. Inguma's skin ('overlapping brown leaves, and where the leaves parted there was grey steel underneath, with a row of rivets') will remind readers of Porter's Dead Papa Toothwort, as the gossip chorus will. The steel is this book's own.

It is not derivative of any of them. Its debts are local: the Horla's weight, Toothwort's leaves, and the marked hand of the other self from James's "The Jolly Corner".

## 3. What is its own

- The dead of the village at bingo, with corn for markers and 'a duck in white paper, with its feet sticking out'. It is a secular Mass of the dead, as in Le Braz, and I have not read it anywhere.
- A demon defeated by clerical work, whose speed matters: 'Maylis had worked out the count at the speed of the village tractor. Inguma was doing the roadsides on foot.'
- The helper as the curse. 'You're the water', heard by a hall as a request for a drink.
- Both arms of the choice lived through, by the same woman, and each one kills. 'She wanted it not to be her.'
- A planted number that closes like a tale. 'Twelve. That's my door', then, among the dead, 'Twelve. One, two', and 'Somebody pulled a chair in to a table.'

## 4. Scores

- **Originality: 8.** 'You'll count the fingers of my right hand. Then you'll take her home.'
- **The central idea and how far it is taken: 8.** It goes to the end. 'Then it wasn't Inguma that came for her. It was you.' One point lost for the hole named under fault 2.
- **The prose: 7.** At its best exact: 'The loudspeaker on the bank road said "drowned him" after the square had finished hearing it.' It has one register only.
- **The people: 7.** Jeannot, Coralie and Madame Etcheto are an 8. 'It's a hole in a field. I know what it is… You've got your shed. Leave me my hole.' Maylis herself is thinner than the people around her.
- **The comedy: 8.** 'Opened what? Castagnet, Opened. It reads as if he was opened.' I smiled throughout the bingo night and the call to Hontaube.
- **What the ending does: 8.** It refuses reward. Letter 42 'doesn't thank you', she refuses its excuse ('At 3 I never tried the handle'), and the last act is a grave dug by the man she saved for nothing.
- **Whether I will remember it in a year: 8.** The duck's feet, the delayed loudspeaker and the empty forty-second chair.

## 5. Five faults, worst first

1. **No voice.** The uncanny shelf is a shelf of voices: Jackson's Merricat, Clarke's capitals, Bulgakov's gossiping narrator. Here every sentence carries its information and nothing else, so the strangest place in the book is described like a job sheet: 'The hall smelled of floor wax and of the coffee pot.' I would not decorate. I would allow Maylis's counting mind into the narration more often and more oddly, a dozen times across the book, so the plainness reads as hers.

2. **The marvel is explained twice, and then the one thing left unexplained looks like a hole.** Madame Etcheto gives the rule ('Some nights it takes the sleeper away with it'). The university gives it again: 'What we want to know is whether there is another you, somewhere, who sees the other one.' Then 'Somewhere was another Peyrebarthe. So she could cross.' A book this fond of reasons invites the question it cannot answer: if Lesca is the water now, what was the water in 2014? Lesca's 'I don't know why it comes' is not enough. Keep the box, the chair and the two cards. Cut the sentences where the box explains itself, and let the last image ('the steel under its leaves knocked against the steel of the frame, like one part of the room touching another') do that work alone.

3. **The possession is choreographed limb by limb.** 'Lesca had the rest: the legs, the left hand and the mouth.' Across chapters 5 to 9 the reader keeps a ledger of who holds the jaw. It is farce at the bingo, where it belongs, and bookkeeping elsewhere. Aickman would give one handover and leave the rest dark. Thin it by a third, and leave at least one crossing unaccounted for.

4. **The motifs are clockwork.** Either, the pad and its boxes, two fingers on the wrist, four steps of landing, the pillow count, 'I'll be brief', forty-two, the timer, twelve. All of them return on schedule, and by the third 'Either' addressed to a demon the reader sees the gears. A tale repeats three times. Drop the first 'Either' to Inguma in chapter 2, so that chapter 9's lands, and let two of Coralie's scenes end without an object.

5. **Coralie argues the theme, and Larrère is one gesture.** Her best speech states the book's question outright: 'Either you opened it for forty houses, and I live with the mayor. Or you opened it because I was in that building.' Cut it to the phone and the time, and let the reader make the two answers. Larrère is a window gap and a folder until one line: 'She has the kitchen door open before I've switched the engine off.' Give him that sooner.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

- The bingo of the dead, and Lesca's apologies to it.
- Madame Etcheto: the charm, the care plan ('Staff to wait. Staff not to join in'), and room twelve.
- The unrewarded ending: Jeannot 'was in no danger', the café's verdict, the second shovel.

## 7. The verdict

It would stand on the shelf, at the Aymé and Porter end, not beside Jackson, Bulgakov or Clarke. The invention is at their level and the voice is not. I would put it forward for a prize judged by readers who know those books, and I would expect a shortlist rather than a win. Such readers will forgive the box and the ledger of limbs. They will ask who is telling this.
