# The literary read: the comic village

## 1. Where it belongs

A comic village novel that turns into a reckoning. A road-mender in a Gascon village shares her body with the self who became mayor of a second Peyrebarthe, where one man was drowned to keep forty houses dry. It stands nearest to Pagnol's *Jean de Florette* and *Manon of the Springs* (water, and what a village knew and did not say), to Chevallier's *Clochemerle* (municipal farce that ends with a body), and in size and temper to Fitzgerald's *The Bookshop*. Its supernatural is Marcel Aymé's, the Aymé of *La Vouivre*.

## 2. Against those books

**Pagnol.** As well: water is property before it is scenery. "The water down there is mine. A duck comes down onto standing water." And the public telling. In *Manon* the village admits in the open that it knew about the spring. Here eleven years of "No one came to tell him" give way to "Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him", and "The loudspeaker on the bank road said 'drowned him' after the square had finished hearing it." That scene is as good as Pagnol's. Worse: Pagnol's hills can be smelled and eaten, and Peyrebarthe is a square, a café and a bank. Pagnol also keeps his hidden fact (Papet's son) for the last pages, where it remakes the book. Here the hidden fact, that Larrère rang from Maïté's kitchen, is signalled from chapter 4, so the reader waits for the characters to catch up. What Pagnol does not do: the guilt here belongs to the decent person, not the schemer, and the choice is run twice, costing a life each way.

**Chevallier.** As well: the civic object. He has a urinal. This has a handle that sticks at nineteen turns, a car window lowered "a few centimetres, which was as far as it went for anybody", and a stone that lacks a verb: "Castagnet, Opened. It reads as if he was opened." The association chaired from a running car at the café door ("I'm heating the whole square") is a Clochemerle scene. Worse: Chevallier has a narrator, worldly and digressive, who follows the quarrel up to the ministry. This novel has none. What Chevallier does not do: the farce and the reckoning use the same props. The kitchen timer that cuts Larrère off in the middle of "hydraulic" is the one Lesca turns to nothing on herself.

**Fitzgerald.** As well: brevity, and power used through procedure. Mrs Gamart's Act of Parliament is Larrère's undated resignation: "Stop there. Leave room for a date, and sign under it." Her poltergeist is a fact of the premises, and so is Inguma: "It's in the plan now. Staff to wait. Staff not to join in." Both end with a village that did not want the woman. Worse: Fitzgerald's sentences judge ("She had a kind heart, though that is not of much use when it comes to the matter of self-preservation"). This novel allows itself such a sentence perhaps a dozen times ("Eighteen years of work made a thin folder"). What Fitzgerald does not do: a last page that gives mercy without consolation.

**Aymé, *La Vouivre*.** As well: a folk creature treated as parish business, in a village with a gravedigger and a mayor. Worse: Aymé needs one marvel. This has five (the steel room, the demon, the double, the hall of the dead, the weather), each with rules. What Aymé does not do: give the creature clerical work.

It is not derivative of any of them. It owes matter to Pagnol and manner to Aymé, but the plot, the double and the counting are its own.

## 3. What is its own

- A demon kept off by being given something to count, in a village where everyone counts: Jeannot's "One. Two.", Coralie's pulses, Marie-Jo's numbers, Kévin's fingers, and at the end "Forty-one."
- The afterlife as bingo in the village hall, with grains of corn, and a mayor who declines her seat by procedure.
- Two women in one body, divided limb by limb, first as farce (coffee over Josiane's shoes) and then as the crisis (one cut hand on a handbrake).
- The rescuer is the flood. "You're the water."
- "Either" as a whole character, and the shopping pad that answers it: "I don't cook Either. There's no recipe for it."
- A second chance that is not a correction. She goes to the man, the man "was dry", and an old woman drowns in room twelve.

## 4. Scores

- **Originality: 8.** "The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies… She has a ceremony at 11, and she will not be taking this seat."
- **The central idea and how far it is taken: 8.** "If she turned that handle, Jeannot drowned and her hand had done it. If she went on, whatever the river did behind her was the river's doing. She wanted it not to be her." The idea is followed to a motive that does her no credit.
- **The prose: 7.** "It wasn't much. She took more than that to the dump in a morning." Exact, and best when it measures in her trade. Between those moments it has no voice.
- **The people: 8.** "It's a hole in a field. I know what it is… You've got your shed. Leave me my hole." Coralie, Jeannot, Madame Etcheto and Maïté are at shelf level. Maylis is one trait until chapter 12.
- **The comedy: 8.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station." I smiled on most pages, even inside the flood ("Then they'll want the bins out").
- **What the ending does: 8.5.** "It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you." Both villages get the truth and neither gets relief.
- **Whether I will remember it in a year: 8.** The window that "went all the way down, into the door. Maylis hadn't known it could."

## 5. Five faults

1. **Nobody is telling it.** Every book on this shelf has a narrating intelligence, and this one reports. "Maylis took the jar in her right hand and stood it with the others. On the platform Lesca read the pillar." I would not add wit. I would let Maylis's own measure judge more often, as in "Twenty-five minutes was a trailer of leaves to the dump and back." That is the only narrator the book has.
2. **Too much apparatus in chapters 5 to 9.** "Lesca had the rest: the legs, the left hand and the mouth." The reader keeps a ledger of limbs, counts and hours. The steel box is the least rooted of the five marvels, since Madame Etcheto has already explained the crossing ("It comes to your bed"). I would thin it, add no rule after chapter 6, and give "You're the water" more than two dry days to stand on.
3. **The theme is asked out loud.** "What do you want, Maylis?" opens and closes the book and is put in other words in nearly every scene between. Coralie also explains her own gestures: "I've held hands for a living since I was twenty, so I know when a hand is holding on, and I know when it wants to be let go." Pym would leave the hand. Keep the first asking and the last, and cut the glosses.
4. **Refrains worked once too often.** Josiane's syllogism is said in the café, retold at supper and recited by Larrère in council. "Either" turns up in some ten scenes, twice to Inguma and once on Sarthou's form, before the one that matters: "'Either,' Maylis said. 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said." Monsieur Daguerre's "Here" comes four times and is perfect. Take one use from each of the others.
5. **The place is thin.** "It rained all day Tuesday, straight down." Nothing is tasted, nobody speaks a word of Gascon, and beyond the bank there is no country. A dozen particulars like "He smelled of his stove and of tall wet grass" would do it.

## 6. Three things it must not lose

- Coralie, with two fingers on the wrist, the pad and the oven door. The oven-door joke she cannot finish is the best grief in the book.
- The same props carrying the farce and the reckoning: the timer, the count, the key, and letter forty-two.
- The refusal at the end. "Forty-one." "Somebody has to stack the chairs." A man who says he was in no danger steps over the string and digs.

## 7. The verdict

Yes, it would stand on that shelf, at the Fitzgerald end of it, as a short, hard book. It is not beside Pagnol for breadth or Chevallier for voice. I would put it forward for a prize judged by readers of those books. Some will resist the machinery of the middle chapters and the plainness of the telling. Chapters 11, 14 and 15 answer both.
