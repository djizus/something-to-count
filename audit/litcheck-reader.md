# Cold read: reader

Read 00 to 17 once, in order, at speed. Nothing else opened.

### Chapter 1

**1.** `'It's my water. One.` Kind 1.
The line before it ends on the hut ("It was a cellar dug into the field"), so "It's my water" first reads as the hut. It is also the first time Jeannot counts his points, and "One." lands before I know he does that.
Change: `'That letter's about my water. One.`

**2.** `She closed her hand around nothing and turned it for the oven door.` Kind 1.
"Turned it for the oven door" read as if she were turning something to open the oven.
Change: `She faced the oven door, closed her hand around nothing and turned it.`

### Chapter 2

**3.** `'It hasn't come for you, girl. It comes to your bed. You've been lying where it sits since Monday, and you've given it nothing to count.` Kind 5. **Understood late.**
I could not tell the difference between coming for her and coming to her bed, and Coralie's question two lines later ("When has it ever sat on me?") gets no answer. It came clear in chapter 3, when Lesca lies "in the middle of the bed" and has had the thing on her "every night for twelve years".
Change: tie it to what Maylis has just said about sleeping at the edge. `You've moved into the middle, where it sits, and you've given it nothing to count.`

**4.** `There was still the barn, where the smell came from.` Kind 1.
I read it as a plan to go to the barn. She never goes, and the next line is the night. I went over it again to see what I had missed.
Change: cut the line.

### Chapter 3

**5.** `Lesca lay on her back and listened to the rain on the roof.` Kind 3.
For two sentences I thought the book had started calling Maylis by her surname. "She had handed that coat in with her keys when she became mayor" settled it.
Change: put the strangeness in the first sentence. `Lesca lay on her back under a roof that wasn't hers and listened to the rain on it.`

### Chapter 4

**6.** `The first half had come out and the rest had stuck, the way the handle stuck at nineteen turns.` Kind 5. **Understood late.**
At this line I had no idea why the word stuck. I got it about forty lines on, when Lesca's own sentence sticks and she says "It gives me somebody to blame, and it won't come out." Chapters 9, 11 and 17 confirm it.
Change: the reason is already on the page, three sentences too late. Move `Tonight she wanted one of the two, badly.` up to follow this line directly, before `'I can't,' she said.`

### Chapter 5

**7.** `Maylis had lain still for the village's sake.` Kind 1.
I had to work back from "lain still" to "let Lesca in", and then supply the unsaid half.
Change: `Maylis had let her in for the village's sake, not for this.`

### Chapter 6

**8.** `'For the minutes.'` (after `'You were the keyholder, Madame le maire,' the secretary said.`) Kind 3.
No speaker is given, and later in the same scene the secretary uses the same three words. I did not know which of them said it.
Change: `'For the minutes,' Maylis said.`

**9.** `At home it was raining. Coralie hadn't gone in to work.` Kind 3.
I was inside Maylis, inside the mayor's body, in the dry village. Then "Maylis lay on her back beside her". It took a line to see the cut.
Change: the second cut home in this chapter does it right. Use the same form here. `At home, in Maylis's bedroom, it was raining.`

### Chapter 7

**10.** `Maylis pushed until the church clock struck 4, when Inguma got down and left the way it had come.` Kind 2.
I went back to chapter 1, where she pushes and it gets down at 2:54, after three minutes. I could not tell why it now takes over an hour.
Change: let her notice it. `The first night it had got down after three minutes. Tonight it stayed until the church clock struck 4, and then left the way it had come.`

### Chapter 8

**11.** `'I'd have you tomorrow.` Kind 1.
"Would" and "tomorrow" pulled against each other. I could not tell whether she meant she would say yes tomorrow or would say yes as soon as tomorrow.
Change: `'Ask me tomorrow and I'd have you.` The line in chapter 9 ("I said I'd have you tomorrow") still works.

### Chapter 9

**12.** `'Since the night of Monday the 23rd. That was my first night on my back. It was her first night in, and it started raining.'` Kind 5, with a check back (kind 2). **The rain understood late, and one part never.**
That Lesca brings the rain I understood at the end of chapter 7 (two dry days after two nights of pushing), and "You're the water" then landed at once. But this line sent me back to chapter 1. On the first night nobody is under the thing with her, she pushes it off in three minutes, and it is already raining. In chapter 4 Lesca says "You pushed it off, the first nights." So pushing kept the road dry in chapter 7 and did not in chapter 1. I never squared it.
Change: drop the claim that does not match. `'Since the night of Monday the 23rd. That was my first night on my back, and it started raining.'`

### Chapter 11

**13.** `'I've watched you up there eleven Decembers. You never had to.'` Kind 1.
"You never had to" read first as "you never had to do what you did".
Change: `You never needed to before.'`

### Chapter 12

**14.** `From the step Maylis could see the whole length of the building. The old wing came first.` Kind 1.
I pictured her outside, looking along the wall. Then she watches Coralie do eleven chairs, so she must be looking through the door and down the inside. I rebuilt the picture.
Change: `Through the open door Maylis could see down the whole length of the building.`

### Chapter 13

**15.** `Inguma hadn't taken her home.` Kind 5. **Understood partly.**
I knew the old woman's rule and I had seen the card, so I knew this was the price of holding on. But Lesca had just said "I'm letting go", and I went back over the last lines in the van to find where it had become too late.
Change: show it becoming too late before she speaks. After `She held on to the wheel for the whole of that ring.` add one beat, for instance `The weight came off her chest.` Then her letting go drops her where it drops her.

**16.** `In front of her the tables ran in three rows under the lights, out past the place where the back wall of the village hall should have been and on further than she could see, and at every place along them, each with its bingo card and its small dish of corn, one of the village's dead sat with their head down, first the ones she had known, then the ones her father had buried, then row after row of the ones nobody living had ever met.` Kind 1.
Eighty-seven words, and "three rows" turns into "row after row" before the end.
Change: `In front of her the tables ran in three rows under the lights, out past the place where the back wall of the village hall should have been and on further than she could see. At every place there was a bingo card and a small dish of corn, and one of the village's dead sat over them with their head down. Nearest were the ones she had known. After them came the ones her father had buried, and after those the ones nobody living had ever met.`

### Chapter 14

**17.** `Kévin took him from above.` Kind 1.
Kévin is standing in the water behind the van, so I could not see what "above" was.
Change: `Kévin bent over the chair and took him.`

### Chapter 15

**18.** `Madame Etcheto sat at the end nearest the stone with her handbag on her knees.` Kind 3.
Three pages earlier I had watched her eyes being closed. For one line I was in the wrong village. I think the jolt is wanted, and it passed quickly.
Change, only if it is not wanted: `Her own Madame Etcheto sat at the end nearest the stone`, which matches "her own Coralie" earlier in the chapter.

**19.** `In this village he had retired, and he hadn't been in the square since.` Kind 1.
He is standing in the square as I read it.
Change, if this is the meaning: `In this village he had retired, and this was the one day a year he came back to the square.`

### Chapter 17

**20.** `'I said that house,' she said. 'I only had the one name wrong.'` Kind 1.
"I said that house" needed a second pass to become "I said it was in that house".
Change: `'I said it was in that house,' she said. 'I only had the one name wrong.'`

### The six things the book shows and does not explain

- **How one woman gets into the other's village.** Understood from what I was shown. Chapter 2's last page and chapter 3's "The warmth had got up to go, but Lesca had stayed sitting" were enough.
- **Why one wakes in the other's bed.** Understood from what I was shown. Chapter 6 ("So she would do what Lesca had done to her, and stay sitting") confirmed it.
- **How long she can stay.** Understood from what I was shown: twenty minutes, then for as long as Inguma is kept counting. The tractor and on-foot line in chapter 6 made the overrun clear.
- **Why a word will not come out in the steel room.** Understood late (entry 6). Why the room does this I never learned, and I did not miss it.
- **What the rain has to do with them.** Understood late, at the end of chapter 7, which felt like the right place. The first night never fitted (entries 10 and 12).
- **Where a person ends up and why.** Lesca in the hall: partly (entry 15). Madame Etcheto: understood at "Twelve. One, two." in chapter 15. Why Inguma comes to Maylis's bed at all: late (entry 3). Why it leaves for good in chapter 17: understood from the two cards going back into one envelope.

No word sent me to a dictionary. "Rivets" and "plane trees" I took from the sentence around them.

## 2. Errors

**Count: 7.** The text is very clean. Three of these are also in part 1.

**1. Chapter 16.** Motions elsewhere are in the subjunctive ("That the mayor remain seated", "that on the 12th the prefect speak alone").
As it stands: `'That the council asks the prefect to remove the mayor,' the deputy mayor read, with a finger under the line.`
Corrected: `'That the council ask the prefect to remove the mayor,' the deputy mayor read, with a finger under the line.`

**2. Chapter 14.** The blanket reads as being above his head.
As it stands: `He was holding a child wrapped in a blanket above his head.`
Corrected: `He was holding a child above his head, wrapped in a blanket.`

**3. Chapter 3.** Correct, but "ran ... down" reads for a moment as a verb of its own, straight after an action.
As it stands: `She ran the care home down in the Port.`
Corrected: `In this village she ran the care home in the Port.`

**4. Chapter 11.** Correct, but it reads as a dropped quotation mark. The paragraph ends without a closing mark because the speech runs on, and this is the only place in the book where that happens.
As it stands: `By 4 it had stopped coming, and at breakfast they told me what had been done.` followed by a new paragraph opening `'So for twelve years I've had two answers.`
Corrected: run the two paragraphs together. `By 4 it had stopped coming, and at breakfast they told me what had been done. So for twelve years I've had two answers.`

**5. Chapter 1.** Pronoun with an unclear owner (part 1, entry 1). The narrator's "It" is the hut and the next "It" is the letter.
As it stands: `'It's my water. One. A duck comes down onto standing water.`
Corrected: `'That letter's about my water. One. A duck comes down onto standing water.`

**6. Chapter 15.** Reads as a contradiction (part 1, entry 19).
As it stands: `In this village he had retired, and he hadn't been in the square since.`
Corrected: `In this village he had retired, and this was the one day a year he came back to the square.`

**7. Chapter 13.** "Three rows" against "row after row", and "one of the village's dead" followed by "first the ones" (part 1, entry 16). Corrected text is given there.

Not counted: the spelling is British throughout, but three words are not. "Dish towel" (chapters 1, 5, 11), "truck" (chapters 11, 14) and "baby carriage" (chapter 17) stood out beside "torch", "pavement", "cool box" and "windscreen".

## 3. Who is against her

**Who or what stands against Maylis**

- Lesca, her other self. She wants the body, the bed and Coralie, and she brings the river. 5.
- Her own "Either". It shuts the gate at 5 on Friday, with Jeannot standing on the boards. 5.
- Larrère. The undated resignation, Kévin's name on a sheet, "it stays shut". 3. He is always behind glass or on a phone, and never there at night.
- Jeannot. He votes shut, locks his trapdoor and makes her say which. 3.
- The river and the bank. 3.
- Inguma. 2. It carries more than it attacks.
- The café. 1.

**A point where I wanted somebody working against her and nobody was**

Chapter 12, from 5:30 to 2:51. Larrère has gone up the lane, Jeannot is under his trapdoor and Lesca has not come yet. For nine hours nothing stands between her and the handle but sleep, and two missed calls are all the pressure there is. Chapter 10 has the same gap, more lightly.

**Does the book lack an antagonist?**

No. Lesca is one in full, since she writes down what she means to take and comes for it, and Larrère is enough of a second; what goes missing is a person in Maylis's way during the last hours before the bank breaks.
