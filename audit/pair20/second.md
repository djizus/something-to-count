# Pair 20 — second

Read B first, then A. Fourteen of the eighteen chapters are word for word the same. A carries four extra passages, about 160 words, in chapters 3, 4, 9 and 17, all about the steel room and the cards.

## 1. Which one

A, slightly.

## 2. By criterion

- **Stakes:** level. Same text: "If she turned that handle, Jeannot drowned and her hand had done it."
- **Tension:** level. The phone counted ring by ring through chapters 12 and 13 is identical.
- **Progression:** A. Ch. 9: "I got your rib that way. You're getting my flood." The grey-water picture of ch. 2, the rib and the rain become one rule. In B, "It comes with me" is only asserted.
- **Comedy:** A, by one smile. Ch. 4: "They asked whether I also saw the other one." / "You're looking at her." The rest is shared: the oven door, "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station", "And six. Bugger off."
- **Characters:** level. Coralie on the landing: "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak."
- **The ending:** A. "Maylis put the two cards in together. [...] One lamp went out. Then the other."
- **Clarity:** A. Read twice in both: the river sum in ch. 7 (a metre, seventy, fifty-five) and which hand is whose in the van in ch. 13. In B only: at the end I went back to ch. 2 to find what the *right* card had been for, and found nothing. In A only: ch. 17 puts the raincoat card in her hand in the room without saying she took it to bed.

## 3. The first fifty pages

The same place in both, the last line of ch. 2, about page 17: "'Come back across,' her mouth said. Maylis hadn't said it." Neither gets there sooner. A's exchange in ch. 4, still inside the fifty, answers the question ch. 1 asked. B never answers it.

## 4. The night of the flood

Identical text, and I believed it in both. At the dip she walks on because "She wanted it not to be her." Sixteen chapters of Either earn that, and Madame Darrigrand's "You had the key" stops the book forgiving her. A makes the 2:51 rule firmer, because the flood is already something Lesca carries across. In B I took that rule on trust.

## 5. The last two chapters

Ch. 16 is identical. A's ch. 17 ends better. The two answers go back into one envelope, the lamps go out, and "The card from the steel room wasn't in there any more", the book's only physical proof that the room was real. B stops at "I won't" and a door shutting, with the room still lit.

## 6. What the loser does better

- B, ch. 3: "Lesca lay in the middle of the bed with her left hand closed, as if those fingers were still in it. / So she could cross." A puts a recap of ch. 1 between those two lines. Ch. 4 does that job better and funnier.
- B, ch. 17: "She didn't call anything to order. / 'Forty-one,' Maylis said." A holds the number back for three sentences of card business. The cards could come in after "I won't".
- B, ch. 9: "'Now I know. It comes with me." A then repeats the ch. 4 rule in full before its new line. B's run-in plus only "You're getting my flood" would do.

## 7. Against the comparison

For A: below, narrowly. It has rules stated and then used against the people who stated them, and village comedy that made me smile. The flood night and "and I drowned him" are level with either. The one thing that decides it: the box never turns. It is what the woman at the door said it was in ch. 1, the university never comes back, and after "You're the water" in ch. 8 no reversal of the premise is left for the last act. Crouch and The Good Place both keep one.

## 8. The steel room and the two cards, by the last page

**B.** The steel room is where the two women meet: one chair, shared knowledge, the seated one wakes in the other's body, nobody standing can lie. What the university built it for is said once, in ch. 1, and never joined to any of that. I had to work out for myself that the test succeeded. The *left* card tells Maylis which lamp she saw. The *right* card, seen once in ch. 2, tells her another hand of hers wrote the opposite. After that the *right* card is never mentioned, and the *left* card rides in her pocket to the last page and does nothing.

**A.** The steel room is the experiment that split her, and it worked: one lamp each in June 2014, and "Somewhere was Peyrebarthe." It is also the pipe between them, the rib one way and the flood the other. The cards are the two answers to the woman's question, one from each village. Put back into one envelope, they close the experiment: lamps out, Inguma gone, the card gone from the pocket.

In both, the card reads *left*, the verb Maïté wanted cut on the stone. I couldn't tell whether that is meant.
