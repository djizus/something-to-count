## 7. Against the comparison (version A)

**Verdict: slightly below, and one thing decides it.** Between chapter 5 and chapter 10 the book has one new turn (chapter 7: the rain comes when Lesca comes). A Crouch novel and a Good Place episode both give a turn every chapter. Here the plan stays the same for five chapters (get the gate opened on Friday afternoon), and the same contest is replayed in new rooms: hand against hand on the stair post, the timer, the jaw at the bar, the mouth in the kitchen. Each scene is good. Together they are a holding pattern, and a reader of those two will feel it.

Outside that stretch it holds its own:

- **Against Crouch.** Level in chapters 0 to 5 and 10 to 14. "That village hadn't had its flood yet" (chapter 3) is a Crouch turn. Chapter 14 cannot be put down: the clock times, the van on its belly in the dip, "She wanted it not to be her." Above Crouch on people. Coralie's "2:51. I've known that for twelve years" (chapter 11) is better than anything he writes about a marriage.
- **Against The Good Place.** Level on the moral machine. Standing in the steel room you cannot say a thing you know is untrue; the dead play bingo and call your name off a card; a trolley problem is run with a real handle. Below on warmth at the close, which is a choice and I would keep it.
- **Comedy.** I smiled often up to chapter 9, then rarely, which is right for the story. It is dry village comedy, not gag comedy. A Good Place fan will smile, not laugh.
- **Clarity.** I read three places twice. Chapter 6, "At home she got him a few centimetres at a time" (the car window, as it turns out). Chapter 9, Lesca's rule that only Friday at 2:51 matters. Chapter 14, "it had come too late", where I went back to check the rule had not been broken. It had not, but I had to check.

**Chapter by chapter: would I have stopped there?**

- **00.** No. "It rang once" is a promise.
- **1.** No. Smiled at the postman asking for "the man who works for the village" and at Coralie talking to the oven door. The last line pulls.
- **2.** No. This is where I could no longer stop: "'Come back across,' her mouth said."
- **3.** No. Best turn in the book. Smiled at "It reads that she opened him."
- **4.** No. Smiled at "There's nobody in the chair." The chrysanthemums are funny and sad at once.
- **5.** No. Funniest chapter: the window going all the way down, "It takes a small m", Josiane's two-way theory of adultery.
- **6.** Nearly. It is the longest sit: half a day waiting for Inguma to come. The pulse chart going down to 34 kept me. Smiled at the letters signed on the floor and at "timed like an egg".
- **7.** No. Bordenave's cross turning into proof is neat.
- **8.** No. Smiled at "She wants water" and "How's that spelled?"
- **9.** Yes. This is where I would have put it down for the night. Seven scenes, a vote I knew would fail, and a second chair meeting that restates the rules. The card with her name on it at the end brought me back.
- **10.** No. The night itself, then Jeannot's six points. "And six. Bugger off" was my widest smile.
- **11.** No. It delays the flood by a chapter and earns it.
- **12.** No. Long, but "Either" on the platform hurts in the right way. Smiled at "Then it should have gone last Monday."
- **13.** No. Smiled at "apologies received".
- **14.** No. Could not have.
- **15.** No. The sentence travelling back through the square "at walking pace" is the image I will keep.
- **16.** I could have stopped here satisfied about Lesca. It reads as her epilogue. I went on because Maylis was owed one.
- **17.** The end. Letter 42 pays off and Jeannot picking up the second shovel is the right last picture.

**What I would tell the friend.** The first five chapters are as quick as Crouch and funnier. The middle is slower than you are used to; chapter 9 is the wall. From chapter 10 you will not put it down, and the ending does not let anyone off.
