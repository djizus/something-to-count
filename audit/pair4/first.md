# Pair 4 — first

Read A in full, then B. The opening and chapter 13 are word for word the same; the versions part mainly in chapters 7–10 and 15–16. Reading A first means B's surprises reached me second-hand.

## 1. Which one

**B, slightly.**

## 2. By criterion

- **Stakes: B.** Ch. 7, at the gauge: "The river had stood still for the two nights she kept Lesca out. It was raining now because Lesca was back." From there every crossing costs a day of river. A holds this back until the end of ch. 8.
- **Tension: B.** Bingo night. B puts a clock on the shut teeth: "if Lesca couldn't say the words to it, it would take her home and the river would stop." A has only "she meant to keep them shut until the hall was empty."
- **Progression: B.** Ch. 9, the pad: "She crossed out the line about the hatch and wrote it again at the top, above the key, with *before dark* after it." In A she fights Lesca on Sunday, offers her the right arm on Tuesday and forbids her on Wednesday.
- **Comedy: B.** "'She wants water,' one of the Lassalle sisters said. 'She's got a bar full of coffee,' said her sister." And Josiane: "She lives with a nurse, and that's to throw us off."
- **Characters: B, narrowly.** Kévin talks in questions ("Do I turn it off? Will they mind it, at the care home?") and Maïté cannot finish a sentence ("In the— It's blankets."). A has the better Lesca: "You hear everybody, and then you do the sum by yourself."
- **The ending: A, narrowly.** Coralie in ch. 16: "At four I shut her eyes."
- **Clarity: B.** A sent me back twice: why her teeth are shut at the bar, and the handover note in ch. 8. B sent me back once, for Marie-Jo's "'One,' … 'On its own. Sit down on it.'"

## 3. The first fifty pages

Level. Same place in both, in the same words. Hooked at the end of ch. 2, about page 15: "'Come back across,' her mouth said. Maylis hadn't said it." Unable to stop from the ladder in ch. 3, about page 20: "That village hadn't been spared its flood. It hadn't had it yet." B moves a little quicker through ch. 4–5 on Josiane and Jeannot's counting; A has made me like the couple more by then, with the laugh in the corridor.

## 4. The night of the flood

The night itself is identical. What differs is what she carries onto the bank. I believed **B**, by a little.

In B "hatch first" is on her pad, in her own hand, before Lesca leaves "Go to the blind first" in her mouth. Driving past the handle is then her own plan, made for daylight and carried into the dark. And "You're the water" has already been said once, in public, and laughed off, so her certainty at the handbrake is earned. In A the idea comes from Lesca and from rivalry, and the mistake feels more Lesca's than hers.

B has one hole. At five, with Jeannot standing on the bank, she says "Either" and takes the handle off, and B no longer says why. A does.

## 5. The last two chapters

**A ends better, narrowly.**

B's ch. 15 is the better of the two: it stops explaining the clock and the timer rule, and the council scene is quicker. But B's ch. 16 cuts into the bone. "Maylis had meant to stop there" now follows a single word, "Forty-one". Lesca never owns the river in the one room where it would have to be true. Coralie's account of room twelve loses its three hardest sentences.

Against that, only B earns Kévin's "I flew", because only B has him say in ch. 2 that he wants to.

## 6. What A does better

1. Ch. 11: "If the gate went up now and Lesca never came tonight, the flood on the meadows was Maylis's alone. At three, if it came, it would be the river's doing."
2. Ch. 16: "At three you said the bank end, and I went. I was at her door when the window came in, and I couldn't open it. At four I shut her eyes."
3. Ch. 16: "'It was mine,' Lesca said. 'You asked me not to, and I brought it.'" Then "'I wanted your sash,' she said. 'And your square.'" and "'It folds in three,' Lesca said."
4. Ch. 2: "She put her forehead against the wall beside the door and shook, while Maylis held her arm across her side and leaned on the wall as well. They couldn't look at each other. Down the corridor Bixente studied his mop."
5. Ch. 15: "'Whole, and then in parts?'" / "'Twelve. One, two. And a chair was pulled in.'"

## 7. Against the comparison

**Below**, for B.

Chapter 13 is level with a Blake Crouch set piece, and B's bingo night is level with a good half-hour of The Good Place. What decides it is the heroine. Her trait is not choosing, so for the first hundred pages the book is driven by the visitor and the calendar. Crouch and The Good Place both open on a lead who wants something out loud from page one.
