# Pair 8: second reader

Read A first, then B. They are the same book to within about fifteen sentences: nine of the seventeen chapters match word for word, and 10, 11 and 13 differ by one sentence each.

## 1. Which one

**B, slightly.** On clarity it is clearly B. On everything else the two are close to level.

## 2. By criterion

- **Stakes: B.** Ch. 8, Lesca in the steel room: "If I'm in your village at 2:51 on Friday night, the water comes through your bank as it came through mine, and your bank goes. If I'm not, it holds." A stops at "Now I've got both".
- **Tension: B.** Ch. 11, at 02:49: "She laid her left hand on the steering wheel and touched each finger in turn. She counted five." Two minutes later the thing lands on the van roof, and after the ch. 8 line I knew what that cost.
- **Progression: level.** Same scenes, same order.
- **Comedy: A, by one line.** Ch. 4: "Both mine, and I had your rib. That isn't one of the points." B turns the joke into a rule. Everything else that made me smile is in both: the oven door, "I cannot summon Either to the station", Jeannot's six points.
- **Characters: level.** B gives Maylis "all that time it was doing my job". A gives Lesca "She had picked."
- **The ending: level.** Chapters 14 to 16 are identical.
- **Clarity: B.** Pages read twice: **A six, B two.**
  - A: Lesca's first waking (ch. 3); how Maylis knows about the gutter (ch. 4); who won at the bingo bar (ch. 7); why only Friday counts (ch. 8); the two hands fighting in the van (ch. 12); "it had come too late" (ch. 13).
  - B: the ch. 3 waking and the ch. 12 hands only.
  - Reading B second flatters it, but the four pages that cleared are the four where B has a sentence A lacks.
  - One snag in B: "I've been pushing it off since the rib, and all that time it was doing my job". Pushing it off is also what kept Lesca out on Wednesday and Thursday.

## 3. The first fifty pages

The same place in both, because the text there is the same. At about 275 words a page, the hook is the last line of ch. 2, near page 15: "'Come back across,' her mouth said. Maylis hadn't said it." I could not stop from about page 21, in ch. 3: "That village hadn't been spared its flood. It hadn't had it yet."

B's two additions before page fifty did not bring that moment forward.

## 4. The night of the flood

B's. The choice itself is word for word the same: she slows at the handle, drives past, and at the dip "She wanted it not to be her." I believed that in both. B earns it better in three places.

- I already knew that Lesca being there at 2:51 was the flood. So "She had put Lesca out, and it had come too late" was a blow in B and a puzzle in A.
- In B, Lesca's "Go to the hut first" comes with no explanation and reads as Lesca mending her own night, which is what she admits in ch. 16. A adds that Maylis "could be trusted with the rest of the night", a second motive that disagrees with the first.
- B says "By her own count she could have had the gate up at 3." A says flatly "The gate would have gone up at 3", and Jeannot disputes that in the last chapter: "it took two arms and a knee. You had the one arm."

## 5. The last two chapters

Level, since they are identical. They land a little better after B, for the last two reasons above.

## 6. What A does better

1. Ch. 4: "Both mine, and I had your rib. That isn't one of the points." B needs its rule, but it could keep this joke.
2. Ch. 10: "The secretary had her hand over her mouth." The only sign of how "I won't be brought back" sounds to someone outside it.
3. Ch. 7: "Maylis felt the night go out with them." Better sound than "Inguma was gone. Maylis had lost.", though B's is the one I understood.
4. Ch. 7: "She had put her own pillow under Maylis's knees and added two columns of ticks to the shopping pad, headed *eyes* and *turned*." B's fuller chart repeats ch. 6.
5. Ch. 7: "They walked back. Maylis did the sum at the water and again at the fence." B spells the sum out in trailers, which ch. 6 had already given me.

## 7. Against the comparison

**Below, narrowly.** The one thing is effort. A Blake Crouch novel states each rule once, in a line you could repeat to someone, and The Good Place does the same with a joke on top. Here the rules of crossing arrive as six or seven points over eight chapters: the chair, standing and truth, the right hand, the counting, the river that follows the file. Even B wanted my whole attention where those two would have carried me.

It is ahead of most Crouch in one respect: the ending costs something.
