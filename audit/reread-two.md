# Every place I went back — reader two

Read 00 to 17 in order, once, at thriller speed. Each entry is a place where I stopped, went back or caught up late. The five kinds:

1. read twice to understand
2. went back to an earlier page to check a fact, a rule, a name or a date
3. did not know who, whose head, which village or whose hand
4. a word I would have to look up
5. understood only later

## 1

**1.1** — kind 5
> 'Don't. He'd only ring me to dig the hole.'

I thought it was a joke I had missed: why would the priest ring her? Fifteen paragraphs later ("Maylis dug the graves in Peyrebarthe") I saw she is the gravedigger.
Change: `'Don't. I dig his graves. He'd only ring me for the hole.'`

**1.2** — kind 4
> Maylis was the village's only outdoor worker, so she spent it in the square, raking plane leaves off the drain covers.

I read "plane" as flat, then as aircraft. It is the tree. Chapter 2 says "plane trees" and I understood then.
Change: `raking leaves off the drain covers under the plane trees.`

**1.3** — kind 1
> He nodded towards the flood fields, where his duck hut was a cellar under a trapdoor in the grass.

I pictured a hut with a cellar under it. The hut is the cellar: a hole in the field he shoots ducks from.
Change: `where he shot ducks from a cellar under a trapdoor in the grass. He called it his hut.`

**1.4** — kind 2
> On Monday the river was low. You put the gate up, and my water ran out into the river.

I went back to the prologue: "The gate was there to let the river out onto those fields when it ran too high." I had the gate as a one-way thing, river to fields. It is a door between the two, and water goes to the lower side.
Change: `On Monday the river was lower than my fields. You put the gate up, and my water ran out into it.`

**1.5** — kind 1
> She opened her pocket knife and cut eighteen marks down the handle, starting level with her shoulder.

"The handle" is the gate's handle six times in this scene, so for a moment she was cutting marks in that. It is the rake.
Change: `cut eighteen marks down the rake`

**1.6** — kind 5
> 'Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you, and that was me.'

I thought Coralie meant she had supplied the answer herself. Six lines on, the answer turns out to be the word "You".
Change: `'Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you. The answer was me.'`

## 2

**2.1** — kind 4
> Boxes like that were for glass eels, which came up the river on the winter tides.

I did not know "glass eels". The price and the prison told me enough to go on, but I could not picture them.
Change: `for glass eels, the baby eels that came up the river on the winter tides.`

**2.2** — kind 1, and kind 3 for the door
> That was today, which gave her a reason to stop at the Labats' door on the way.
> By the time she had the village shed unlocked he was standing at the door.

I did not see why Christmas lights were a reason to call on the Labats, and then did not know which door Kévin was at. She needs a second pair of hands because of the rib, and he has come to the shed.
Change: `With the rib she needed help to hang them, which gave her a reason to stop at the Labats' door on the way.` and `he was standing at the shed door.`

**2.3** — kind 1
> 'That's the way in,' Kévin said.

She has just said "I did a rib", so I took "that" for the rib. He means the thing sitting on her.
Change: `'That thing's the way in,' Kévin said.`

**2.4** — kind 1
> 'She rings at 3.'

I took this as "no, it doesn't work". It means the words hold until 3, then it comes and she rings for the staff. "It came at 1" a few lines on put me right.
Change: `'Till 3. Then she rings.'`

**2.5** — kind 1
> 'There's only me in that bed with her,' Coralie said. 'When has it ever sat on me?'

I had lost what she was answering. It is "It comes to that bed", six lines up, on the far side of the bingo.
Change: `'You said it comes to that bed. I'm in that bed,' Coralie said. 'When has it ever sat on me?'`

## 3

**3.2** — kind 1

**3.3** — kind 4

To me a terrace is a paved place outside a house. Here it is the high ground the village stands on. The word comes back in 4 and 6.

**3.4** — kind 5

In the prologue she is the one who rings. I went back to look for a call to her and found none. I only understood in 6 ("My order that night was to keep the gate shut") and fully in 10.
Change: `Larrère would ring her, as he had rung Lesca, and tell her to keep the gate shut.`

**3.5** — kind 4

The chapter is full of three minutes on a timer, so I read "minutes" as time. Two lines on ("with the minutes open beside the pan") it is a document.

## 4

**4.1** — kind 3
> It was Saturday breakfast, with rain on the kitchen window.

Chapter 3 ends on Saturday night in Lesca's village. For a line I thought this was the next morning there. I went back to check it was the same Saturday.
Change: `It was breakfast that same Saturday, in Maylis's kitchen, with rain on the window.`

**4.2** — kind 1
> 'I held yours. She put two fingers on it, and I took them.'

I thought Lesca was saying she had held Maylis's hand. She means she held Coralie's fingers, using Maylis's hand.
Change: `'With your hand. She put two fingers on your wrist, and I took them.'`

**4.3** — kind 1
> 'I had that answer ready.' Lesca's fist opened and closed. 'That nobody has looked after your bank for twelve years.'

Two lines earlier the sentence would not come out, because it was not true. Now it comes out whole, and I thought the rule had broken. She is only reporting what she was going to say.
Change: `'I had an answer ready. I was going to say that nobody has looked after your bank for twelve years.'`

**4.4** — kind 1
> 'You push. I've felt you do it. Your rib will tell you how many nights you've got.'

I read a threat, as in nights left to live. It means she can keep Lesca out only as long as the rib can stand the pushing. Chapter 7 shows it.
Change: `'You can keep me out for as many nights as that rib lets you push.'`

**4.5** — kind 5
> Afterwards his nets were found under the floor of the hut, green and folded wet.

I took them for duck-hunting nets and wondered why they were mentioned. In 6 they are eel nets, something she can hold over him.
Change: `his eel nets were found`

## 5

**5.1** — kind 4
> Lesca counted the clicks of the catch as it came.

"Catch" as a thing, not an action. I worked out from "missed its tooth" that it is the part that drops in and holds the chain.
Change: `the clicks of the catch that held the chain`

**5.2** — kind 2
> The mouth stopped on it. It tried again.

I went back to 4 to check the rule. There, a woman cannot say an untrue thing while standing in the steel room. I thought the rule had followed her into the square. As far as I can tell it is simpler: the mouth starts Maylis's old word out of habit and Lesca cuts it off. I am still not sure.
Change: `Lesca stopped it. The mouth tried again, and Lesca stopped it again.`

**5.3** — kind 1
> You'll stop this car at the café door and tell me that the gate stays shut, and that the Port knows it has a river.

I took "knows it has a river" as "has been warned". It means the people there chose to live beside a river, so the risk is theirs. The line comes back in 9, so the change goes there too.
Change: `and that people in the Port know they live by a river.`

## 6

**6.2** — kind 2

**6.3** — kind 1

**6.4** — kind 1

"The platform" has been the floodgate's platform all through the book, and three paragraphs up he says "When you stood on that platform". I thought of the gate. He means the stage in the square on the 12th.

**8.2** — kind 1

## 9

**9.1** — kind 1, and kind 3 for the speaker
> Maylis squeezed. 'That's all of it.'

I did not know who spoke or what "it" was. It is Maylis, and it is the strength left in her right hand.
Change: `Maylis squeezed. 'That's all the grip I've got.'`

**9.2** — kind 2
> 'The count tells it when to take the visitor home,' Lesca said. 'It doesn't keep it off. 2:51 is my minute.'

I went back twice: to 2 ("You're not to come till you've finished", "It hasn't come for you, girl. It comes to that bed") and to 5, where Lesca's words at the kitchen door send it away for a day. Until here I believed the words kept it off whoever said them. This line says it comes at 2:51 whatever is said, and the count only delays the fetching home of the one who has crossed. I had to rebuild the rule from three places.
Change: `'It sits on the sleeper at 2:51 whatever you tell it. The words only work on its other trip, when it comes to fetch a visitor home.'`
