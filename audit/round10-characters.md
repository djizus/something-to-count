# Critic's report: Characters

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** Counted, and every unit has a face by the time it is spent. "Behind it were forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." (2)
- **Tension: 7.** Chapters 13 to 15 are a 9. The middle runs on procedure and a date that is still a week off. "The dyke had six minutes, and on foot she could reach one of the two." (15)
- **Progression: 7.** Most chapters change the terms. Chapter 10 does not, and ends on the weakest line in the book: "She did not lie down." (10)
- **Comedy: 8.** The people are funny in their own idiom, and the idiom is what later hurts. "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade.'" (7)
- **Characters: 8.** Lesca 9, Coralie 9, Jeannot 9, Maylis 7, Larrère 6. "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'" (9)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 9.** The best thing in the book. The joke from chapter 1 is tried again after a death and will not come: "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the neck. 'The—'" (18)
- **Prose: 8.** Plain, exact, and consistent across both women. "It had been gone over every night for twelve years, and it was worn as smooth as a step." (11)
- **The ending: 8.** It costs a death, a village, a job and a marriage, and the last image is right. "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (18) The chapter that carries it is overloaded (faults 4 and 5).
- **Clarity: 6.** The rules of who rides whom are given once, as a joke, and then relied on for ten chapters. "'The one who wakes first takes the other home in her.'" (5)

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who reads novels, yes. On the Crouch reader the bar names, not yet: that reader is lost at the top of chapter 8 and idle through chapter 10.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Verdict for this lens: above Crouch, a notch below The Good Place.**

What puts it above Crouch:

1. **The spouse is a person.** In Dark Matter, Daniela is what Jason wants. Coralie wants something that is neither woman, says so, and posts it: "I posted it this afternoon, and I posted it for me." (9) She also refuses to be the prize while one of them cannot speak. Crouch has no scene like the landing.
2. **The double is wrong in a way we enjoy.** Lesca moves a motion to take another woman's bed, corrects the capital on the café's score pad ("It takes a small m"), and presides over a room with nobody in the chair. Crouch's other Jason is only a threat.

What keeps it below The Good Place:

3. **The central pair never get a scene that runs its length.** Eleanor and Chidi change each other in rooms they cannot leave. Maylis and Lesca meet standing five times, for perhaps six pages in all, and are cut off each time (fault 2). The ensemble is level with the series: each villager is wrong in their own way, and the reversals re-read what came before ("Come back across" from chapter 3 to chapter 4; the prologue against chapter 11; "still in the box" becoming "gone for a walk"; Kévin in prison in the village where the keyholder became mayor).

And one place where it gives away Crouch's best effect: the terror of being replaced in your own house is told every time from the side of the woman doing the replacing (fault 1).

**The lens questions, answered.**

- *Distinct?* Yes, within a line. One prices things and says Either; the other makes three points and calls for those in favour.
- *Wanted-for?* Lesca, fully. Maylis's one want is announced in chapter 10 and gone by chapter 11 (fault 3).
- *Alive?* Lesca throughout. Maylis in chapters 7, 8, 13 and 15, when she is doing something. For the rides in between she is a hand.
- *Softened or excused?* Lesca is not softened by events: "Your counting comes out right for you each time" (16) is said to her face, and she refuses Larrère as an excuse. She is shielded by arrangement: nobody who loves her is allowed to press the one question (fault 8). Maylis is softened twice: by Lesca's closing verdict (fault 5), and by the price of her "both" being a woman of ninety-four who had refused the chair and been walked back to her room by somebody else.
- *Described from outside as a woman?* No. Neither has a face or a figure. They have a rib, a seam, a tall L and a job. "The commune's girl", "little Lesca" and "Monsieur le maire" belong to the village, and the book shows the village saying them.
- *Who would be missed?* Jeannot, Madame Etcheto, Monsieur Daguerre, Kévin, Sarthou. Not Josiane or Lafitte, who are one joke each. Not Larrère, who is a car window.
- *Which relationship carries it, and does it pay?* Coralie and whichever Maylis is in the room. On Lesca's side it pays in full (12, 17). On Maylis's side it pays at the back door of the home (13) and is then settled in fifty lines of a crowded last chapter.
- *Does the reader care who lives?* Yes. Jeannot from chapter 1, Madame Etcheto from chapter 3. Room twelve hurts.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The woman being ridden is never the point of view. (6, 9, 10; structural)**

> "In the right hand Maylis was awake. Lesca could feel her in the fingers, which shut whenever they were left alone." (6)

> "Maylis went for her own mouth, to say that it was not she who asked. She could not get one word into it." (9)

The point of view goes with whoever is driving. So every scene in which somebody with Maylis's face ticks her boxes, makes her wife laugh and walks her body up her own stairs is told from the thief's side. Maylis's dread reaches us as a report from Lesca ("more afraid of her own stairs than she had been of the winch") or in two-sentence flashes. This costs the book twice. The reader is never afraid for the marriage from inside it, which is the one thing Crouch does better than anyone. And the woman who has to choose in chapter 15 arrives there with the fewest pages of interior of any principal.

*Do:* give the landing at midnight in chapter 9 wholly to Maylis. She hears her own voice ask. She hears Coralie say "I'd have you tomorrow" to it. She has one hand. Keep every line of dialogue; change whose head we are in. If one scene only, that one. The soup in chapter 6 is the second candidate.

**2. The two women never finish a conversation, and the one thing Lesca owes Maylis arrives by leak. (5, 8, 10, 11; structural)**

> "'Then tell me. What's in the stone?' / 'You've had the date and the sum.' / 'That's what's on it.' / Lesca did not answer." (10)

> "Lesca was not there. Her night was. She was awake over it, and it came without her, entire." (11)

Each steel-room scene ends the same way: the hard question is put, and Inguma gets into the chair. Chapter 5 ends on it, chapter 8 ends on it, chapter 10 ends on it. Then the answer Lesca has refused twice to her double's face is delivered by the mechanism while she is at her kitchen table. Her confession to the square is an act. Her confession to the one person who needs it that night is an accident. The book's concept is two versions of one woman, and they are never made to stay in the room.

*Do:* let chapter 10's scene run. Either Lesca gives the night herself, standing, to her own face, or she lies down at the end of chapter 10 so that it will be had. Both are acts. Keep the telling of the night in chapter 11 word for word.

**3. Maylis's one want is declared and dropped. (10, 11, 13; structural)**

> "'I want your side. The chair, the timer, a table that won't sit down till I have.'" (10)

> "Maylis had meant to lend that woman her arms for the night, and take a sash for it." (11)

> "She put the pencil through each line in turn." (13)

It is seeded properly: the sash in chapter 3, "I want that" at the telephone in chapter 8. Then it is given up in a subordinate clause and a pencil line. Lesca's want gets a motion, a landing, a letter to the prefect and eight rings. Maylis's gets one speech. A woman whose whole flaw is that she will not want cannot have her first want removed offstage.

*Do:* make her feel it go at the moment it would have been useful. The call to Hontaube in chapter 13 is already that scene: in chapter 8 a prefecture answered her at the second ring, and here a clerk asks whether she is a request or a notification. One line there, in her terms, pays for the pencil.

**4. Chapter 18 carries too much, and Coralie leaves for four reasons. (18)**

> "'I chose you, Maylis, over somebody who asked.'"

> "'That was Either, with the river in it.'"

> "'Don't give me the measurement. I could. I'm not going to.'"

The chapter holds the last sitting, Larrère and the dated letter, Sarthou, the tow, Kévin's flight, Arlette, Madame Darrigrand, the home, Maïté's bus, the café, Marie-Jo, Coralie's departure, the priest, and the grave. The scene the book has built since page one gets about fifty lines among thirteen exits. Inside it Coralie gives room twelve, the landing, "both", and Bayonne, one after another. Any one would do. Four reads as the design making sure, and forty hours earlier she said she would carry the pillow over herself.

*Do:* split it. The Port walk is one chapter. The kitchen, the bag and the grave are another. In the kitchen keep "Either, with the river in it" and the question with no box, to which Maylis's answer is not Coralie. That exchange is the reason. Cut the recap of the landing and the second use of the twenty minutes.

**5. Lesca delivers the book's verdict. (18)**

> "'No. You won't sign it, and I'll say why at length.'"

> "'A woman is dead of that, and it still isn't what I did. Don't hand me your sum to sit behind.'"

For seventeen chapters the book refuses to say which woman did better. Here one of them says it, at length, and finds for the other. It tells the reader what to think of Maylis and lets her off. The same scene shrugs at the premise: "'Why did it rain?' ... 'I don't know.' ... They left it there."

*Do:* cut Lesca's speech to the facts she alone can give: "I let it ring once. You rang nine times." Then stop. Let Madame Darrigrand's pram be the other half of the argument.

**6. The two minutes do not add up, so Lesca's second guilt is soft. (15, 18)**

> "'Those two minutes are your two hundred metres, and they're mine.'" (18)

> "'at nineteen it stopped as if it had come to a wall ... Third, it's up a hand. A hand.'" (15)

Lesca's wanting is meant to have cost a life: "She paid for it with hers." A reader who checks finds that two more minutes put Maylis at the foot of a winch with nineteen turns to make on a fresh break, and a handle that stopped a fit man at nineteen. They also find a van Maylis sank herself after two warnings. Lesca's charge against herself is generous, and the reader can acquit her.

*Do:* attach the two minutes to something they would have bought. The call to Coralie goes two minutes sooner; nine, ten and eleven come out sooner; twelve's door is reached before the water is on it. Coralie already has the line: "Twelve wouldn't open."

**7. Larrère never says a true sentence. (10, 17, 18)**

> "'did you—' / He stopped." (10)

> "'I'll be brief,' he said. ... Nothing came." (17)

> "'They tell me,' Larrère said, and stopped." (18)

The man whose order sits under both floods is a window that goes up. That is very funny. But the affair, the kitchen on the night, and "it made no odds where he was" all reach us through Maïté, and when he is given the floor he trails off three times. The third silence is worth less than the first. In Maylis's village he is in that kitchen again on the night and nobody says so to him.

*Do:* keep the silence at the garden gate. Let him finish the question in chapter 10, and let it be the wrong question, asked for his own sake.

**8. Nobody who loves Lesca is allowed to press her, and the non-answer is given three times. (11, 12, 16, 17)**

> "'I've never wanted to hear which. Don't say it now.'" (12, Coralie)

> "'I'm not asking why once,' her mother said. 'I don't want telling.'" (16)

> "Coralie asked nothing about the gate." (17)

> "'I can't find which part of it turned.'" (17)

That Lesca cannot find which part of the sum turned the handle is honest, and chapter 12 gets it exactly ("That came out no truer, and no less"). But it is pre-stated in chapter 11 ("nothing in what came told Maylis which of them turned the handle") and restated in chapter 17 with a recap of chapter 12. Meanwhile the wife and the mother each decline to ask. Repeated, a principled blank starts to look like protection.

*Do:* keep chapter 12 entire. Cut the sentence in chapter 11. In chapter 17 cut the recap and let Maïté, the only one who asks, be the only one who is told.

**9. The ride is hard to follow at the moment it reverses. (8)**

> "At home Coralie had a late on the Wednesday and no alarm rang, and when the warmth beside Maylis in the chair stirred to go, she had kept her forearms on the steel arms and stayed."

This is the first time Maylis rides Lesca. The reader has to recover a rule stated three chapters back, apply it backwards, and place two Coralies, in one sentence that begins "At home". Chapter 6 does the same job in eleven words: "On Monday it rained, and Lesca woke in Maylis's bed."

*Do:* open each ride with whose body, whose village, who drives, in that order, in one plain sentence. Then the weather.

**10. Dropped beats and one figure. (7, 8)**

> "'Then she tells you which you ought to have had.' 'You sat when I told you.'" (8, Arlette)

> "'... she tells me which side the rule was made for.' 'That's the first order I've given in this house ...'" (8, Coralie)

Twice in chapter 8 one speaker closes a quotation and opens another with nothing between. A beat has been cut and the join left. Separately: "Four winters you've held that key and looked the other way" (7) reads as four winters of holding the key, against a keyholder who was writing about the winch in 2011 and had the key "from her father".

*Do:* restore the beats or merge the speeches. Make the four winters belong to the nets.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The oven, both times.** Coralie doing Larrère through the oven door and strapping the rib so that Maylis can laugh ("Now you can", 1), and the same impression failing on its second word (18). The first makes the marriage; the second is the cleanest turn from comic to dark in the book.
2. **Coralie and the call log.** "'Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one.'" and "'Because you'd have told me.'" (12). The whole scene, including the two sentences tried aloud.
3. **The cellar, then the telephone.** The torch finding the sole of a wader, and thirty lines later "'Who's dead?' said Jeannot Castagnet", ending "'And six. Bugger off.'" (11). It is the book's argument made as a joke.
4. **Kévin's fingers and room twelve.** "'Five,' he said. He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." Then Coralie's two fingers on the old woman's wrist, the line through the last name, and "'Forty-one.'" (15)
5. **What happens after the sentence.** "'Madame Castagnet will lay the wreath'", the cheek turned away before it is asked for, and Marie-Jo: "'Somebody has to stack them. ... You can't do it in that.'" (16)
