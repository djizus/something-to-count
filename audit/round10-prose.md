# Critic's report: Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `manuscript/18.md`, in order, in full. About 44,900 words in 90 sections. Counts below are mine, taken from the text.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." (2) The sum is exact and stays exact. It is first given as a recital at a committee table, which is the coolest place it could be given.
- **Tension: 6.** "The dyke had six minutes, and on foot she could reach one of the two." (15) The prose does not run against a clock until chapter 13. Before that, most sections open on a date and close on a quip, and each closes its own tension.
- **Progression: 6.** "On Monday it rained, and Lesca woke in Maylis's bed." (6) and "On Saturday it rained, and Lesca woke in Maylis's bed." (9). The book marks progress by the calendar. The same sentence opens two chapters.
- **Comedy: 8.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade.'" (7) The jokes are made of procedure and objects, and they are level with the comparison at its best.
- **Characters: 7.** "'I've held hands for a living since I was nineteen. I know when one's holding and I know when one wants to be let go.'" (5) Coralie is alive in every line. Five older women speak in one voice (fault 4).
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the neck. 'The—'" (18) The joke from chapter 1 fails to arrive, and the failure says what the flood cost.
- **Prose: 7.** "She listened to all of it." (11) The sentences are better made than Crouch's. They do less for a reader, because a good number of them have to be read twice and a handful of constructions carry the whole book.
- **The ending: 8.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (18) It costs a death, a job and a marriage, and the last sentence is the right one.
- **Clarity: 4.** "She had taken Jeannot Castagnet, alive, to mean a village that the flood had passed by." (2) This is the sentence that tells the reader what the book is about.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. From chapter 11 to the end of 15 I did not stop. In chapters 2 and 4 I went back over paragraphs to find out what had happened.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who reads slowly, yes. On the reader the bar names, no. They would put it down in chapter 2.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below the bar, for prose.** Three things put it there.

1. **Below Crouch on transparency.** A Crouch reader never has to work out what has happened. The premise is said in plain words early, the rules are said in plain words once, and a chapter ends on a new fact. Here the premise arrives at the end of chapter 2 as a chain of inferences ("It was 2026 in that village as well. But the rain that had drowned a man on its nineteenth day had begun there that morning."). The first statement of what Lesca wants is hidden behind a punctuation mark ("She made the full stop into a comma, and went on in the same ink to the end of the line, with a bed in it, and a name.", 4). The book keeps its facts back in the same way it keeps its feelings back, and only the second is a virtue.
2. **Level with The Good Place on the running joke that turns.** "Either" goes from eggs (1) to a gate (13) to "Both" (15) to "That was Either, with the river in it" (18). "Here" goes from the loto to a flooded yard to the prefect's speech. "I'll be brief" ends at a garden gate with nothing to follow it. The series reverses its jokes in the same way, and this book does it as well.
3. **Below The Good Place on voice, above Crouch on sentences.** In the series you can cover the name and tell the speaker from one line. Here that is true of Josiane, Père Honoré, Sarthou, Larrère and Daguerre, and false of Arlette, Maïté, Marie-Jo, Madame Etcheto and Madame Darrigrand. Against Crouch, the object-work is far better: a shopping pad, a kitchen timer and a car window do the work his prose gives to italics.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. Plot facts are withheld by ellipsis (2, 4, 5, and throughout)

> "She had taken Jeannot Castagnet, alive, to mean a village that the flood had passed by. It was 2026 in that village as well. But the rain that had drowned a man on its nineteenth day had begun there that morning." (2)

The narrator's habit is to name the edge of a thing and leave the thing out. That habit is right for a feeling and wrong for a fact the plot stands on. The reader has to assemble the premise from "the other lamp", "forty letters" and "dry tiles". More of the same:

- "At that table she spoke to abstain." (2)
- "It was lost, as it had been each time before." (2). The subject is a motion made eighteen lines earlier.
- "Her hand went up shut. She had kept it so since she woke, and she opened it to be counted." (4)
- "The fault was mended, and the motion stood as she had written it at her desk." (4)
- "Lesca declined that. Maylis saw how it was done. She did the same herself, with a shorter word." (5)
- "No keys lay by the bowl. In this village the home was not hers to run." (6)
- "'What did you say to her?' he said. 'To whom?'" (6). The reader cannot know who "her" is.
- "He did not count to twelve." (15)
- "'For the blessing of the dyke I am promised six thousand,' he told the table. 'I have prepared for nine.'" (8). Six thousand of what?

**What I would do.** Say three things in plain words, once each. In chapter 2: there are two villages, they split at the lamp in June 2014, hers had the flood and the other is getting it now on the same dates. Before the first crossing in chapter 4: who wakes first carries the other. In chapter 4: what the motion says, when she writes it, and not six sections later. Then go through every chapter and sort the ellipses into two piles. Keep the ones that hide a feeling. Fill in the ones that hide a fact.

### 2. Two women share one name, and the speakers break the narrator's rule (7, 8, 14, 16, 18)

> "'That's not like you, Lesca,' he said." (7, Jeannot to Maylis)
> "'You'll not get that down two metres with one arm, Lesca.'" (18, the last line of dialogue in the book)

The narrator calls one woman Maylis and the other Lesca. Jeannot calls Maylis "Lesca". Arlette and Marie-Jo call Lesca "Maylis". The final spoken line of the novel addresses the wrong woman by the narrator's own convention. One word in every 83 is one of the two names (272 "Maylis", 266 "Lesca"). In the ridden chapters the narrator falls back on possessives, 36 of them:

> "Lesca dropped the telephone in Maylis's lap and put a foot on the clutch. Then Lesca reached across with the left hand and turned the key." (14)
> "At three Lesca stood Maylis's body on the dyke." (9)

**What I would do.** Take "Lesca" out of every mouth that is speaking to Maylis. Jeannot has "little Lesca" and can keep the adjective or use her first name. Keep the two places where the confusion is the point ("'Sit down, Maylis,' her mother said", 8, and Marie-Jo's "'Maylis,'", 16). In the ridden chapters use the device the book already has, the body part with the definite article ("The right hand slid him his water", 9), and cut "Maylis's body", "Maylis's legs" and "Maylis's foot".

### 3. The hinge of the book is written as an argument (14)

> "For as long as she did, nobody drove, since a van does not go down a track with one woman at the wheel and another at the gear lever. A man sat under a hatch at the end of that track. He would be left a second time, and it would take both of them to do it."

This is the moment Lesca gives up what she came for. The prose stops to explain the mechanics, then explains the price ("She said them over, as the cost of a motion is read out before the vote. Not one of them would drive a van."). The paragraph before it tells the reader what Maylis intends from inside Lesca's head ("Maylis meant to turn the handle"), and the next chapter has Maylis do the opposite. "Lesca" appears twenty times in 1,486 words. I had to rebuild who wanted the gate and who wanted the track.

**What I would do.** Rewrite the van scene as actions only: a hand on the key at the belt, a hand on the gear lever, the stall, the horn, the rings counted. Let "Go to the tonne first" carry the decision. Keep "She had come for rain on the tiles, and for a bed with the wall side slept in, and for a man at a counter who owed her for diesel." Cut the two sentences that reason about vans.

### 4. The narrator and half the cast share one sentence (throughout)

> "'I'm not asking why once,' her mother said. 'I don't want telling.'" (16)
> "'I wasn't giving,' Maïté said. 'I was telling you whose kitchen it was.'" (17)
> "'Don't give me the measurement. I could. I'm not going to.'" (18, Coralie)
> "'I'm saying it to you. I'd sooner you had it from me than from the café.'" (18, Madame Darrigrand)

Cover the names and these are one woman: clipped, corrective, a grievance stated as bookkeeping. The shared marks are countable. "That" in place of "the" before a noun ("that gate", "that man", "that mairie", "that winch") occurs about 160 times and belongs equally to Larrère, Jeannot, Coralie, Lesca and the narrator ("Lesca knew that reach", "She knew that car"). "I'll not" and its kin (13) belong to Jeannot, Arlette, Madame Etcheto, Larrère and Lesca. "The one" for "a single" or "the same" occurs 38 times. The correction in two beats ("It isn't a statement. It's a list", "It has no date, so it isn't a letter. It's a sheet of paper", "I do not complain. I mention it", "The office has no interest. It has a duty", "I'm not calling. I'm her mother") is spoken by seven different people.

**What I would do.** The book already knows how to do this. Josiane talks in one unpunctuated run ("she's stared at him since the first number to see does he know, and he's breathing because nobody's told him"). Père Honoré never contracts. Give Arlette and Maïté each a sentence length and a habit that nobody else has, and take the two-beat correction away from everyone except Larrère and Lesca, whose trade it is.

### 5. The narrator explains what the scene has shown, and steps into heads it has no door to (5, 8, 10, 11, 15, 17, 18)

> "She was a wife in a housecoat, with her husband alive at the café counter. Maylis looked at her as people look at a woman at a graveside all the same." (5)
> "They had said what there was indoors, and out here they kept to the gutter." (17)
> "He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." (15). This spends Kévin's "I wanted six, Maylis" three thousand words early.
> "If she went back she turned the handle on him, as Lesca had. If she went on, the river had the Port." (15)
> "He had come out of a dry cellar half an hour before, by the neck, and had been told that the gate was to be opened on him." (15). A recap of the previous page.
> "She went back to the landing of the Saturday before, and the woman in Maylis's coat who had asked her across and been sent down the stairs." (18). A recap put inside Coralie's speech.

And from inside the wrong head, in chapters held to one woman's view:

> "He had telephoned an order that night from somewhere, and in twelve years nobody had asked him where." (10). Lesca does not know this. The narrator is nudging, and the ticking kitchen clock in chapter 11 does the same work better.
> "He did not hear himself say it." (10)
> "They braced for it. Not one of them had got to the end of a request since 2015." (8)
> "He did not seem to know what came next." (8). The sentence before it already shows this.

The book explains feelings it has dramatised and withholds facts it has not. That is the wrong way round.

**What I would do.** Cut every line above. Spend the words on fault 1.

### 6. Every scene has the same frame: a time stamp at the top and a button at the bottom

52 of the 90 sections open on a day or an hour. Chapter 6 runs "On Monday", "At eight", "at nine", "At noon", "At seven". Chapter 7 runs "At nine", "At four", "At five". 30 of the 90 close on a line of fourteen words or fewer, most of them a callback in dialogue: "'It's getting long,' Jeannot said." (3), "*Nil.*" (10), "'It's good soup,' Maylis said." (8), "'How's that spelled?' said Lafitte." (9), "'He's not left one before,' he said." (7). Each button is good. Thirty of them teach the reader that a scene is over when somebody is dry about it, and the reader stops being afraid in the last five lines of any scene.

**What I would do.** Keep the stamps where the date is the plot (the twenty-fourth, the eighth, the eleventh, and all of chapter 15). Elsewhere open on the event. Let one section in three end open, on an action that is not finished, as chapter 7 does ("She knew that car.") and chapter 13 does ("The telephone began to ring in her hand.").

### 7. The counters are worn smooth by the second half

The book runs on fixed tokens. Counted: "forty houses" 18, "forty-two" 21, "twelve years" 21, "eleven years" 14, "each year" or "each December" 19, "two fingers" 16, "nine minutes to three" or "02:51" 16, "rang once" and its forms 11, "a hand of water" 11, "ten minutes" 10, "Either" 24, "telephone" 76, "shut" 70. Most are the spine and must stay. The fault is where a token stands in for a new sentence:

> "'A gate is a door in a bank. You don't open it because the house is full.'" (13). Its fourth outing, at the point where Larrère should be newly frightening.
> "It was not her hour that it came at. She had never had one." … "Nothing sat there." … "and nothing sat on it." (11, 12)
> Chapter 2 says "twelve years" four times in 1,600 words.

**What I would do.** Cut "twelve years", "eleven years" and "each year" by half. Give Larrère a new sentence in chapter 13. Leave "Either", "Here" and the two fingers alone.

### 8. One mould for every comparison

> "as a dog gets up" (1), "as at a sum that had come out" (3), "as she priced a job" (5), "as a man votes" (10), "as things do that are filed" (11), "as she would have tried a dyke" (8), "as the cost of a motion is read out before the vote" (14), "as a child is carried" (15), "as a stage is" (15), "as he would have carried a sack of maize to a hide" (15), "as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another" (15), "as the warmth goes out of a chair" (15), "as it goes out of a bench when the person beside you stands" (4)

The figure is always "as" plus a general clause, and the source is always the character's trade. The same mould holds the valuations: "She put it at thirty-five kilos" (1), "She put what was left in it at ten minutes" (11, 15), "priced" five times, "in the voice she used for reading a dose off a chart" (5) and "as she would have read it off a chart" (12). By chapter 15 the reader hears the mould before the image.

**What I would do.** Keep the ones a scene turns on: the dog, the bench, the sack of maize. State the rest plainly or drop them.

### 9. Default verbs, default shapes, and the negative kicker

148 sentences begin "She had", "He had", "Lesca had" or "Maylis had". Knowledge is always a possession: "She had the date." "Lesca had the rest." "Lesca had answers." "She had it whole." "She had it to the minute." "She had the sum and nothing else." "Gave" appears 58 times for speaking, telling, reading and allowing. 67 sentences begin "It was" and 71 begin "Then". A trailing "with" phrase appears 173 times, often stacked:

> "She said it standing, in her tunic, with the coffee pot in her hand, in the voice she used for reading a dose off a chart." (5)

Emphasis comes by negation. "Nobody" occurs 64 times, and sections turn on what did not happen: "and she had not told it to", "and she had not said it" (3), "and she had not sent them" (5), "Nobody came up the steps. Nobody shouted." (16), "Nothing came." (17), "No sheet had come down from the mairie, and no car had stopped to tell her how her father dug." (18).

**What I would do.** Go through for "had" as a verb of knowing and replace two in three with the verb that is meant. Allow one stack of adjuncts to a page. Where a paragraph ends on what was absent, try ending it on what was there.

### 10. Copy and consistency

- **Two dropped beats in chapter 8.** A speech closes and another opens with nothing between: "…which you ought to have had.' 'You sat when I told you." and "…which side the rule was made for.' 'That's the first order I've given…".
- **"Four winters you've held that key"** (7) against "It's had grease since 2013" and "In 2011. I wrote twice more" (3), "mended that dyke each spring for six years" by 2014 (00), and "it had not been off it since she had it from her father" (15). If four is the winters of netting, say so, as Kévin does in 18.
- **Eleven and twelve.** Maïté says "I've stood here with you for eleven years" (8) and "twelve Decembers" (17). Coralie says "for eleven years" and "for twelve years" in one speech (12). Set one rule: twelve years since the flood, eleven commemorations, eleven years in office and on the landing.
- **Dyke and bank.** "She had mended that dyke each spring for six years. She gave it ten minutes." (00) becomes "She had patched that bank each spring for six years. She put what was left in it at ten minutes." (11). "Bank" means the dyke only in chapter 11. Choose one word.
- **Order of the night.** In 00 she takes the handle out, holds it, then turns the van. In 11 "Before she touched the winch she turned the van."
- **"I've sat by that bed three times with the telephone"** (12). Lesca has been gone four times: the Saturday, the Monday, the Saturday of the loto and Tuesday the eighth.
- **"She knew the voice before it had finished her name."** (15) He does not say her name.
- **"were in the Port by nine"** (16). They live on the terrace and the square is in the village.
- **Bread.** The café sells bread "on Sundays from a crate" (5). Lesca goes in "for bread" on a Monday (6).
- **Clock convention in 15.** "The screen said 02:55." and bare "02:59." are two ways of doing one thing in one chapter. Choose the bare line.
- **Leaf.** Inguma has "brown leaves lapped one over another" (1), then "the brown leaf" as a mass, then "its leaves against the granite" (8). Choose.
- Spelling is otherwise consistent and British throughout (towards, grey, leaned, learned, headlamps, round, judgement). "Telephone" is held for a mobile in 2026 in all but Kévin's speech. It is a choice, and it is kept.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The oven door, both times.** "'In my tray,' she said, and shut the door on him." (1) and "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the neck. 'The—'" (18).
2. **Jeannot's six points down the telephone, and the line after them.** "'And six. Bugger off.'" / "She listened to all of it." (11)
3. **The old wing.** From "Nine was open." to "where there was a hatbox, and nothing else." (15), including "Then she took the pencil on its cord and put a line through the last name on the list."
4. **The sentence leaving the square.** "The loudspeaker on the dyke road said drowned him when the square had finished hearing it." and "She watched her sentence go back through the square and the road behind it at a walk, row by row." (16)
5. **Every "Here".** Above all "'Here,' said Monsieur Daguerre, to the roof of the van." and "'I know you are.'" (15)
