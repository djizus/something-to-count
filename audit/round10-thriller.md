# Critic's report: Thriller

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

Verdict against the bar: **7. Below it by one.** From the last line of 13 to the last line of 15 the book is at the bar. For the eight chapters before that it is a comedy with a date in it.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** Countable and concrete, but the largest one (a woman losing her body to her better self) is never made to pay. "'Behind it are forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds.' She turned to Lafitte, who slept under that dyke. 'And out on the meadows there is one man.'" (6)
- **Tension: 6.** Three chapters at 9, eight at 5. This is how the chapter that ends one day before the flood ends: "It was the sheet with Jean Castagnet in it. She did not lie down." (10)
- **Progression: 7.** The gauge is the best device in the book and it is used one line at a time. "'Ninety under. That's the third of December in my file.'" (8)
- **Comedy: 7.** Dry, built, and in character; it makes me smile more often than laugh. "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade.'" (7)
- **Characters: 8.** Coralie is as good as anyone in the comparisons. "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'" (9)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Five,' he said. He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." (15)
- **Prose: 7.** Exact and its own, with one construction worn out by chapter 5: "Her left hand came across and closed over Coralie's fingers, and she had not told it to." (3; again as "and she had not said it", "and she had not sent them", "and she had not sat there long enough to warm them")
- **The ending: 8.** It costs more than any Crouch ending does. "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (18)
- **Clarity: 5.** The best reversal in the book has to be decoded: "Maylis meant to turn the handle. Coralie was under that dyke. Maylis's right hand moved first. It went across to the belt, where the key of the handle box hung, and shut on that." (14)

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. I would have stopped for a day somewhere in 8 to 10 and read 11 to 15 in one sitting.

**Would I press it on a friend?** Not on the friend the bar describes. That reader stops in chapter 1 or chapter 10.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch, for three reasons.**

1. **Nobody is in danger for twelve chapters.** A Crouch chapter has a body at risk in it. Here, between the prologue and the dusk of chapter 13, the worst that happens to anyone is a cracked rib, a weal and an undated letter. The threat is a date, and the date is not even printed: chapters open "On Wednesday", "On Saturday", and the reader does the subtraction.
2. **The double is not dangerous.** Dark Matter runs on a man who has taken your wife and will kill you to keep her. Lesca announces the same intention ("take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it") and is sent downstairs by Coralie at the midpoint. After that the only thing she can take is two minutes.
3. **The premise never gets worse.** Crouch turns his machine over every fifty pages: the box has more doors, the chair kills timelines, the town is not when you thought. Here chapter 2 tells you there are two villages, chapter 5 gives the three rules, and that is all the reader ever learns. The one question the mechanism raises is asked in the last chapter and answered "I don't know."

**Level with Crouch** on speed of premise (two worlds and a deadline by the end of chapter 2) and on chapter endings in the first third (00, 02, 03, 04 and 08 are as good as his).

**Above Crouch** on cost and on the marriage. He gives the family back. This book ends with a pillow in a car, a dated resignation and a grave dug by the woman blamed for it. The fear for the marriage is also a better fear than his: the rival is preferable, and the wife says so ("I'd have this one again").

**Against The Good Place, on reversals: level in the small, below in the large.** Three of them re-read what came before: Coralie has had the call log for twelve years; Larrère gave the order from the dead man's kitchen; the lesson Maylis borrowed was wrong for her world, because with the gate shut the man was dry. None of them turns the premise itself over.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The fight for the body is the thriller, and it never comes to a head. (8, 9, 12, 14)**
"'It's a life in one hand. […] I'd sign where you put the pen. At night, when you were asleep, I could shut it.'" That is the most frightening thing in the book, and it never happens. Every ride ends when Inguma has finished counting, so the reader always knows Maylis gets home by dusk. The one way to stay is announced in a line ("'I mean to sit on it,' Lesca said"), tried and given up on the same page: "She let go. In the steel room she got up off Inguma's back." Eight rings.
*What I would do:* let Lesca stay once before the eleventh. Move the sitting-on-Inguma to the Tuesday ride. Dusk comes, the counting is done, and nothing fetches her. Maylis spends a night and a day in one hand with no end in sight, and gets back only because somebody on the other side says the words. Then the eight rings in the van are a choice between a life and a kilometre, and the reader has already seen that she can keep it.

**2. The fatal choice is cut into four, and the one that mattered is the quietest. (13, 15)**
At five, in daylight, with the man standing beside her and the handle on the shaft, she can have both for the price of her job: "'Either,' Maylis said. He waited for the rest of it, and there was none. 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said." Twelve lines. The reader is told it was the point of no return five chapters later, by a woman with a pram: "'You had it on your belt then.'" At the dip the real trolley choice gets one sentence: "She got out, and went on." And the death is then shared among Lesca's two minutes, the word "both", the van, the winch, a door that would not open and an old woman who would not sleep in a chair.
*What I would do:* make five o'clock the scene. Let her price it as she prices everything: the letter, the pension, three hundred hectares on a figure Pau puts lower, the man's fortnight of water. Let her take it to the second click. Then the word. At the dip, give her one look back at the light of the home before the sentence. Reduce the causes of the death to the two that are choices.

**3. Lesca's own village has no clock until chapter 12. (2, 4, 8, 10, 12)**
Half the book is set where the flood is twelve years over. What can be lost there is a speech, and the reader is not told the speech is a choice until "'Read that line on Saturday or take it out, Madame le maire.'" Before that the stake offered is this: "the council of Peyrebarthe sat in extraordinary session on a single item, which was whether the mayor should open her mouth on Saturday." In chapter 8, nine days out, the woman in the mayor's body spends her day letting a man talk for eleven minutes. It is the funniest scene in the book and the river is not in it.
*What I would do:* bring Coralie's call log forward to chapter 4 or 8, so that Saturday's sentence hangs over five chapters and not one. Cut the chapter 10 council sitting to its result. Print the date and the gauge reading at the head of every chapter in both villages.

**4. Chapter 14 cannot be read at speed, and what Lesca risks in it has no price. (3, 14, 16)**
In six sentences the two women swap sides: Maylis reaches for the key that drowns him, Lesca drives for the track. It is the turn of the whole book and I had to read it twice to see whose hand was where. Then Lesca stands in the hall of the dead, a place the reader has been given as a proverb ("'The one it cannot fetch is found in her bed, and plays loto with the rest'"), declines her seat ("'The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies'") and is fetched anyway. If declining works, there was no danger. The next morning: "'Bayonne has had you three hours and found nothing.'"
*What I would do:* double the length of the van. Say in plain words, once, which woman wants which. State as a fourth rule in chapter 5 what happens to the one who will not be fetched, show it happen to somebody before 14, and let Bayonne find something.

**5. The premise never turns over, and the book says so. (1, 5, 18)**
"'I don't know either.' They left it there." The rain is the engine of every chapter and it is given no cause. Lesca's own theory ("I brought my weather with me") is the frightening one, and Maylis disproves it on her fingers in the scene where it is offered. The university "counted one of her, paid her, given her a sandwich and gone back", and never returns.
*What I would do:* commit to the frightening answer. It sat on Maylis on the Monday night and it rained on the Tuesday: let the meeting be what brings the weather, and let them know it by chapter 10, so that every night in the chair has a cost. Failing that, do not ask the question again in 18.

**6. The winch pays off in a report. (1, 3, 6, 9, 15)**
"Nineteen" is planted in five chapters. The payoff: "'It's stopped,' he said. 'Your winch. I've three things to say about it.'" It is the right joke with the camera in the wrong place. What is in the slot ("'In seventy-one it was a ewe'") is never said, though a net is hauled "up out of the throat of the sluice" two chapters earlier.
*What I would do:* as she runs the crown, let her hear the pawl behind her over the water, click by click, and then not hear the nineteenth. Forty words. And decide what is in the slot. If it is the nets she spent four winters not looking at, the book has its Good Place reversal for nothing.

**7. Larrère is never made to answer. (15, 17, 18)**
He gave the order from the dead man's kitchen in both villages and extorted a resignation in one. His exits: "'I'll be brief,' he said. […] Nothing came", and a walk back to a car. The evidence is on the page ("Under the time it said Castagnet, house") and is then put in an envelope: "'It says who had rung her, and from whose kitchen.'" Nobody reads it out.
*What I would do:* one line. When Sarthou says "'There is a file. It has a name in it'", have him say there are two.

**8. Three chapters of aftermath, with the bill presented as dialogue. (16, 17, 18)**
After "Forty-one" the reader wants Maylis's morning and gets two Lesca chapters that run to the following Tuesday; 18 then steps back to Sunday. What the eight rings cost is held for three chapters and delivered as arithmetic: "'Those two minutes are your two hundred metres, and they're mine.'" Chapter 17 is six short scenes of settling up ("On Monday at nine Lesca laid a telephone on a desk at Bayonne").
*What I would do:* cut the Bayonne statement and the Tuesday vote to a paragraph each. Let Lesca count the two minutes herself in 14, as she lets go, so the reader carries the sum into the flood.

**9. Chapter 1 puts a postman in front of the hook. (1)**
After a ringing telephone and a thing on a wardrobe: "'I've got a registered letter for the mayor,' the postman said into the gap. 'They told me the mairie. Which is that?'" Three pages of square before the reader has a reason to care who signs. The clearest statement of the premise comes last: "'They shut you in a box once, to see whether there were two of you. Would the other one know what she wants for her supper?'"
*What I would do:* cut the square by a third and put Coralie's line in the first two pages.

**10. The night is told before it is revealed. (00, 11)**
Prologue: "She laid her hand flat on the dyke beside the water, and the clay moved under her palm." Chapter 11: "put her hand flat on the bank beside it, and the clay moved under her palm." The centrepiece reveal opens by repeating a page the reader has, and the prologue has already told them that she looked down the track and rang. What chapter 11 adds (the reflector, the call ended after one ring, the four minutes) arrives inside a repetition.
*What I would do:* keep the prologue whole. Start chapter 11's account at the telephone by the bed and cut from the platform straight to the headlamps finding the reflector. Keep the echo for chapter 15, where it is doing work.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **13's last line through 15's last line.** The clock times, "Under the time it said Castagnet, house", Daguerre's "Here" / "I know you are", Kévin's five fingers, the hatbox on the wardrobe, "Forty-one."
2. **The dry cellar.** "'you were a kilometre off, fetching a man out of a dry hole.'" The borrowed lesson was wrong for this world. Do not soften it and do not let anybody forgive it.
3. **The call log.** "'Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one.'" And what it does to twelve years of a landing.
4. **The reflector.** "The reflector burned in the lamps at the end of the track for four minutes more. Then the water came up the door to it and put it out."
5. **The last two pages.** Eleven stairs, "the car door shut on a pillow", letter 42 that "doesn't thank you", and the second shovel.
