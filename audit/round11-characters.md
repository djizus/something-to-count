# Round 11: Characters

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** The handover lists a key, a van, a mayor, and then: "*Thirty metres from the patch.*" Coralie as a distance is the stake in four words. But neither woman risks more than a bed and a job, and the one life spent is the oldest in the book.
- **Tension: 7.** "If she went back she turned the handle on him, as Lesca had. If she went on, the river had the Port." Chapters 11 to 15 are a 9. Chapters 5 to 10 are a 5.
- **Progression: 6.** "On Monday it rained, and Lesca woke in Maylis's bed." (6) "On Saturday it rained, and Lesca woke in Maylis's bed." (9) The same day is run three times: Lesca rides, speaks as mayor, the café misreads, Maylis pays.
- **Comedy: 8.** "'Opened what? There's a name over it. It reads that she opened him.'"
- **Characters: 7.** Coralie is a 9, the village an 8, Lesca an 8, Maylis a 6, and the two women as a pair a 5. "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'"
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** Coralie starts the oven voice the morning after: "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the neck. 'The—' She stayed bent for a moment, with her hand on the door."
- **Prose: 7.** "The tall L came out of the wrong hand like a fence post set by a boy." It never lets me inside anybody, and at three points that costs the book its centre.
- **The ending: 7.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." The last page is a 9. The steel-room verdict that opens the chapter is a 5.
- **Clarity: 5.** "The one it cannot fetch back is found in her bed, and plays loto with the rest." That is the rule that tells me a woman can die of this, and it is a riddle.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. From the last line of 4 I was not going to stop.

**Press it on a friend?** On one friend, who likes village comedy. Not on the Crouch reader. He would ask me which of the two women the book is about, and at present the honest answer is Lesca, while the climax is given to Maylis.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Against Crouch: above on two things, below on the one that matters most to him.**

- Above: the wife is a person. Daniela gets her say in the last act of Dark Matter. Coralie has a want neither woman supplies from chapter 5, when she lifts an envelope from behind the fruit bowl, and she says so: "'I posted it this afternoon, and I posted it for me.'" Both Coralies refuse to be the prize. One sends Lesca down the stairs; the other makes her wait for a vote.
- Above: Crouch has no village. Here a dozen people can be told apart by one line each. "'Here,' said Monsieur Daguerre." "'I cannot file a forecast.'" "'For the blessing of the dyke I am promised six thousand. I have prepared for nine.'"
- Below: fear for the marriage. Crouch puts me inside a man who aches for his wife on every page. This book tells me what Coralie has waited for and what Lesca has lost, and tells me nothing of what Maylis feels for the woman beside her except that she has never had to ask. "The wall side of that bed was the one thing in the house she had never had to ask for." I was not afraid for that marriage. I was afraid for Coralie in it.

**Against The Good Place: below.**

- Level: each is wrong in her own way. Maylis's "Either", Lesca's sum, Larrère's minutes, and Coralie's twelve years of not asking: "'Because you'd have told me.'" The running joke that turns lethal ("'Either,' Maylis said." "'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said.") is exactly their method.
- Level: the reversals re-read what came before. The telephone log re-reads the landing. The kitchen clock re-reads Larrère.
- Below: that show is carried by people who change each other by arguing, on screen, at length. Maylis and Lesca meet five times, for a page or two, and four of those meetings end with Lesca saying nothing and the creature walking in. The relationship the premise promises is the thinnest in the book.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The central choice is Lesca's, carried out by Maylis, and the book does not say so. (14, 15, 18)**

Chapter 14 shows it: "Maylis meant to turn the handle. Coralie was under that dyke. Maylis's right hand moved first." She knocks the van out of gear to stop Lesca driving to the tonne. Then Lesca leaves her five words, "'Go to the tonne first,'" and Maylis does it: "Now the one woman who had ever opened that gate on him had taken her mouth to tell her not to. Not first."

So the commune's girl did what she was told, one more time, by a mayor. That is the darkest and best thing the book has, and chapter 18 buries it. Maylis says "'The van in the dip was mine. So was saying both.'" Lesca owns the eight rings and not the five words, then delivers the verdict: "'A woman is dead of that, and it still isn't what I did. Don't hand me your sum to sit behind.'" The narrator closes it: "Maylis let that be."

Lesca spent Maylis's night on her own ghost. Madame Etcheto and Maylis's marriage paid for it. Lesca is excused here, and Maylis is denied her own act.

Do this: in the last steel-room scene Maylis says what her hand did and who sent her down the track. Lesca has to own the five words as she owns the rings. Maylis refuses the absolution. Half a page, and the book becomes about both of them.

**2. Maylis is not wanted-for in her own life. (1 to 9, 11)**

Lesca's want is the best sentence in the book: "*That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*" Maylis's want arrives in chapter 8 ("'I want that,'" of a telephone answered at the second ring), is stated once in 10, and is dropped in a subordinate clause: "Maylis had meant to lend that woman her arms for the night, and take a sash for it."

Of her own house the book gives me only that she prefers being ordered: "She had not had so easy a meal since she left her mother's." That is a true and ugly note. It is not enough to carry chapters 13, 15 and 18, which are hers.

Do this: before chapter 5, one scene from inside Maylis wanting Coralie and not saying so, so that I know the love is withheld and not absent. And keep the wish for the chair alive to the dusk at the gate. When she fits the handle with nobody's order, that is her being mayor for ninety seconds. Say so there, and "Either" costs double.

**3. The scene on which Coralie's leaving turns is missing. (between 9 and 10)**

Coralie shuts the box-room door on Lesca with "'Then she can say so in the morning.'" The book then skips Sunday entirely and opens 10 on Monday night. Nine chapters later it is reported: "'I know you couldn't. I had two fingers on you. You had it back by breakfast.'"

That breakfast is the one time Maylis has her own mouth, knows Coralie would take the other woman tomorrow, and says nothing. I never saw it. The departure in 18 rests on it.

Do this: write the Sunday breakfast. It is short. The pad is on the table.

**4. Coralie leaves for the wrong stated reason, and I cannot tell whether Maylis is withholding the right one. (18)**

"'The gate, or Castagnet. You said both. I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it.'" The narrator agrees: "Maylis could not have put it shorter than Coralie had."

It is a fine line and it is not what happened. Maylis did choose: at the dip she "got out, and went on", away from the building Coralie was in. And before that her hand had gone to the key, for Coralie, and been overruled. The other Coralie has lived twelve years afraid the gate was opened for her. This Coralie learns it was not. That mirror is the richest thing in the two marriages and nobody says it.

Maylis has a true answer available and the page gives no sign that she has it and swallows it. I could not tell silence from oversight.

Do this: let Coralie say the harder thing (you walked away from the building I was in). Let Maylis be seen to hold back that her hand went to the key. One line each.

**5. The two women never have it out. (5, 8, 10)**

Every hard question in the steel room is met by narration closing the door:

- "Lesca gave no answer." (5)
- "Lesca had nothing to put against it." (8)
- "Lesca had a minute's speech for forty houses. She had none for this, and gave none." (10)
- "Lesca did not answer." (10)

Then the creature gets into the chair and the scene is over, four times running.

Chapter 10 is the worst case. Maylis has just been made to stand in her own body while another woman asked her partner to bed with her mouth. She gets one good speech ("'At night, when you were asleep, I could shut it'") and then proposes a swap. There is no anger in it.

Do this: let chapter 10's scene run. Lesca answers, at length, and is wrong. Maylis is angry before she is reasonable.

**6. The dead woman has nobody to speak for her to Maylis. (18)**

Jean Castagnet has a widow who faces Lesca for twelve Decembers. Madame Etcheto has "'Her daughter is having her buried here on Tuesday,'" offstage. Maylis's cost reaches her through a café joke and through Madame Darrigrand, whose baby lived.

The book has also chosen the easiest death it had: ninety-four, in her own bed by her own wish, on nightly terms with the thing that fetches. Coralie even takes part of it ("'I walked her back to it on my arm'"). That softens Maylis.

Do this: the daughter at the cemetery gate, in place of Père Honoré or beside him, for six lines. She need not accuse.

**7. The contest for the body in chapter 14 has to be diagrammed, so the reader misses fault 1. (14)**

"Lesca dropped the telephone in Maylis's lap and put a foot on the clutch. Then Lesca reached across with the left hand and turned the key, and the engine caught." Then: "Maylis's right hand came off the belt and knocked the lever out of gear."

This is the one physical fight between the two women, and it is the moment that shows what each of them wants first. I had to read it three times to work out that Maylis wanted the gate and Lesca the track.

Do this: say once, plainly, before the engine starts, which of them wants which. "Maylis wanted the gate. Lesca wanted the man." Then run the hands.

**8. Three relationships pay off on quarrels the reader is never told. (2, 4, 16)**

- Marie-Jo: "until the night she handed her sash back across the council table in a carrier bag". What about?
- Kévin: "in the coat you gave him when he came out of prison". For what, and on whose report?
- Coralie: "after a week of shouting". About what, if she never asked?

If Lesca had Marie-Jo's son sent down and then gave him her coat and her job, that is the most telling fact about Lesca in the book: she does the thing by rule and pays for it privately, as with Maïté's roof. Marie-Jo's "'Maylis,'" on the steps in 16 would land twice as hard.

Do this: one sentence each. The carrier bag first.

**9. Larrère in Maylis's village is a mechanism, and the plot makes him act against his own nature. (7, 13, 15, 17)**

The mayor's Larrère gets the eleven minutes, the unfinished question and the garden gate. The keyholder's Larrère gets a window gap and a folder. He is the man who says "'I can't see that you'd remember a telephone'" and "'The association is a body,'" and at 02:53 he rings from his mistress's landline so that the screen reads "Castagnet, house".

His guilt in 2014 is also blurred. His order was shut, which kept Jean dry. Maïté's "'You said it made no odds where he was'" is the only line that says what he did wrong, and it comes at the garden gate, two chapters after the confession.

Do this: a clause for why the careful man uses that telephone. And move Maïté's line, or a version of it, up into the kitchen scene so that I know his fault before he is silent at the gate.

**10. Lesca and Jeannot share a signature. (5, 6, 9, 11, 15)**

Lesca: "'I make it three points. One.'" Jeannot: "'One. I've a hand of water, and I waited for it. Two.'" and "'I've three things to say about it. First,'" and the six points down the telephone.

They are the two strongest voices in the book and they count the same way. If that is meant (she smiles at him in the café when he goes "'to the third'"), give it a clause there: she hears her own habit in a man she drowned. If it is not meant, take the numbering off Lesca. She has motions.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The telephone log (12).** "'Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one.'" and "'Because you'd have told me.'" The whole scene, including "'Don't say it now.'"
2. **Coralie's refusal on the landing (9).** "'I'd have the one who answers'" followed by "'It's her house. It's her bed.'" It is what makes her a person and not a stake.
3. **Jeannot from end to end.** The nine rings and "'And six. Bugger off,'" the letter numbered 42 that "'doesn't thank you,'" and the second shovel.
4. **"Either" at the gate at dusk (13).** "'Which is it to be? Shut, or open?'" "'Either,' Maylis said." "'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. 'That's how it was left.'" Do not add a word of her thinking here. The fix for fault 2 goes before it.
5. **Two fingers on a wrist, carried to room twelve (1 to 15).** "Coralie laid the hand back on the blanket. She closed the eyes with her thumb." And "'Forty-one.'"
