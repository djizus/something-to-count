# Critic's report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.5.** The body is the stake as well as the village, and the book says so in one line: "'It's a life in one hand. … At night, when you were asleep, I could shut it.'" (10)
- **Tension: 6.5.** It is real from chapter 13 and thin before it. "03:11. She had given it ten minutes. It had held sixteen." (15) is the level the book reaches; it takes twelve chapters to get there.
- **Progression: 6.** The plan is stated three times in nearly the same words and does not change between statements: "'by order, with the meadows cleared and nobody on them'" (5), "'in writing, and nobody has to choose anything at night'" (8), "'by day, and nobody has to choose anything in the dark'" (10).
- **Comedy: 7.** It is funny in one way, often, and never in a second way. The top of its range: "'Opened what? There's a name over it. It reads that she opened him.'" (2)
- **Characters: 8.** "'I've had five answers with one soup, and one of them was an instruction.'" (6) Coralie is as good as anyone in the comparison.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.5.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the neck. 'The—'" (18) The same joke, seventeen chapters on, and she cannot finish it. This is the thing the book does better than its comparisons.
- **Prose: 7.5.** "The rule applied to all members and had been needed for one." (2) It is that good and it is always that sentence.
- **The ending: 7.5.** "'It's four sides, and it doesn't thank you.'" (18) The cost is paid in full. The village that built the comedy is not in the room when it is paid.
- **Clarity: 5.** "At home Coralie had a late on the Wednesday, and no alarm rang to wake Maylis first. Lesca woke first. When the warmth beside Maylis in the chair stirred to go, Maylis had kept her forearms on the steel arms and stayed, and so the day was hers to drive." (8) A swap comedy cannot ask the reader to work out who is driving.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes.
**Pressed on a friend?** Not on the friend the bar describes. That friend finished The Good Place having laughed aloud several times an episode. This book gets about eight laughs aloud in eighteen chapters and a great many dry smiles.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below The Good Place as comedy. Level with it on how a joke is turned into a wound. Crouch does not compete on this lens.**

What puts it there:

1. **One register.** The Good Place runs four kinds of funny at once: a liar's patter, a ditherer's panic, an idiot's serenity, a snob's reflex. This book has dry understatement, delivered by the narrator after the line of dialogue, at one volume. Josiane's run-on sentences are the only broad voice in the village and she has six speeches. The last laugh of pleasure is in chapter 6 ("Coralie laughed, with her head back"). After it there are two laughs on the page: one breaks a minute of silence (8) and one is told to shut up (18). A comedy in which nobody on the page laughs reads as irony, and irony gets a smile.

2. **The premise is under-spent.** The Good Place takes its indecisive man and builds whole episodes on the indecision before it shows what the indecision costs other people. This book has the same design in "Either", and it is the best-built running joke in the manuscript: eggs (1), the demon's work (3), the winch (3), the gendarme's form (7), the deputy's diary (8), the gate (13), "'Both'" (15), "'That was Either, with the river in it'" (18). But the other half of the premise, two women in one body, each wrong in the opposite way, is played for comedy in one meeting (5), one morning (6), one day (8) and one sentence of summary (9). The book has a body-swap and rations it.

3. **Level on reversal.** The Good Place's great reversal re-reads every earlier smile. This book's method is the same on a smaller scale and it is rigorous: every comic device is planted light and cashed dark. The finger count (3 → 15). "Here" (9 → 13, "Nobody called"). "I'll be brief" (1 → 17, where nothing comes). The three-minute timer (2 → 17, where she uses under one). "Put it in forty-two" (1 → 18). The boxes on the pad (1 → 17, the form with two boxes). That is as good as the comparison. Where it falls short is that the comic reversal, as distinct from the dark one, is never staged: the café decided in chapter 6 that the keyholder was after Castagnet's wife, the reader learns the mayor was, and the café is never shown learning it.

4. **Level on making the moral problem funny without making it small.** The verb for the stone is a trolley problem conducted as a point of grammar, and it never once cheapens the dead man. Darrigrand does the same job from the other side: "'It's four when you're the one counting. When it's your house it's one, and they knock it down.'" (4) Nothing here needs changing.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The double act performs once.** Chapter 5 against chapters 8, 10 and 18.
> "'Then it's carried, or it's tied, and in a tie the chair decides.' / 'There's nobody in the chair.'" (5)
> "Lesca had a minute's speech for forty houses. She had none for this, and gave none." (10)

The two women are the premise. They face each other four times. The first meeting is a comic scene with an engine: one of them chairs a meeting of two and the other prices things. The other three are negotiation, with the engine switched off; in chapter 10 Lesca's replies are "'Yes'", "'Yes'" and silence. The Good Place is funny because its people argue in the same room. Here they are in the same room four times and argue once.
**Do:** keep the procedure running in every sitting and let it change hands. By chapter 10 Maylis has chaired a council for a day. She should open that sitting, put "I want your side" as a motion, and be ruled out of order by a woman who is standing where the chair should be. Chapter 18's sitting should be the one where neither of them reaches for a form. That makes the last meeting land harder, and it costs no plot.

**2. The swap is rationed, and its best image is summarised.** Chapters 8, 9, 10.
> "For ten minutes the bar of the loto was better run than anyone in the hall could remember." (9)

Maylis drives Lesca's body for one day in the whole book, and that day is the funniest chapter in it: "'I want that.' / 'You have it, Madame le maire … It's in the inventory.'" Lesca drives Maylis's for three days, of which one is told in summary (10). The meek woman holding power is the richer joke and it gets a third of the stage time. Then the only passage where the two work one body together, before the van, is the sentence quoted above: one mouth saying too much, one hand giving the right change, and the book tells us it went well.
**Do:** play the bar. Three customers, in turn, each getting a sentence too many from the mouth and the correct change from the hand. It is the set-up for the gear lever in chapter 14, where the same two hands kill the engine, and at present that set-up is one line. Then give Maylis a second day in the mayor's chair between chapters 8 and 10, paid for by cutting the summarised Tuesday in 10 down to the scene at the car window. "I want your side" currently rests on one day of wanting.

**3. One register, and nobody laughs.** Throughout; worst from chapter 7.
> "The association resolved to seek an estimate." (3)
> "He took it home in the commune's own bags, laid a course of them across his step, and wrote the commune a bill for his labour." (10)

Nearly every punchline is the narrator's flat sentence after the speech. By chapter 6 the reader hears it coming. The two women laugh together in chapters 1 and 3, helpless, against a wall, and those are the scenes that make the house worth losing. They never laugh together again. Coralie laughs once more, alone, in chapter 6, and from there the reader is left to find it funny without company.
**Do:** move a third of the punchlines out of the narration and into mouths. Give Josiane four times the lines. Let the café laugh at its own pad. Give Coralie and Maylis one more shared laugh after chapter 3 and before the landing in chapter 9, so that the oven door in 18 closes on something the reader has had three times, not twice.

**4. The chorus leaves before the farce is paid.** Chapter 18.
> "Somebody inside said that the commune's girl had gone for a walk. Somebody laughed, once, and was told to shut up, and it was too late for that." (18)

Josiane and Lafitte built the book's farce in chapter 6, with a pad and a heading. Larrère used it as a weapon in 13 ("'The café has it on a pad, under a heading'"). The pad is last seen in chapter 10 and Josiane last speaks there. In chapter 18 the café is "somebody", and Maïté gets on the bus past a door nobody comes out of. Josiane was right that it was about the wife and wrong about everything else, and the one comic reversal the book has earned is not staged.
**Do:** one café scene on the Sunday, short. Lafitte with the pad turned to its heading. Josiane revising the theory in one unpunctuated sentence as the bus goes by the window. The "walk" line given to a named man, and the pad turned over. It keeps the cruelty and it lets the comedy be alive in the last chapter beside the oven.

**5. Nobody can tell who is driving.** Chapters 5, 8, 10.
> "'Two. The one who wakes first takes the other home in her.'" (5)

That is the rule and it is given once, as item two of three, in a speech. Which hand the host keeps is never stated as a rule at all; it has to be inferred from "You kept your right hand". Every later swap opens with a paragraph of rota and alarm clocks to justify itself. Farce depends on the audience knowing more than the people in the room. Here the audience is behind.
**Do:** say the rule in one plain sentence the first time it matters, and say the hand rule with it. Then make the logistics the joke they already are: which village gets which woman is decided each morning by a care assistant's shift pattern. "'They've changed the rota. I'm on nights from Friday'" (10) is the most consequential line in the chapter and nobody in the book notices why.

**6. Chapter 10 throws away the prophecy.**
> "He did not hear himself say it." (10)

In chapter 6 Lesca tells Larrère word for word what he will say on the eighth. On the eighth he says it, through the window, in front of the open café. This is the turn of the whole middle, where the village's joke about the woman who thinks she is mayor becomes belief, and it is over in six lines, with the narrator telling us what the mayor failed to notice. The café is the audience for that line and it is given one entry on a pad.
**Do:** play it at length. Let him get to the end of the third sentence before the card table goes quiet. Let Lafitte read the chapter-6 entry back off the pad, aloud, while the window is still down. Then the petition.

**7. "Still in the box" is planted once and paid seventeen chapters later.** Chapters 1 and 18.
> "'Still in the box, were you?' somebody said inside, and the door shut on the laugh." (1)
> "'That's what late is now. They've retired the other.'" (18)

The village's oldest joke being replaced by a new one about her is the right last word on what the night cost. A reader has heard the old one once, seventeen chapters back, through a closing door.
**Do:** say it three more times. To Kévin at the plane trees (3). To Sarthou, late to the loto (9). Once to Lesca in Maylis's coat, who does not know what it means (6).

**8. The loto of the dead is the book's best comic idea and it is given as a riddle.** Chapters 3, 10, 14, 16.
> "'The one it cannot fetch back is found in her bed, and plays loto with the rest.'" (3)

An afterlife that is the village hall on loto night, where "Nobody at those tables was asked anything", is exactly the kind of invention the comparison is loved for, and it is heaven for a woman who cannot choose. It appears as one line from an old woman, a glimpse (10), half a page (14) and a number called off-stage (16). Because the rule arrives as a riddle, "'Twelve. One, two.'" lands as a puzzle the reader solves a chapter later, when Coralie explains it.
**Do:** put the rule in plain words in the mouth that knows it, and make the glimpse in chapter 10 a scene: Maylis walks to the empty chair and reads the card laid at it. Then the number in 16 needs no gloss in 17.

**9. Kévin's five is explained twice.** Chapters 15 and 18.
> "He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." (15)
> "'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything as much.'" (18)

The count in the water is the cleanest switch in the manuscript: a comic bit from a loader bucket becomes the moment a boy checks whether the flood is a dream. The simile tells us what it meant. Three chapters later he tells us again.
**Do:** keep chapter 15 as it stands. In 18 keep the flying, which is new and terrible ("'I didn't like it. You can see what's under you'"), and cut the hand.

**10. Punchlines that need knowledge the reader has not been given.** Chapters 2, 6, 8, 17.
> "'It takes a small m,' she said." (6)

The reader can tell a pedantic correction has been made and cannot tell what it is. "'Through the chair'" (2) is used twice before anyone could know it is a way of not speaking to a person. The firework bull is ordered in a list in chapter 8 and arrives in 17 as "The bull went up in white fire" with no line to say what a man is carrying.
**Do:** half a clause each. They are the only jokes in the book that are not self-explaining, and they stand out for it.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie at the oven door**, chapter 1 ("'The eggs. There's the question of the eggs. It isn't settled, and you're not to worry about it.'") and the same door in chapter 18, where she gets as far as "'The—'".
2. **The verb for the stone**, all of it: the committee in chapter 2, the timer ringing on Maïté's open mouth, "left" in 17, "'Drowned'" in 18.
3. **Jeannot on the telephone at five in the morning**, chapter 11: six numbered points ending "'And six. Bugger off.'", and then "She listened to all of it." It is the hardest switch in the book and the best.
4. **Larrère's eleven minutes with no bell**, chapter 8 ("He had been saving it since 2015, and there was less of it than he had thought"), and his three minutes with no bell at the garden gate in 17, where nothing comes.
5. **Madame Etcheto**, from the report on Bixente ("it had been told twice and given two lots of stars, and it got through them early and came at one") to the sandbags in 13: "'Nineteen,' Madame Etcheto said. 'That's few. It counts quickly.' / Nobody called."
