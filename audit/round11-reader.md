# Round 11: Reader

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `manuscript/18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

I read as somebody who knows nothing about sluice gates, French village government, Basque folklore or duck shooting, and who has read the Crouch novels and seen The Good Place.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** By the end of chapter 4 I knew what could be lost and by whom: "*That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*" A flood, a man, forty-two beds, a marriage and a body.
- **Tension: 7.** It is a 5 across chapters 9 and 10 and a 10 across 13 to 15. "She was two hundred metres short of the dyke when it went."
- **Progression: 6.** The same ground is walked once from each side, and the night of 2014 is told five times. Chapter 4 gives me again what chapter 3 ended on: "*left* / The t was crossed low."
- **Comedy: 7.** I laughed aloud about eight times. "'Opened what? There's a name over it. It reads that she opened him.'" The rest is a dry smile at procedure, and the procedure joke (the timer, the minutes, through the chair) is told in five chapters.
- **Characters: 8.** Coralie is the best thing in the book from her first speech: "'I don't cook Either,' she said. 'There's no recipe for it. Soup or duck.'" Maylis is the weakest of the main four until chapter 10.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** In the flooded yard, with a woman's wheelchair half lifted: "'Five,' he said. He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another."
- **Prose: 7.** Plain, exact and easy to read aloud, but it withholds by habit and I paid for that in re-reading. "When the warmth beside Maylis in the chair stirred to go, Maylis had kept her forearms on the steel arms and stayed, and so the day was hers to drive."
- **The ending: 8.** It costs a death, a marriage, a job and an office. "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it."
- **Clarity: 5.** The climax hangs on a rule given to me once, in chapter 3, as a riddle: "The one it cannot fetch back is found in her bed, and plays loto with the rest."

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. I came close to stopping twice, in chapter 4 and in chapter 10. From chapter 11 I could not have stopped.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On one friend, with a warning that the first ten chapters ask for work. Not on the friend who reads Crouch on a plane.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below the bar as a whole book. Level or above from chapter 11 to the end.** As a reader I put the whole at 7, where the bar is 8.

Three things put it there.

1. **I never lose my place in a Crouch novel, and I lost it here.** In Dark Matter the box has one rule, said in a sentence, long before the climax needs it, so that when Jason is in danger I am only afraid. Here the rule of the crossing is given in pieces across chapters 3, 5, 8, 10, 12 and 14. When Lesca sat down on Inguma in chapter 14 I was working out what that meant when I should have been frightened. The Good Place restates where everybody stands at the top of nearly every episode, as a joke. This book never restates.
2. **The marriage is above Crouch.** He makes me afraid for a marriage by threatening it from outside. This book has the same marriage twice, wrong in opposite ways, and each wife wants the other woman's fault: "I'd have the one who answers" in one house, and in the other "Do you know what I'd give for a woman at this table who sat when she was told?" I was more afraid for Maylis and Coralie than I was for Jason and Daniela, and the book let me lose them.
3. **The moral problem is as human as The Good Place makes it, but the reversal is weaker.** Monsieur Darrigrand does in two lines what the show's trolley episode does in twenty minutes: "It's four when you're the one counting." But the show's reversals make me re-read everything before them. Here the prologue and chapter 2 had already shown me that she looked down the track and rang once, so chapters 11, 12 and 16 confirmed a thing I had known for two hundred pages. Only Larrère in the Castagnet kitchen (the ticking clock in 11, "Castagnet, house" in 15, Maïté in 17) made me turn back.

### What I did not understand

- Why the woman being ridden keeps her right hand, and only that. It is the rule of half the book's best scenes and no reason is given.
- What Lesca was risking when she sat on Inguma, and why letting go put her at a table with the dead and not in her bed. I rebuilt it afterwards: she meant to be found dead at home and live on in Maylis, and a place had been laid for her. Nobody says it.
- Whose chair was empty in the hall of the dead in chapter 10, and what it meant that Maylis "looked too long" at it.
- Why it rained in one village in 2014 and in the other in 2026. The book asks this itself and answers "I don't know."
- Why Marie-Jo gave her sash back "in a carrier bag." It is mentioned three times and never told.
- Whether the seven councillors walked out on Lesca or on Larrère. Chapter 11 reads one way and chapter 16 the other.
- What "a hand of water" is. I guessed a hand's depth. It is used a dozen times and never glossed.
- What Maylis gained by blackmailing Jeannot in chapter 7. I only saw what it cost her, six chapters later, at the hatch.
- What became of "the signs" the prefecture wanted counted by the eleventh.
- Which day it was, most of the time, and how many days were left.

### Where I was bored, lost or skimming

- The first half of chapter 4: the envelope, the card and the chair again, from the other bed.
- The opening of chapter 8. I read the first paragraph three times.
- All of chapter 10 except the scene in the chair.
- Sarthou's speech at the microphone in chapter 9.
- The sum (forty houses, forty-two beds, one man) from its fourth recital onward.

### Where I could not stop

- Chapter 2, from the stone to the last line.
- Chapter 6, all of it.
- Chapter 11 to the end of chapter 15, without a break.
- The square in chapter 16, from "I knew that he was" to the chairs.

### What I will remember in a month

- Kévin counting his fingers in the water and making five, and "I wanted six."
- "Forty-one."
- Coralie doing Larrère through the oven door, and not being able to finish it the second time.
- Larrère at the garden gate saying "I'll be brief," and nothing coming.
- Monsieur Daguerre calling "Here."
- Jeannot's six numbered points down the telephone, ending "Bugger off," and the same man getting down into the grave.
- "That was Either, with the river in it."

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. The price of the crossing is never said plainly, and the climax depends on it (chapters 3, 5, 8, 12, 14)

> "The one it cannot fetch back is found in her bed, and plays loto with the rest."

> "Lesca let go. In the steel room she got up off Inguma's back." … "Lesca was not in her bed."

What is wrong: by chapter 14 I am holding at least seven rules. The one who wakes first carries the other. The one carried drives. The host keeps the right hand. Inguma fetches the visitor when its counting is done. A third person can set it counting and send it. Lying on your side no longer keeps it off. Sitting on it stops it fetching. The rule that matters most, what happens to a woman it cannot fetch, was given once as an old woman's saying eleven chapters earlier. So the bravest act in the book and the sacrifice that follows went past me as a puzzle.

What I would do: have Arlette say it in chapter 12, where she already stands in the mayor's office and already knows "what my mother's words were for." She tells her daughter in so many words what her own mother said became of the one it could not fetch: found in her bed in the morning, and sat down among the dead. Then chapter 14 needs no explanation. Give the right hand one sentence of reason in chapter 5, or let Maylis ask and Lesca fail to answer, so that I know the book knows.

### 2. The book walks the same ground twice, and tells the night five times (chapters 00, 2, 3 to 5, 6, 11, 12, 16)

> "*left* / The t was crossed low." (chapter 4)

> "'Come back across. I've left you on that landing eleven years.' She said it standing, in her tunic" (chapter 5, the third time I hear it)

What is wrong: chapter 3 ends on the card and on "Come back across." Chapter 4 goes back three days and gives me the card, the chair and the same sentence from the other side. Chapter 5 opens with Coralie repeating it word for word. The night of 2014 comes in the prologue, as a speech in chapter 2, as a speech in chapter 6, in full in chapter 11 and at the microphone in chapter 16, with Coralie's version in 12 between. The sum is recited each time. Because the prologue shows the track and the single ring, chapter 11 confirms what I knew.

What I would do: start chapter 4 on the Friday, at the gauge sheets, and give the envelope and the first sitting in the chair in five lines of what Lesca already knows. Go straight to the bed with two pillows. Cut Coralie's word-for-word repeat in chapter 5 to her question. In chapter 11 keep only what is new (the reflector, Coralie's car in the yard, the kitchen clock behind the voice, the four minutes) and drop the houses and the beds, which I have by heart. Let the sum be said in full twice: once in chapter 2, once at the microphone.

### 3. There is a countdown and no calendar (throughout)

> "On Wednesday the window was white with frost and the tiles were quiet, and Maylis lay in the middle of Lesca's bed, in Lesca's body."

What is wrong: the book has a fixed date eighteen days off and two villages on the same calendar, which is a gift, and after chapter 2 it names almost every day by its weekday alone. Which Wednesday? I had to count back from the eleventh more than once. I felt the days running out in chapter 8 ("nine days off") and chapter 10, and nowhere else before chapter 13.

What I would do: put the date in the first sentence of every chapter and every change of day, as chapters 1 and 2 do. "On Wednesday the second of December." It costs four words a time.

### 4. Maylis's decisions happen offstage (chapters 7 to 8, and the first half generally)

> "At home Coralie had a late on the Wednesday, and no alarm rang to wake Maylis first. Lesca woke first. When the warmth beside Maylis in the chair stirred to go, Maylis had kept her forearms on the steel arms and stayed"

What is wrong: this is the first time the woman who says Either chooses anything, and it is told in the pluperfect, as a mechanism, in the middle of the hardest paragraph in the book. Chapter 7 ends on Larrère's car in the lane and says nothing of what she means to do that night. For ten chapters I watch her from outside while the book counts what she does not say. It left me more interested in Lesca and Coralie, and not much afraid for a woman who did not seem to mind being driven.

What I would do: end chapter 7 with Maylis giving Inguma the loto cards to count, on purpose, in her own bed, with Coralie beside her. It is three lines. Open chapter 8 with her already there. And give her one want of her own that I see before chapter 8's "I want that," which is the first.

### 5. Chapter 10 is where I would have stopped

> "She did not lie down."

What is wrong: it is five fragments. The river reaches the line, the two women quarrel in the chair, Lesca rules a ledger, the council votes on whether she may speak, she sits up over a sheet. Three days from the flood, a vote on who holds a microphone is the lowest stake in the book, and it gets the most room. The chapter ends on its flattest line.

What I would do: make chapter 10 the Tuesday and the chair. Larrère repeating her forecast word for word, Lafitte's petition through the window, Jeannot's hand up, then "It's a life in one hand" and the empty chair. End there. Carry the ledger and "*Nil*" into chapter 12. Cut the vote to one paragraph in chapter 12, reported by the young man from Pau or by Coralie, and keep Larrère's broken question for it.

### 6. The book builds a scientific premise, drops it, and then points at the hole (chapters 1, 5, 18)

> "'Why did it rain? There, that year, and not here. And now here.' / 'I don't know. I said it was mine, and you had the dates against me.' / 'I don't know either.' / They left it there."

What is wrong: chapter 1 gives me a university team, a steel room, a Dr Kessler and two lamps. I expected that to be answered for, and Dr Kessler never comes back. Chapter 5 raises the rain and proves Lesca's answer wrong. Chapter 18 asks again and shrugs. A reader of Crouch will take that as a promise broken. I took it as the book answering a note in the margin.

What I would do: cut those lines from chapter 18. The question was settled in chapter 5, where nobody was to blame for a dry winter either. If an answer is wanted, give it to Madame Etcheto in chapter 13, wrong and certain, in her own terms. A believer's answer would satisfy me. Two shrugs do not.

### 7. Two villages share one cast in different jobs, and I am not told who is what where (chapters 2, 8, 11, 16)

> "Marie-Jo had been her first deputy, until the night she handed her sash back across the council table in a carrier bag."

> "Seven councillors stood up in the council room and left it." (chapter 11) against "In April 2015 seven had got up from that table over Guy Larrère, and she had been voted into the room they emptied." (chapter 16)

What is wrong: about twenty named people, most of whom exist twice. Over there Coralie runs the home, Kévin has the yellow coat and has been in prison, Sarthou is retired, Marie-Jo speaks to the wall. I learn each of these sideways and some of them late. Kévin's prison is told in chapter 16. The carrier bag is never told. The seven councillors read as a contradiction until I worked out that it was one walk-out.

What I would do: chapter 8 is Maylis's first walk through the other village and she would notice every difference. Give each one a clause there. Say in one sentence what Marie-Jo resigned over. Make chapter 11 say that the seven left Larrère's table.

### 8. The last two chapters are in the wrong order for a reader (chapters 17, 18)

> "'I said it at eleven. Into a microphone, in front of the square, a metre from his widow. The van, the one ring, and a verb for the stone. They've had the medal back since, and the keys.'"

What is wrong: chapter 17 takes Lesca through to Tuesday night and closes her story with the pillow. It felt like the end of the book. Chapter 18 then steps back to three in the morning on Sunday, and Lesca tells Maylis what I watched in chapter 16. For a page I did not know where I was in time.

What I would do: keep the argument about the sum, which is the centre of the book ("Don't hand me your sum to sit behind"). Cut Lesca's recap to the verb alone. Then either move the Monday and Tuesday of chapter 17 to follow the chair scene, or give chapter 18 a first line that says plainly that this is the same night as the landing.

### 9. The local words and the water are not explained where I first need them (chapters 1, 3)

> "'I had a hand of water at the tonne on Sunday night. Last night I sat over mud.'"

What is wrong: in chapter 1 I am told that raising the gate drained a duck hide. In the prologue I have just watched the same gate about to be raised to flood the same meadows. It is chapter 3 before Larrère tells me that the gate works both ways depending on the river. The pawl is named in chapter 3 after the winch has already broken a rib. "A hand of water," loto, the brigade, and yellow, orange and red are never glossed at all.

What I would do: move Larrère's two sentences on the gate from chapter 3 into the car-window scene in chapter 1. Give "a hand" a depth the first time Jeannot says it. Let somebody call the loto by what it is played with.

### 10. I could not follow what the complaint was for (chapters 7, 9)

> "'The complaint at the brigade. Take it back. And the one in the mayor's tray.'"

> "'While you are all seated,' Sarthou said. 'The eighteenth of December is maintained. I retire with my files closed. That has always been stated.'"

What is wrong: Maylis spends her one piece of Lesca's knowledge on getting a complaint withdrawn, and I did not know what the complaint could do to her or why the forty-first letter had to go too. Sarthou's announcement at the loto is in his own manner, which is funny in the kitchen and hard to follow from a stage. The point of the whole thread, that Jeannot stops trusting her and so will not come up out of his hatch, arrived for me only at "You were given what's under my boards."

What I would do: let Maylis tell Jeannot in chapter 7, in one line, that hers is the only name in that file and that Hontaube reads it in January. Cut Sarthou's speech to three sentences. Let Jeannot say at the café counter, before he leaves his glass, that he will not take her word again.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 15, whole.** The clock times, "Both," the van in the dip, the key thrown back along the crown, the corridor of chairs, room twelve, "Forty-one."
2. **Kévin's fingers.** The loader bucket in chapter 3, "Five" in chapter 15, "I wanted six, Maylis" in chapter 18.
3. **Coralie's kit.** The shopping pad and its boxes, "Eggs is what Either gets in this house," two fingers on the wrist, the oven door, and the whole of her scene in chapter 12 down to "Because you'd have told me."
4. **Larrère's window and his minutes.** The few centimetres of glass, the window going all the way down in chapter 6, the eleven minutes with no bell in chapter 8, and "I'll be brief" at the garden gate in chapter 17 with nothing after it.
5. **The last page.** Letter No. 42 brought by hand, "it doesn't thank you," and Jeannot stepping over the string.
