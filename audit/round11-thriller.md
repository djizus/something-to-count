# Round 11 — critic, Thriller lens

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else but the brief.

Verdict against the bar: **below** Crouch as a thriller across the whole book; **level** with him from chapter 11 to chapter 15. Overall 6.5.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes — 6.** The brake on the woman holding the key is 'You'd have your years of pension in a commune that stopped existing at midnight. You could write to it.' A pension, set against forty-two beds, does not stop a reader's hand, and the forty-two come down to one, aged ninety-four.
- **Tension — 6.** Chapters 13 to 15 are an 8. Chapters 6 to 10 are a 4: 'The river was still under her father's line' (7), and it is still under it in 9.
- **Progression — 5.** The day the river reaches the line is told in summary: 'The association had voted before the café lit its lamps.' The one request (open it by day) is refused five times by five desks and never changes shape.
- **Comedy — 7.** 'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade.'
- **Characters — 7.** Coralie carries it: 'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.' Maylis wants nothing on the page until chapter 13.
- **The switch between dark and comic — 8.** Kévin in the flood, counting his fingers at four: 'He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another.'
- **Prose — 7.** 'Then the water came up the door to it and put it out.' Against it: one gesture ('two fingers on the inside of') used in ten chapters, and a flood account recited six times.
- **The ending — 6.** 'I don't know either.' / 'They left it there.' The book asks its own central question aloud in the last chapter and declines it, after three chapters of aftermath.
- **Clarity — 5.** 'Third, it's up a hand. A hand.' After nineteen turns, where chapter 13 says nineteen turns is the whole raise. The reader cannot say what the lever does.

**Finished unpaid:** yes. I would have stalled at chapter 10 and been carried from 11 without a stop.
**Pressed on a friend:** on one who reads village comedy, yes. On one who reads Crouch, no. They would stop in the middle.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below.** Three things put it there.

1. **Crouch's double acts in bad faith, and the wife does not know.** Dark Matter frightens because another Jason is in the house and Daniela cannot tell. This book has the same threat written down in chapter 4 ('and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it') and then disarms it: Lesca announces herself at the door, Coralie diagnoses her by a pulse, and by chapter 10 Maylis is asking to swap. Nobody is ever deceived. The reader is never afraid for the marriage; the reader is told about a marriage being discussed.
2. **Crouch's clock tightens by a physical fact in every chapter.** Here the clock is a date in a file. Between chapter 2, where it is set, and chapter 13, where it runs out, the river is on stage only as a gauge reading and a dark patch of clay, and the middle advances by people saying no.
3. **The Good Place's reversals re-read everything before them. This book's secret is on its first page.** The prologue shows the headlamps down the track and the single ring. Every later 'Did you know he was out?' is put to a reader who knows. The one true reversal, that Coralie has had the call log for twelve years, is as good as the comparison in kind and a tenth of it in reach.

Where it is level: the run from 02:49 to 'Forty-one' is as good as Crouch at his own trade, and the warnings that cause the disaster are better plotting than he usually manages.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

### 1. The middle has no water in it (chapters 6–10)

> 'The river was still under her father's line.' (7)
> 'The river was under the line, as her file had it for the fifth.' (9)
> 'The association had voted before the café lit its lamps.' (10)

From the Monday to the Thursday the flood is a figure read off a gauge, and the figure is 'under'. The only physical escalation in five chapters is a patch of clay going dark. Meanwhile the plan (an order to open by day) is refused by Maylis's own estimate (5), by the prefecture's rule (8), by the association (10), by Pau (13) and by Hontaube (13). That is one beat played five times. Each refusal is funny; none closes a door the reader thought was open. Chapter 10 is where the book gets put down: five fragments, the eighth of December and its vote in summary, a ledger entry, a council motion about a speech, and a last line with no pull ('She did not lie down').

**Do:** put the river on stage in every chapter from 6 on, and let each appearance remove an option. The dip in the track fills on a named day, so the van is already lost before the night and the reader knows it when she drives. The home's back door takes water once, early, and the towels fail. The ebb window is shown shrinking. Play the eighth as one continuous scene with the association voting on stage, seven men and forty houses, ending on Jeannot's raised hand. Move the mayor's speech vote to chapter 12.

### 2. The real choice is at five on Friday, and it gets a page and a catchphrase (chapter 13)

> ''Either,' Maylis said.
> He waited for the rest of it, and there was none.
> 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. 'That's how it was left.''

She has the handle on the shaft, the pawl in its first tooth, daylight, the ebb, and the one man who goes out on those meadows standing behind her. It is the solution the book has asked for since chapter 5. She gives it up in twenty-five lines, on her tic, under the pressure of one question and a parked car. Everything at 02:55 is forced by this moment; this is where Madame Etcheto dies. The reader does not feel what stopped her hand, so reads it as the author's hand.

**Do:** make it the longest scene in the chapter. Let her turn. Count the clicks, five, ten, twelve. Let the chain take the weight and the first water go through the throat onto the grass. Put the telephone ringing on the dashboard and the undated letter in the car a hundred metres off. Put Jeannot between her and the handle. Then the word. Then make her wind it back down with water already on the meadow, so that the retreat has a cost the reader can see. And give Maylis the sentence about it herself in the last chair scene; at present only Madame Darrigrand says it.

### 3. Lesca is never dangerous (chapters 6, 9, 10)

> ''Which of you is that?'
> 'Lesca. The mayor. Maylis is in the right hand, and she'd stop me if she could.'' (9)

'I'm coming to stay' ends chapter 8 as a threat and is never backed. On the landing she tells the truth at once and accepts the verdict. In chapter 10 the horror is stated well ('I'd wash the cups with it while you told her what I wanted for supper') and Maylis answers it by asking for the other side. An invasion that is invited and announced has no dread in it.

**Do:** one act of bad faith, in her own manner. She has answered a different question for twelve years ('No one came to tell him'); she can do it on a landing. 'Which of you is that?' gets a sentence true word for word and meant to pass. Coralie comes half across. The right hand is what gives her away, under two fingers. Coralie's speech stands as written, the reader has had a page of fear, and Lesca has something to tell her own Coralie in 17 that is worse than having asked.

### 4. The lever is in doubt, and the sums built on it do not close (chapters 6, 13, 15, 18)

> 'It gave all at once, and the pawl missed its tooth, and the gate dropped on its chain.' (6)
> 'It needed eighteen turns more, and her shoulder for the last of them, and it would be up in daylight' (13)
> 'at nineteen it stopped as if it had come to a wall… Third, it's up a hand. A hand.' (15)
> 'Turn the handle first, and it keeps them all.' (18)

Three accounts of the nineteenth turn, three outcomes. If a woman with one arm and a rib gone twice could not have raised that gate, then going to the tonne cost nothing and the death is not hers. If she could, the reader must know it before 02:55, and know how many minutes a full raise takes. A moral choice with a lever that may be broken is not a choice, and the book's last conversation assumes it worked. Lesca's 'Those two minutes are your two hundred metres' has the same hole: at the winch at 03:11 Maylis still has nineteen turns to make. In a book where both women price everything, the reader checks.

**Do:** raise the gate fully once before the night, on the page, timed, by the shoulder trick, so the reader holds a number. Make Jeannot's failure plainly his own (the knee, the boots, not knowing the trick). Then either correct Lesca's two minutes or let Maylis correct them; she is the one who would.

### 5. The secret is spent in the prologue, and the night is told six times (00, 2, 5, 6, 11, 16)

> 'She looked down that track for as long as the wipers took to cross the glass twice.
> When she took out her telephone the number was already in it.' (00)

Chapter 11 is built as the revelation and repeats the prologue almost by the sentence ('the clay moved under her palm', ten minutes, the van turned on the crown). What it adds is the reflector, the hour, and the car in the yard. The minute speech has by then been given in 2, paraphrased in 5 and recited in 6. Crouch does not open with the answer.

**Do:** end 00 on the handle. 'She held it while she counted three breaths, and did not fit it to the winch.' Cut the turn of the van and the telephone. Then the track, the reflector and 'It rang once' arrive in 11 for the first time, and Coralie's call log in 12 lands on a wound a chapter old. Cut the indirect retelling in 5 to the date and the name.

### 6. What staying would cost Lesca is never said, so chapter 14 is read as a puzzle (3, 10, 14)

> 'The one it cannot fetch back is found in her bed, and plays loto with the rest.' (3)
> 'Lesca was not in her bed.' (14)

That proverb, from a minor character in a comic scene, is the only statement that to stay over there is to die here. Nobody applies it to Lesca's plan. So when she sits on Inguma the reader does not know she is choosing to be found dead in her bed, and when she lets go and arrives among the tables instead of at home, the reader does not know why. The best image in the book is spent working out the rules.

**Do:** say it once in plain words before the night. Arlette is the one; chapter 12 has her within a line of it ('You say them so as to stay out of yours'). And when the wall goes in chapter 10, let Maylis tell Lesca whose the empty chair is. Then the eight rings are counted against a life.

### 7. Inguma's timer cannot be read, so it rings when the scene is over (6, 8, 10)

> 'Then the last load was counted, and Inguma was on her breastbone' (6)
> 'Somewhere the last square was counted on the last card, and Inguma came to fetch her.' (8)
> 'At dusk, somewhere, the last stone was counted.' (10)

Each ride ends at the moment the scene has delivered its line: the fourth stair, the stone, the raised hand. The reader cannot see the count running and so cannot fear it running out. The two places where the count is legible both work: the kernels at the bar, and 'You'll count my ponies on the commons. That's soon done.'

**Do:** give every job a size the reader can hold against a clock. Monday's gravel bought thirteen hours; let Lesca know it and size the next against it, and let her be caught short once, mid-sentence, where it costs the plan.

### 8. Larrère's hold is paper, and his card is played after the game (7, 13, 17)

> 'And every winter there are nets at a platform you hold the key to. I've written nothing down about that.' (7)

That is a lever: Kévin, who has been inside once in the other village. On the Friday the book uses the weaker one, the pension, and she loses the key in January whatever she does. On the night Larrère's whole part is a call she ignores. His real card, that he rang from the Castagnet kitchen and knew the man was out, reaches the reader at 'Castagnet, house' (15) and is then explained to Lesca by Maïté in a kitchen two chapters later, with no consequence for him in either village but an abstention and a walk down a lane.

**Do:** on Friday at the car window, make the threat Kévin. Then the boy who turns up at the home with two mattresses is the one her open gate would have sent down, and the reader has believed the brake. Let 'Castagnet, house' be said to somebody on the night, in the water, by Maylis or by Sarthou reading her screen, and have it cost Larrère something that Sunday.

### 9. Three chapters of aftermath, and Lesca pays in what she had priced at nil (16–18)

> '*Nil.*' (10)
> 'That night Lesca was in the bedroom when Coralie carried her pillow back across the landing and laid it on the wall side of the bed.' (17)

After 'Forty-one' come 16, 17 and 18. The confession holds. Chapter 17 then closes seven threads in seven short scenes, and 18 walks Maylis past Larrère, Sarthou, Kévin, her mother, Madame Darrigrand, the bus, the café, Marie-Jo, Coralie and the priest, each delivering a verdict to a woman who stands and receives it. Maylis's cost is right: the job, the name, the bed, a death. Lesca's is not: she loses a medal and an office she had already entered as nothing, the gendarmerie's question is opened and dropped, and she has the pillow back by Tuesday.

**Do:** end Lesca on 'The pillow stays where it is till after Tuesday', with the door open and the lamp lit, and cut the last sentence of 17. Reduce 17 to the Castagnet kitchen and the landing, with the form and the vote at a few lines each. In 18 keep Madame Darrigrand, Coralie and the grave; the rest can be heard through the café door.

### 10. The machine is set up and walked away from (1, 5, 18)

> 'What we want to know is whether, somewhere, you also see the other.' (1)
> ''Why did it rain? There, that year, and not here. And now here.'' (18)

Dr Kessler, the university, the two lamps and the sealed cards are the first thing the book builds, and none of them returns. The card does one job as a dream test and is then a thing in a pocket: 'the card from the steel room came out with it. She pushed it back.' Lesca claims the flood ('That flood is mine'), is refuted by dates, and the question is then put aloud in the last chapter so that both can say they do not know. A reader who came for the concept is shown the hole and told it is one.

**Do:** the book need not explain weather, but it must not point. The text already holds a sufficient cause: the rib on the Monday, two under one weight that night, rain on the Tuesday. Let one of them say it came with the seat, and stop. Give the card work on the night; chapter 13's 'She read it, looked away at the gauge and read it again' is the place it should be in her hand.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The last line of chapter 4.** '*That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*'
2. **Coralie's call log and her reason (12).** 'Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one.' and 'Because you'd have told me.'
3. **The warnings that make the night.** 'I'll go down on Friday the eleventh because you've said it', and the five o'clock call that produces 'The telephone stays in the van tonight.' Every attempt to save him puts him further under the hatch.
4. **Kévin's five (15) and his six (18).** The finger count set up as a joke in a loader bucket, paid in a flooded yard with a woman's chair on the tail of the van.
5. **The clock from 02:49 to 04:16, with the reflector.** 'screwed on crooked', 'It had held sixteen', 'Castagnet, house', the three open doors before room twelve, and 'Forty-one.' Do not add a word to room twelve.
