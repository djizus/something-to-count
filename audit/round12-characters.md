# Critic's report: Characters

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

In this report "Maylis" is the keyholder in the village where it rains, and "Lesca" is the mayor in the village with the stone. "Coralie-M" and "Coralie-L" are their two Coralies.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "One woman could turn the winch, or she could reach this hatch. She could not do both in ten minutes." (9) The choice is clear and both sides have faces. It loses a point because the man turns out to be "as dry as a kitchen" and the death that pays for him is spread over six other people (fault 4).
- **Tension: 6.** "On Thursday night she pushed again. On Friday night the cracked rib had no push left in it." (9) Three nights go by in one paragraph so the possession can happen again. Chapters 6 to 9 run the same loop four times: ridden, a fight for the jaw, fetched. Chapters 11 to 15 are a 9.
- **Progression: 6.** "First business is how this works, so that it's said once and we both have it the same. I make it three points." (5) The rules arrive as an agenda in the fifth chapter. Until then the reader is collecting lamps, cards and warm chairs without knowing what they buy.
- **Comedy: 7.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade." (7) The people are funny in themselves. The jokes are thinner on the ground than in the comparison.
- **Characters: 7.** "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." (9) The cast is an 8. The woman at the centre is a 6.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** Kévin in the flood water, counting his fingers to see whether he is dreaming: "'Five,' he said. He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." (15)
- **Prose: 7.** "She priced it, as she priced any job." (7) The pricing, the hand-widths and "as her father had" each recur until they read as a habit of the narrator and no longer of the woman.
- **The ending: 7.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (18) It costs a wife, a job and an old woman. It would be an 8 if the steel-room scene before it did not hand down the verdict first (fault 5).
- **Clarity: 5.** "Lesca had the legs, the left hand and the mouth. Maylis had kept the right hand." (6) The reader keeps a ledger of limbs, two Coralies, two Castagnets, two Larrères and a fetching rule, and the book never says why the rain came: "They left it there." (18)

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes, but I would have stopped once, in chapter 5, and come back. From chapter 9 I could not have stopped.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On one friend in ten, with a warning about the first five chapters.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below the bar, by one point, and all of that point is Maylis.**

- **Above Crouch on the wife.** Daniela in Dark Matter is what Jason stands to lose. Coralie has a form in her bag since March, a care plan that forbids her to join in, and her own reading of a pulse. She refuses the intruder herself, on a landing, with two fingers. Crouch never wrote that scene.
- **Below Crouch on making you want the life at risk.** Dark Matter spends its first chapter making a reader want that kitchen, in the husband's own words. This book gives the marriage two scenes of laughter (1 and 3) and then besieges it. The only person who says she wants Coralie in a bed is the one trying to steal her. Maylis never does, even in her own head.
- **Level with The Good Place on people being wrong in their own way; below it on what they do to each other.** Lesca is wrong by sums, Maylis by "Either", Jeannot by grievance, Sarthou by forms. That is the comparison's design, and it works. But Eleanor and Chidi change each other by liking each other. The two Maylises conduct five meetings, and at the end exchange advice about each other's wives like two colleagues swapping files.
- **Level on the reversal that re-reads.** Chapter 12 (Coralie has known about the call for twelve years) re-reads every scene on the landing, and 02:51 turns out to be the minute of the call. Both are at the standard.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. Maylis has no inside. (Whole book; sharpest in 5, 9, 13, 18.)**
"Maylis had no shorter word for it than hers." (18) "She listened to all of it." (11) "Maylis let that be." (18)
The narrator reports her as it reports the gauge. Her wanting nothing is the subject, but a woman who wants nothing still fears, envies and notices, and the book withholds all three. Once it does not: "In sixteen years Maylis had never got that laugh out of her. Lesca had got it in ten minutes, with Maylis's mouth." (6) That is the most alive she is in eighteen chapters. We never learn what "Either" protects, when it began, or what Coralie is to her beyond the wall side of a bed. Lesca has a guilt, a motion and a voice. Maylis has a rib.
*Do:* one sentence of that kind at each hinge: the eggs (1), the two boxes (5), the landing (9), the platform (13), the bag (18). Give "Either" one line of origin. The material is already there in Jeannot's "He stopped four turns short for thirty years and never rang me once". Let her know that about her father before he says it.

**2. The real choice is made in twelve lines, for a hostage who is offstage. (13.)**
"'Either,' Maylis said. He waited for the rest of it, and there was none."
At five on Friday she has the whole night to the minute, daylight, the handle on the shaft and the man standing on the dyke. This is Lesca's plan handed to her complete. She has said "Open" to the whole café and asked Coralie back an hour earlier. She puts the handle away because a sheet now carries Kévin's name. Everything that follows comes from this, and Madame Darrigrand is right to say so in 18. But the reader has had Kévin for two scenes and the threat for one paragraph, the book gives the decision one sentence of reasoning, and the sheet is never heard of again. Her worst act reads as the plot needing a night.
*Do:* put Kévin on the platform now. He comes over the rail for his nets later that same evening; move him forward so that she says "Either" looking at him and he hears it. Give her the paragraph. In 18, settle the sheet: Larrère posts it or he does not.

**3. Maylis's one day with power is seen from outside. (8, and the first line of 9.)**
"'I want that,' Maylis said." "Lesca understood it."
The chapter where the woman who wants nothing first says "I want" is told from the head of the woman she is riding. She is shown the price list of deciding: a lake where the commons were ("Maylis looked at that for some time"), her mother's paddock chained, Kévin in her waterproof after six months in prison, a Coralie across a landing. Her whole reaction, when the book returns to her, is: "She had had her day at Lesca's desk, and it had told her that the gate was Guy Larrère's twice over." She learns a point of procedure. The rule that the ridden woman narrates gives the stairs in 6 their force and costs Maylis her best chapter.
*Do:* open 9 with what she brought back, in her own head, before Coralie speaks: the lake, the ponies, Kévin, the box room. That is where a reader should learn why she will say "Open" on Tuesday and why she fears becoming the woman who says it.

**4. Maylis is excused by committee. (13, 15, 18.)**
"I'll not sleep in a chair. I've slept in room twelve since I came. I'll have them put me back at ten." "Twelve wouldn't open. The water was on the door." "Those two minutes are your two hundred metres, and they're mine."
Lesca's guilt is single: a van in her headlamps, one ring, the handle. By Sunday, Madame Etcheto's death belongs to the old woman's own stubbornness, Coralie's arm, a jammed door, Lesca's eight rings, Larrère's order, and only then to "Both". The victim is ninety-four and "the eighth this year". Maylis says "I had the key" and "It's true", but the plot has already shared out the weight for her.
*Do:* remove two of the six. Keep Etcheto in room twelve because the evacuation list, which Lesca dictated and Coralie pinned up, takes walkers first and the dyke end last, so the death is made by the two women's own instructions. Cut Lesca's claim on the two minutes or have Maylis refuse it flat.

**5. The last steel-room scene delivers the verdict and the moral. (18.)**
"A woman is dead of that, and it still isn't what I did. Don't hand me your sum to sit behind." Then: "Sit down when she tells you." "Answer her."
The woman who drowned a man acquits the woman who did not, "at length", in the one room where nobody can lie, so the reader is told it is true. Then each gives the other the key to her wife. The reader worked that symmetry out in 8 and 9. Coralie-M's "That was Either, with the river in it" is a harder and better judgment, and it comes later in the same chapter with nothing left to do.
*Do:* cut Lesca's speech to its refusal and its facts ("No. You won't sign it. I turned the van first."). Cut the exchange of advice whole. End the scene on "Drowned."

**6. Larrère is a window, a phrase and a folder. (1, 3, 6, 7, 13, 17.)**
"'I'll be brief,' he said." "Nothing came." (17)
He gives the order in both villages and is in the Castagnet kitchen on both nights. He has no reason for "shut" that a reader could fear or pity. Chapter 17 gives him three minutes and he has nothing, which is the joke, and also the book admitting he is empty. One line makes him a man: "He's dry as long as you do what you're told." (15) His order is the only one that keeps alive the husband of the woman whose telephone he is using.
*Do:* build on that line. Let him say in 13, in his own words, that shut is what keeps Castagnet breathing, and let Maylis know he is right. Then he is wrong in his own way, and her 3 a.m. is harder.

**7. Coralie is the scale both women are weighed on, and the two Coralies are cut as mirror keys. (6, 8, 9, 17.)**
"I'd have this one again." (6) "Do you know what I'd give for a woman at this table who sat when she was told? … I'd keep that one in the house." (8)
Each Coralie prefers the other woman within one bowl of soup. The mechanism shows. Sixteen years go over to a ticked box, and Coralie-L, who lives in doorways until 12, has her own want delivered in one paragraph in 17: "I didn't want those keys."
*Do:* cut the declaration in 6; the landing in 9 does it better and with a refusal attached. Move "I wanted my tunic back, and earlies" to 4 or 8, so that her offer to "the one who sits" comes from her and not from the design.

**8. The widow's anger is moved to a worse man on the evening of the confession. (17.)**
"I've wanted somebody to say it. I didn't think it would be said into a microphone."
Eight hours after "I drowned him", Maïté has Lesca up her ladder and Guy Larrère at the garden gate, and the two women are on one side of it. Lesca says "Don't give me him for that", and then the scene gives her him anyway. The silence at the gate should be hers to stand in, and Larrère takes it.
*Do:* keep the kitchen and "left". Put the gutter and Larrère at the gate on another night, or let Maïté send Lesca home first and face him alone.

**9. Maylis has no mother on the page. (18.)**
"'I'm not asking where you were,' she said. 'I've been told.'"
Lesca's Arlette has four scenes and the best line about a name ("I know what you're called. I'm the last that uses it"). Maylis's Arlette has eight lines at the end, leading a mare. So Maylis has no one who knew her before "Either", her father exists only as other men's comparisons, and the empty paddock in 8 means nothing to a reader who never saw the ponies on the commons with their owner.
*Do:* one early scene in Maylis's village with Arlette and the ponies on the low meadows.

**10. Neither woman has a face or an age. (5.)**
"They were of a height, and neither of them liked it."
This is the only time one looks at the other, and we get posture and a shut hand. Neither is described from outside as a woman. Both are described from outside as a post: "the commune's girl", "Madame le maire". A reader has to add thirty-one and twelve to learn they are in their forties, and cannot see what twelve years of office did to one face and twelve of verges to the other.
*Do:* three sentences in 5, when Maylis turns round.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie-L and the telephone (12).** "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." and "Because you'd have told me."
2. **The oven, both times.** "In my tray," she said, and shut the door on him (1); "'I'll be brief… The—' She stayed bent for a moment, with her hand on the door." (18)
3. **The landing in 9.** Two fingers on the wrist, "That's her. In this hand", and the pillow going back into the box room.
4. **Jeannot Castagnet from the ninth ring to the grave.** "And six. Bugger off." (11); letter no. 42, "It's four sides, and it doesn't thank you"; the second shovel.
5. **Lesca's voice.** The motion in 4 ("…and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it"), "It takes a small m", and "The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies" to a hall of the dead.

## Which relationship carries it, and does it pay

Each woman with her Coralie, and Lesca's more than Maylis's. It pays twice: a pillow carried back after the vote, and a car door shut on one. The relationship the book thinks it is about, the two women with each other, does not pay. It ends in an acquittal and a swap.

## Does the reader care who lives

Yes for Madame Etcheto from chapter 3. Yes for Jeannot from chapter 11, which is late. For the forty-two, only from 12 and 13, when eight of them get names. The reader never fears for Maylis herself, and that is the hole.
