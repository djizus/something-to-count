# Critic report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." (2) The sum is stated once and never has to be restated to be felt.
- **Tension: 7.** "The dyke had six minutes, and on foot she could reach one of the two." (15) Chapters 13 to 15 hold. Chapters 7 and 10 let it out through paperwork.
- **Progression: 6.** "On Wednesday night Inguma came down at its minute and sat. Maylis pushed up at it with what breath she had... On Thursday night she pushed again. On Friday night the cracked rib had no push left in it." (9) Three nights in one paragraph to put the pieces back where chapter 6 had them. The middle goes round: ride, be ridden, ask across a landing, be refused, three times over.
- **Comedy: 6.** "She said it had been told twice and given two lots of stars, and it got through them early and came at one." (3) That is the book at its funniest, and it is chapter 3. About one real laugh a chapter up to 9, fewer after. One mechanism throughout: a form meets something it cannot hold.
- **Characters: 7.** "So I'll go down on Friday the eleventh because you've said it." (6) Jeannot, Larrère and Madame Etcheto are funny in themselves. The two leads are funny as types and never on purpose.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'" (15) The switch is the best thing in the book and is as good as the comparison.
- **Prose: 7.** "Maylis had spent her word, and that was what she had bought with it." (6) Clean, and then a sentence that tells the reader what the scene just did. That sentence recurs.
- **The ending: 7.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (18) Right image, right cost. Lesca's half ends in summary across six short scenes, and the cause of the rain is waved away in two lines.
- **Clarity: 6.** "The one it cannot fetch is found in her bed, and plays loto with the rest." (3) The rule that carries Lesca's whole risk arrives as a proverb, seven chapters before a reader can use it.

Finished unpaid: yes. Pressed on a friend: yes, and not as a comedy. A friend who loves The Good Place would be told it is a sad book with a dry mouth.

## 2. Against the comparisons

Against The Good Place, **below** on comedy, **level** on the switch.

1. **Amplitude and variety.** The Good Place lands a laugh every twenty seconds and in four or five kinds: pun, sight gag, appetite, stupidity, absurd escalation. This book has one kind, understatement applied to procedure, and it never raises its voice. By chapter 10 the reader knows the mechanism and sees each one coming: "I have Monsieur le maire", "I've nothing on the sheet for this", the form with two boxes, the day book.
2. **The premise's own farce is under-run.** The Good Place would get three episodes out of a mayor loose in a verge-cutter's body who knows everybody's other life. The book spends two days and an evening on it and summarises the best beats.
3. **Gags that carry weight: level, in places above.** Chidi's indecision is funny until it kills him. The book does that five times: "Either" becomes "Both" with the river in it; the oven door cannot be finished; "Here" is called in flood water; "Still in the box" is retired for something crueller; letter forty-two turns out to be testimony. Nothing in the comparison cashes its running jokes this completely.
4. **Everyone wrong in their own way: level in design, below in sound.** Each person evades by a different instrument (Either, the minutes, the window gap, the registered letter, the file, not asking). That is the comparison's method. But they all speak in the same clipped tally, so on the page four of them sound alike.

Against Crouch there is nothing to measure on this lens; he is not funny and does not try to be.

## 3. The ten faults

**1. The body-share farce is summarised where it should be staged (6, 8, 9). Structural.**
"Lesca had the urn and the mouth, and gave each customer what he asked for and a sentence he had not." (9) "He had eleven years of it saved up, and it lasted eleven minutes. Towards the end he was saying things twice, and looking at the timer between them for help." (8) "Lesca filled it again. Maylis tipped it again. It was all she had." (6)
These are the three funniest situations in the book and each is reported in a sentence or dropped after one beat. We hear one of Lesca's sentences at the bar ("You're breathing"). We hear none of Larrère's eleven minutes. Coralie watches a woman tip her own soup back twice and says she will look at the hand in the morning. Farce lives in what the onlooker does next, and here the onlooker does nothing.
Do: play all three. Three customers at the bar, each told something true from the other village (who is dead there, who went to prison, who was her deputy), ending on Jeannot. Eight lines of Larrère dying without a bell. A third and a fourth spoonful, with Coralie's reaction rising. Take the pages from chapter 7. This is what gives the book a second comic mode.

**2. The steel-room sittings turn from comedy into recital (7, 10, 18). Structural.**
"No. You won't sign it, and I'll say why at length." (18)
In chapter 5 the sitting of two is a joke with a form: "Those in favour." "There's two of us." By chapter 7 it opens with Maylis listing chapter 6 back to Lesca ("Three minutes... Then Jeannot... Then my stairs"), in chapter 10 it is terms and counter-terms, and in chapter 18 Lesca delivers the book's moral verdict in a hundred and fifty words that Maylis accepts. The two women agree. Nothing is at stake between them and nothing is funny. It is the one place the book explains its ethics aloud.
Do: cut the chapter 7 sitting to "I'd have gone up" and "I'm coming to stay". In 18, cut Lesca's speech to the telephone and the one ring, let Maylis refuse to be let off, and get to "What was the verb?" "Drowned." in half the length.

**3. The aftermath is two receiving lines (16, 18). Structural.**
"Your counting comes out right for you each time. I wanted that said to your face, with no minutes taken." (16)
After the speech eight people walk up to Lesca in turn; on Maylis's Sunday eleven do. Each says one thing in the same cadence and leaves. Darrigrand in 16 repeats his own line from chapter 4 ("It's four when you're the one counting"), weaker, as a verdict. Arlette in 18 has three lines that do nothing chapter 16 did not. Madame Darrigrand and Marie-Jo carry the same news to Maylis a page apart. Nobody is wrong, nobody wants anything from her, so nobody is funny.
Do: halve both lines. Cut Darrigrand from 16 and Arlette from 18. Fold Madame Darrigrand into the café. Keep Tastet's summons, Marie-Jo's chairs, Kévin's six, the bus fare, Jeannot's letter. Let one villager get it comically wrong.

**4. The sentence after the laugh (throughout).**
"It was not Either, and the café heard that it was not." (10) "She had spent her word, and it had come second." (8) "It was the kitchen that frightened him." (7) "Anybody could send Inguma off to count. A kind old woman had done it from the far side of a hall." (9) "It was true, word for word, and it was meant to pass for Maylis." (9)
Scene after scene ends by restating its own irony. The reader had it. The comparison cuts on the line.
Do: delete the closing gloss wherever the scene's last spoken line already carries it. A dozen cuts.

**5. Four people have the same comic signature (5, 6, 7, 10, 11, 15).**
"I make it three points. One." (5, Lesca) "I'll number that. One," (6, Jeannot)
Jeannot numbers. Lesca numbers. Larrère lays his hand down three times for three conditions. Sarthou itemises. Add the women's reproaches, which all run "I've had X for N years, I'll say it once", and the village speaks with one mouth.
Do: numbering is Jeannot's and nobody else's. Lesca speaks in motions, votes and minutes, which she already has. Strip the "One. Two. Three." from her rules in 5 and move them as resolutions.

**6. The laugh in the minute's silence is not the reader's laugh (8).**
"In the seventeenth he billed the commune for two ruts its tractor had left in his track, pulling his van out of the mud. He took nothing off for the tow, since nobody had asked him whether he wanted one."
This is the hinge of the book: a living man's comedy breaking into a dead man's silence. The letter is paraphrased, so Maylis laughs and the reader watches her do it.
Do: quote two sentences of letter seventeen in Jeannot's voice, numbered, so the reader laughs a half-second before the widow speaks.

**7. The chrysanthemum farce stops (5, 6, 13).**
"A woman carries flowers to the wife because she's after the husband or because she's after the wife, and it's adultery whichever, and that one lives with a nurse." (6)
A whole café decides Maylis is courting a Castagnet, while the real adulterer drives up the back lane. Then the rumour is used once, as Larrère's leverage in 13, and never reaches the nurse. Coralie hears by ten that Larrère got three minutes; she never hears that her partner is carrying funeral flowers to a duck hunter's wife. It is the scene the marriage most needs before it starts to break.
Do: give Coralie the rumour at the soup in 6 or the bandage in 7, and let her be funny about it.

**8. A bit holds up the flood (15).**
"I've three things to say about it. First, I counted, as you do, by the click, and at nineteen it stopped as if it had come to a wall. Second..."
Jeannot arrives chest-deep with the news that the gate is up a hand, and gives it in nine lines and three numbered heads while Castéra waits to be carried and the water climbs the door frame. It is in character and it is the only place where a joke makes the dark wait.
Do: "It's stopped. At nineteen. It's up a hand." Keep "I put it in number six" and "They took note". Cut the rest.

**9. Forty, forty-one, forty-two (1 to 18).**
"Put it in forty-two." / "Coralie with forty-two in their beds." / "'Forty-one,' Coralie said."
Forty houses, forty letters in Lesca's head, forty-one registered, letter forty-two, forty-two residents, forty-one survivors. The catchphrase and the body count share a number and the text never makes the two touch, so it reads as an accident and blurs the last line of chapter 15.
Do: change the number of beds, or make the collision deliberate in one line of Jeannot's in 18.

**10. Gags announced or repeated (12, 13).**
"When the prefect reads out the date he'll call Here. He does it each year." (12) "He's been one off the strimmer since the loto." (13)
Daguerre's "Here" under the prefect's date is forecast four chapters ahead, so chapter 16 confirms a joke instead of making one. The strimmer line is the chapter 9 laugh told again.
Do: cut the forecast in 12 and keep "Can he be asked not to?" for another resident. In 13, stop at "He thinks it's the loto."

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **"Either"**, every use, and its three glosses in order: "with more words" (1), "in pencil" (5), "with the river in it" (18). And "Both".
2. **Room twelve in chapter 3**: Bixente, the two lots of stars, "Staff not to join in", the giggles heard through the door. Chapters 13, 15 and 18 stand on it.
3. **The verb for the stone**: "It reads that she opened him" (2), the timer ringing on Maïté's mouth, "left" (17), "Drowned" (18).
4. **The end of chapter 11**: the sole of a wader, then nine rings, then "And six. Bugger off."
5. **The oven door**: Coralie doing Larrère into it in chapter 1, and "'I'll be brief... The—'" in 18.
