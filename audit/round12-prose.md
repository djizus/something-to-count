Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else. Counts below are across all nineteen files (43,198 words, 91 scenes).

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** "One woman could turn the winch, or she could reach this hatch. She could not do both in ten minutes." (9)
- **Tension: 7.** "She was two hundred metres short of the dyke when it went." (15) From 11 on it is a 9. In 5 to 10 the contest is over who has the jaw, and nobody is afraid of a jaw.
- **Progression: 7.** "Then Inguma was on the breastbone, at the kitchen table, with its leaves against the bread." (9) This is the third ride that ends by being fetched (6, 8, 9). Each moves the plot, and each ends the same way.
- **Comedy: 7.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade.'" (7)
- **Characters: 8.** "'I chose you, Maylis, over somebody who asked.'" (18)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 9.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the neck. 'The—'" (18)
- **Prose: 8.** "She tucked the end in, and her hand stayed on it a little longer than a bandage needs." (18) The sentence is better than Crouch's. Five frames on repeat keep it from 9 (faults 5 to 8).
- **The ending: 8.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (18) Paid for by forty-two letters. Chapter 17 ends three days after 18 begins, which costs it the point.
- **Clarity: 5.** "The one it cannot fetch is found in her bed, and plays loto with the rest." (3) The book's rules reach the reader as a riddle and as a committee agenda, never once as a plain sentence.

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. From 11 I could not stop. In 2 to 5 I reread paragraphs to keep the mechanism straight.

Would I press it on a friend? Yes, with the instruction to reach chapter 5. A Crouch reader handed it cold stops in 3 or 4.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Above Crouch on the sentence, below him on the paragraph that carries the premise, level with The Good Place on the comic line, below it on separate voices.**

- Above: feeling is carried by objects that change state. The shopping pad, the pillow, two fingers on a wrist, the car window. Crouch writes "I love her" and moves on. This book writes "the car door shut on a pillow" and the reader does the rest. He has nothing as good as the oven impression that fails in 18.
- Below: Crouch states his mechanism once, in words nobody can misread, and never restates it. Here the mechanism arrives in three idioms (Madame Etcheto's riddle in 3, Lesca's "I make it three points" in 5, narrator recaps in 6, 7, 9 and 10) and a reader still cannot say after chapter 5 why the host keeps the right hand.
- Below: in The Good Place each person is wrong in a grammar of their own. Here Jeannot, Larrère, Sarthou and the priest have one. Coralie, Lesca, Maïté, Arlette and both Darrigrands share one for anything over three sentences (fault 4).

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The narrator does the sum after the scene has shown it, and at one pivot does it in place of a scene.** Every chapter from 6 to 15.

> "Maylis had shut her own jaw and won, and it had made no difference. Anybody could send Inguma off to count. A kind old woman had done it from the far side of a hall. Lesca need never be fetched, so long as somebody said the words. One hand and one word would not stop her. It would take a second pair of hands." (9)

The scene before it showed all six sentences. Same pattern: "Maylis had spent her word, and that was what she had bought with it" (6); "It was the kitchen that frightened him" (7); "Lesca understood it. In Maylis's village that man could order her gate…" (8); "The man she was trying to keep out of the water had beaten her, with the grudge she had given him herself" (10); "If she went back she turned the handle on him, as Lesca had. If she went on, the river had the Port" (15). The same hand footnotes every callback: "as he had once got into her bucket", "as it had on the dashboard of a tractor under the plane trees", "The two of them had got the giggles, and been heard" (all 15, the last one standing between the reader and room twelve).

The structural case is the end of 7: "She no longer wanted Lesca's Monday undone. She wanted a day at Lesca's desk." The heroine's change of intention, on which 8 depends, happens in two sentences of summary while she watches a car.

Do: delete every one. Where the turn does not survive deletion, the scene is short a beat, and 7 is: give Maylis one thing to do on that terrace that shows her wanting the desk.

**2. The opening and chapter 11 tell the same minutes in the same sentences.** 00, 11.

> 00: "She had mended that dyke each spring for six years. She gave it ten minutes."
> 11: "She had patched that bank each spring for six years. She put what was left in it at ten minutes."

Also "the clay moved under her palm" in both, "a sheet the width of a door" in both, the van turned on the crown in both, "It rang once" in both. Chapter 11 spends about 200 words on what the reader has held since page one, and then closes "Nobody in that village knew that she had looked down the track first" as if it were news. The two versions also disagree: in 00 she takes the handle out of its box and then turns the van, and in 11 "Before she touched the winch she turned the van."

Do: keep 00. In 11, enter at the telephone by the bed, take the dyke and the van-turn in two sentences, and give the room to what 00 withheld: the kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice, the reflector, the second ring that she did not wait for, the wader. The third telling in 15 ("as she had seen it done") then echoes one full telling, not two.

**3. The premise and the rules are never stated plainly.** 2, 3, 5.

> "She had taken Jeannot Castagnet, alive, to mean a village that the flood had passed by. It was 2026 in that village as well. But the rain that had drowned a man on its nineteenth day had begun there that morning. In that village the flood of 2014 had never come. It was coming now…" (2)

This is the most important paragraph in the book for the reader the bar names, and it says its conclusion twice with a non sequitur between. The rules fare worse: "The one who wakes first takes the other home in her" needs a second read, "You kept your right hand" is never accounted for, and the cost of staying is a riddle until 10.

Do: one sentence at the end of 2 ("The flood that had come to her village in 2014 was twelve years late in the other, and it had started that morning"). In 5, cut Lesca's numbered agenda to its comedy and let Maylis give the rules back in the keyholder's words. She already starts to: "So whoever sleeps on rides the other."

**4. One grammar in every mouth for the long speeches.** 12, 16, 17, 18.

> Coralie: "Don't give me the measurement." / "Don't give me the minutes." / "Don't tell me what he was."
> Lesca: "Don't give me him for that." / "Don't hand me your sum to sit behind."
> Lesca: "I'll say why at length." Maylis: "I'll say why, so that it's in the terms." Coralie: "I'll say what it is to me, and then I won't again."
> Arlette: "I'm not asking why once." / "I'm not asking where you were." Coralie: "I'm not telling you so that you'll have less of it."

Fourteen announcements of a speech before the speech, and 23 instances of "I've had" or "I'd have", spread across the cast. In the two last chapters, cover the names and Maïté, Arlette, Coralie and Lesca cannot be told apart.

Do: give each frame to one owner. "Don't give me X" is Coralie's (she is handed figures all day). Announcing is Lesca's (the chair). Strip both from Arlette, Maïté, Maylis and the Darrigrands and let them say the thing.

**5. "Lesca" as a subject that is never allowed a pronoun.** 6, 9, and 8 reversed.

> "Lesca waited for it. Then Lesca got the left shoulder under the handle and drove with the legs." (6)
> "Lesca did not knock. Lesca walked Maylis back up the track." (9)

Forty-four times in the 2,488 words of 6, thirty-seven in 9. The rule keeps two women in one body apart, and it turns the two funniest chapters into a deposition. The body parts already do the work.

Do: after the first naming in a paragraph, drop the subject ("did not knock, and walked her back up the track") or let the part act ("the left hand tied the handkerchief").

**6. The stamp that says a gesture has no precedent.** Thirty-eight year-count phrases, twenty-one "never / never once / not once".

> "In eighteen years beside that car Maylis had never seen it done." (6)
> "In sixteen years she had never ticked one of those boxes and left the other." (6)
> "In sixteen years Maylis had never got that laugh out of her." (6)
> "In thirty years at that counter Jeannot Castagnet had not left one." (7)
> "That chair had not asked the table a question in eleven years." (8)

Four in chapter 6 alone. "For eleven years Lesca had heard that woman get up on the far side of a landing" appears in 4 and again, one verb changed, in 8.

Do: keep the window and the half glass. Cut the rest. The window going "all the way, into the door" needs no eighteen years.

**7. Fifty-six of ninety-one scenes open on a clock time or a day.** Every chapter.

> "On Monday the thirtieth of November it rained, and…" (6) / "At eight Lesca stood…" / "At nine Guy Larrère drew up…" / "At noon Lesca walked…" / "At seven there was soup…"

Sixteen of nineteen chapters open the same way (all but 00, 14 and 17). The reader does need the calendar, since two years run day for day, and that is the reason to take it out of the sentences.

Do: put the date and the village under the chapter number as a dateline, which is also what the comparison does. Then open scenes on the thing that is happening.

**8. Three frames on repeat.**

- The refusal as closer: 33 "did not", 23 sentences opening Nobody / Nothing / No. "He did not count the ninth." "Nobody called." "Nothing came." "No head came up." (twice) "He did not look up from his numbers." (twice) "He waited for more, and there was none" (7) and "He waited for the rest of it, and there was none" (13). Keep the ninth strip and "Lesca did not knock."
- The habitual "as": 34 of them. "as on any night of the season", "as it does each year", "as she did at that table each year", "as she priced a job" (5) and "as she priced any job" (7). Keep the ones with a picture in them ("as a dog gets up", "as a door is held").
- The unit: "a hand" as a measure 15 times, "the width of" 12 (a door four times, a house twice, a finger, two fingers, a date, a hand, a barn). "Stood" 148 times, "hand" 257, and the body is contested with one phrase: "threw everything she had at it" (6), "with everything she had" (8), "threw everything at the jaw" (9).

**9. The narrator leaves her head to explain somebody else's.** 5, 9, 10, 15, 16, 17.

> "Coralie had the two of them in front of her, in one chair. … It did not take her long." (9)
> "He had said it in other winters, and he heard nothing in it." (10)
> "He had come out of a dry cellar half an hour before, by the neck, and had been told that the gate was to be opened on him." (15)
> "…who had kept a gate shut on him for twelve years and had now spoken to him across it." (17)

Each is a one-sentence visit to Coralie, Larrère, Jeannot, Larrère. The book is otherwise locked to one woman per chapter, and that lock is what makes the two-in-one-body chapters legible. Other shifts of voice: "A kind old woman" (9) is the narrator's only sentimental adjective; "worn as smooth as a step" (11) is the only stock simile; "They were of a height, and neither of them liked it" (5) is a joke from above. The two run-on sentences (13, the vigil; 15, the run) are a change of voice that earns its place.

Do: cut the visits. What Coralie works out in 9 is in what her hands do next.

**10. Usage, sequence, and five sentences that do not parse on first reading.**

- The dyke is "the bank" six times in 11's narration and nowhere else. The meadows are "flood meadows" (4), "low meadows" (7), "the commons" (2) and "common grazing" (1). The tonne is also a "hide" (4). Pick one of each outside dialogue.
- "Guy Larrère" in full 35 times of 67, "Jeannot Castagnet" 21 of 42, often twice in a paragraph.
- Chapter 17 ends on Tuesday the fifteenth, and 18 opens "At nine minutes to three on Sunday". The pillow comes back before the reader has seen the other one leave.
- "The bull would be lit at ten" (17) arrives fifty lines before the reader is told that the bull is a firework.
- "Lesca had asked it to be told." (6) "…nothing in what came told Maylis which of them turned the handle." (11: which woman, or which reason?) "Maylis paced it, from the hatch to that door." (18: she is standing in the road) "He stopped four turns short for thirty years" (11) "…and take a sash for it." (11)
- Spelling is consistent: British throughout, "telephone" in narration, "Madame le maire" lower-case and the one capital is the joke. No correction needed.
