# Critic report: Reader

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else but the brief. About 43,000 words.

Lens: an ordinary reader with no special knowledge of anything in the book.

Verdict against the bar: not there yet. From chapter 13 to the end it is as good as the comparisons and in places better. Before chapter 13 it makes an ordinary reader work harder than either comparison ever does, and some of those readers will not reach chapter 13.

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. I nearly stopped twice, in the square in chapter 1 and in chapter 7. From chapter 9 I would not have stopped for anything.

Would I press it on a friend? Yes, with 'give it until chapter four'. A book I have to say that about is not one I am pressing.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, as a reading experience. Above, in what it leaves behind.**

- **Ease.** A Crouch chapter can be read at speed by a tired person on a train, and nothing is lost. This book cannot. It never repeats a fact, and nearly every scene depends on one line from several chapters back: the kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice (11) pays in 'Castagnet, house' (15); 'softer than where I went down the winter you towed me' (13) depends on a diesel bill mentioned once in a café (6). When I caught these I was delighted. I missed about a third on the first pass. The Good Place restates its rules every episode in a line of dialogue; this book states them once, inside a comic routine, and moves on.
- **The premise is in play quickly, and then explained slowly.** Crouch has his man in the wrong world inside thirty pages and tells me the rules in plain speech soon after. Here the other village arrives at the end of chapter 2, which is fast enough. But I did not have a working grasp of who drives whose body, and when, until chapter 6, and I never learned why the one who is ridden keeps the right hand.
- **Above on cost.** Crouch's endings restore the family. The Good Place lets everyone off, kindly. This book kills Madame Etcheto, whom it spent two chapters making me love, and makes the reason a decent choice by a likeable woman. 'You were a kilometre off, fetching a man out of a dry hole.' (18) Neither comparison would have dared the dry hole.
- **Above on the people around the edge.** I would know Jeannot, Coralie, Madame Etcheto, Larrère, Kévin and Monsieur Daguerre if I met them. Crouch's supporting casts are furniture.
- **Below on comedy.** In The Good Place each of the four is wrong in their own way. Here almost everyone is funny in the same way, through a form, a tray, a timer or a minute book.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. The hinge of the book is its least readable chapter (14, and the first page of 15)

> 'Maylis meant to turn the handle. Coralie was under that dyke. Maylis's right hand moved first. It went across to the belt, where the key of the handle box hung, and shut on that.'

Everything turns here, in about 1,450 words, the second shortest chapter. I needed to take in four things while a telephone counted rings: that Maylis, at the moment, would open the gate on Jeannot exactly as Lesca did, and for the same woman; that Lesca tries to do the opposite of what she did in 2014; that the two of them fighting over one van is what loses the two minutes; and that Lesca gives up the life she came for. On the first read I got the third and fourth. I missed the first entirely, and it is the moral centre: the heroine is no better than the other one until she is told to be. Then chapter 15 hands me the consequence in the cryptic sentence quoted in the scores table.

What I would do: give the van twice the room. Say once, flatly, that Maylis was about to open it and that Lesca knew the reach because she had made it. Give Maylis a stated reason for knocking the van out of gear. In 15, replace 'Not first.' with what Maylis actually concludes. Nothing needs adding to the events. They need to be legible at the speed the scene wants to be read.

### 2. The square in chapter 1 asks me to hold too much before I care about any of it

> 'The mayor was Guy Larrère, and Guy Larrère was also president of the landowners' association, which owned the sluice gate on the flood meadows below the village. Maylis kept the key to its winch.'

The creature on the wardrobe had me. The kitchen with the eggs had me completely. Between them, in about 700 words, I am given a mayor, an association, a gate, a winch, a registered letter numbered 41, a second man in a van, a duck hide with a foreign name, and how tides drain it. This is where I nearly put it down. The postman joke works alone. The winch mime is needed for the oven. Jeannot and his hide are not needed yet.

What I would do: keep the postman and the winch mime in chapter 1. Move Jeannot's window, the hide and the ebb to the café in chapter 3, where he turns up again anyway and where I had the attention to spare.

### 3. The rules are given once, as a comedy sketch, and the main one reads backwards

> 'Two. The one who wakes first takes the other home in her.' ... 'So whoever sleeps on rides the other,' Maylis said.

The sketch is funny ('Those in favour.' 'There's two of us.'). But it is the only statement of the rules, and after it at least five chapter endings depend on my working out, from a rota on a fridge and somebody's alarm, which woman will wake first and therefore who will be driving whom. The end of chapter 9 is the clearest case:

> 'Her alarm would go at six on five mornings, Friday's the last, and each time it would wake Maylis first.'

I had to stop and reason out whether that was a threat or a relief. A chapter ending that needs a pencil is not a hook. And nobody ever says why the ridden one keeps her right hand, though half the book's physical comedy hangs on it.

What I would do: after the sketch, one plain sentence in the narrator's voice, cause then effect. One sentence, anywhere, for the right hand. End chapter 9 on what the rota means, stated.

### 4. The night of 2014 is told four times and its reveal is given away on page one (00, 2, 11, 16)

> 'She looked down that track for as long as the wipers took to cross the glass twice. When she took out her telephone the number was already in it.' (00)

The prologue is a fine hook and I would not lose it. But it means that chapter 11, which replays the night in full so that Maylis can learn what I already know, is the one big scene I skimmed. 'Forty houses' appears seventeen times in the book and 'forty-two' twenty. Lesca is meant to have said her sum until it is worn smooth; by chapter 11 it was worn smooth for me too.

What I would do: cut the replay in 11 to what is new (Larrère's voice with a kitchen clock behind it, the crooked reflector, the four minutes, the sole of the wader, the confessional) and get quickly to the telephone. The nine rings and Jeannot's six numbered points are among the best things in the book and should not lose a word.

### 5. The middle is made of paper, and I lost track of who held what (7, 8, first half of 10)

> 'With no date on it, it isn't a letter. It's a sheet of paper that I keep. You're the one who dates it, and there are three ways you can.'

In two chapters I am asked to follow an undated resignation, a blank sheet waiting for a boy's name, a complaint against persons unknown, a statement to a gendarme, a threat that gets the complaint withdrawn, a petition and a vote. Each is clear on its own. Together they are the slowest stretch in the book, and it comes at the point where a Crouch reader wants the screw turned. The resignation is also a weak threat: chapter 3 has already told me that her post goes to Hontaube in January.

What I would do: one lever per person. Kévin's name on the sheet is the lever that matters, because it produces 'Either' on the platform in 13. Let that carry Larrère's hold on her, and thin the rest.

### 6. Everybody is funny in the same way

> 'Is this a request, or a notification?' the woman said. 'I've got a tray for each, and a book.' (13)

That is a good joke. It is also the joke of Larrère's tray, Sarthou's form ('Either is not a person'), Tastet's diary, Madame Ducasse's day book, the young man's sheet, Lesca's motions, Jeannot's numbered letters and the care plan. By chapter 13 I had met it nine times. The laughs that surprised me came from elsewhere: Kévin wanting to fly up and look in his mother's gutter, Josiane's rule about flowers and adultery, Monsieur Daguerre calling 'Here', Coralie talking into the oven.

What I would do: keep the paper for Larrère and Lesca, who are defined by it. Give two of the others a different way of being wrong.

### 7. Three chapters stand between me and the flood (10, 11, 12)

> 'On Thursday at seven Coralie changed Maylis's dressing at the kitchen table.' (11)

Chapter 9 ends on Sunday the sixth with a real threat. The river then reaches the line on the eighth and I am made to wait until the eleventh through a vote, a replay of 2014 and a rehearsal in the other village. Chapter 12 holds one of the best scenes in the book, and I still read it wanting to be on the dyke. The tension is carried only by the gauge figures.

What I would do: with 11 cut down as in fault 4, run it into 10 or 13 so that the wait is two chapters and not three.

### 8. The book keeps ending (16, 17), and then goes back in time to end again (18)

> 'At nine minutes to three on Sunday Inguma sat on Maylis once more.' (18)

Chapter 17 has taken Lesca through to Tuesday night and the pillow coming back. The next sentence I read puts me on the Sunday before, in the steel room, with a Lesca whose fate I already know. The scene that should be the two women's reckoning has no risk left in it. Before that, chapter 16 follows the confession with seven people coming up to her one after another, and 17 has six scenes, each of which closes something. I skimmed the queue in 16.

What I would do: put the last steel-room meeting where it happens, in the night between Saturday and Sunday, before Lesca's Monday and Tuesday. Cut the queue in 16 to the three that land: Marie-Jo and the chairs, Tastet's summons with the place left out, and Darrigrand's 'Your counting comes out right for you each time.'

### 9. In the ridden chapters the names are repeated until it reads like instructions (6, 9, 14)

> 'Lesca waited for it. Then Lesca got the left shoulder under the handle and drove with the legs.' (6)

I see why: 'she' would be useless. It kept me straight, and it tired me. 'Lesca stood Maylis on the platform', 'Lesca walked Maylis into the café', 'Lesca took the body up one stair'. On some pages a name opens every sentence.

What I would do: fewer changes of hands per page. Let a paragraph belong to one of them, and let the other break in once.

### 10. Things I never understood, or understood late

- **Why it rains.** 'I don't know either.' They left it there. (18) I could have lived without a cause. But the book raises the question twice (5 and 18) and Dr Kessler, who opens the book with two lamps and a promise, never comes back. I expected the box to matter and it turned out to be a room.
- **What Maylis wanted with a day in the mayor's chair.** 'For it, your chair for one day, when I ask.' (10) She had already had one and learned that it could not help her.
- **'The bull would be lit at ten.'** (17) I had no idea what this was for half a page. Nor did I know why the anniversary of a drowning has a bar, a band and fireworks, which I think is a point the book is making and which went past me.
- **'She gave it ten minutes.'** (00) I first read this as her waiting ten minutes. Chapter 11's 'She put what was left in it at ten minutes' is the clear version and belongs on page one.
- **Who nets the eels.** The nets are under Jeannot's boards and the boxes are in Kévin's van. I never knew whether they were partners.
- **'It doesn't need you asleep.'** (13) I did not know what Kévin meant, and it is the last thing said before the night.

What I would do: one clause each. For the rain, either a rule or silence, not a question asked twice and dropped.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 15 from the dip to 'Forty-one.'** The clock times, 'Here', 'Then they'll be wanting the bins out', and above all Kévin in the water: ''Five,' he said. He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another.' I will remember that longest.
2. **The oven, both times.** Coralie doing Larrère into the oven in chapter 1, and failing to in chapter 18.
3. **Madame Etcheto, every line.** The stars, Bixente reported, 'A rib doesn't laugh', the sandbags ('Nineteen. That's few. It counts quickly.'). Her death works because of them.
4. **The bedroom in chapter 12.** ''Why not?' 'Because you'd have told me.'' and 'I thought I could have the one without the other, if I kept a landing between.'
5. **The last page.** Letter No. 42 that 'doesn't thank you', and Jeannot getting down into the grave in his waders.
