# Critic's report: Thriller

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. 43,198 words. Nothing else opened.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "*That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*" (4). A body, a bed and a village are all on the table by 15% of the book. It stays under 8 because at the hinge of the plot (13, five o'clock) the thing weighed against forty houses is a sheet of paper.
- **Tension: 6.** "Nothing had sat on her, and nobody had come home with her at six." (10). The threat that ran chapters 4 to 9 turns out to have a tap, and the heroine's hand is on it. Peaks of 9 (the stairs in 6, the landing in 9, 13 to 15); troughs of 4 (7, 8, 17).
- **Progression: 6.** Chapter 8 sends Maylis across "to learn how a gate was ordered over that man's head" and she comes back with "Her rule gave that gate to him twice over", which page two had already given me: "The mayor was Guy Larrère, and Guy Larrère was also president of the landowners' association." The gauge readings progress every chapter; the plot does not.
- **Comedy: 7.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade.'" (7).
- **Characters: 8.** "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'" (9).
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." (15, Kévin counting five fingers in the flood).
- **Prose: 7.** "Lesca moved the right hand. Lesca got it across the oilcloth as far as the edge of the plate" (8). Exact and consistent, and slow where a thriller needs to be fast. The name opens 33 sentences in chapter 6 and 25 in chapter 9.
- **The ending: 8.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (18).
- **Clarity: 5.** "Her alarm would go at six on five mornings, Friday's the last, and each time it would wake Maylis first." (9). I read that line three times and still took the wrong threat from it.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. I would have put it down once, in the run of desks and council tables in 7 and 8, and picked it up again for the loto.

**Pressed on a friend?** Yes, on the friend who reads novels. Not on the friend who lent me Recursion, and not described as a thriller.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch as a thriller. Above him on what the ending costs.**

1. **Rules a tired reader can use.** Crouch gives the rule of the box or the chair in a sentence, then never bends it, so the reader can run ahead of the hero and be afraid in advance. Here the rule is "The one who wakes first takes the other home in her", which needs an alarm clock, a rota on a fridge, who is on her back and who is on her side, and who gave the thing how much to count. Position is not the problem: the premise can be stated at 9% (end of 2), the first crossing is at 15% (4), the two speak at 20% (5), which is Dark Matter's pace. Legibility is.
2. **Dread that cannot be switched off.** In Dark Matter the other Jason is in the house and nothing Jason does will get him out. Here, from chapter 10, Lesca comes in only when Maylis lies on her back for her. The book is level with Crouch, or above, for exactly five chapters: "'I'd have this one again.'" (6) is worse than anything Daniela says, because the wife is not fooled, she prefers.
3. **Cost.** Crouch's families walk through a door at the end. This book kills a woman the reader has laughed with, by the heroines' own two decisions, lets the man they saved say "I was in no danger, and I'll say so to anyone", and takes the marriage from the one who tried hardest. That is above.

Against The Good Place's reversals: level. "The floor was dry. It was as dry as a kitchen." re-reads the whole rescue, and the clock heard behind Larrère's voice in 11 and seen on Maïté's wall in 17 is a fair clue fairly paid.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. Chapter 13, five o'clock: the hinge does not weigh.**
"On Tuesday, when she had said open, the sheet in the folder had been blank, and now it carried Kévin Labat's name and sat in the car along the crown. / 'Either,' Maylis said."
She has the handle on the shaft, daylight, the ebb, and the one man who goes out on the meadows standing behind her. She puts it back for six months of somebody else's prison and a job that ends in January anyway. The book has itself priced that cost, and low: in 8 Kévin "had done six months, for eels" and came out to a waterproof and a mower. So I was with Madame Darrigrand ("You had it on your belt then") five chapters before she spoke, and I watched the night that follows as the result of a failure I did not believe, not of a trap.
*Do:* make opening by day cost her the night. At noon Larrère says it: move that gate before I ring and the letter is dated on the spot, the key comes off your belt, a man of the association lets the gate down within the hour, and nobody of yours is on that dyke at three. Then "Either" is still her word and her relapse, but the reader can see the sum under it: shut now, or absent at 02:51. And take the comfort out of chapter 8: show what the six months did to that Kévin.

**2. Chapters 3, 5, 9, 10, 13: the rules are a thicket, and one of them bends.**
"'Two. The one who wakes first takes the other home in her.'" (5)
To know who is in danger on any given night I must hold: on her back or on her side, lying still or pushing, sitting or standing in the chair, whose alarm rings first, and how many hours of counting were set. The end of 9 asks me to do that sum and 10 cancels the answer in its second paragraph. The price of staying is a riddle in 3 ("The one it cannot fetch is found in her bed, and plays loto with the rest") and is not decoded until 10 ("Nil"), so "I'm coming to stay" runs for three chapters without my knowing it means she dies. And Madame Etcheto's "It comes to that bed" is set aside in 13 with "Where Inguma would come down from she had no idea", because the plot needs it on the roof of a van.
*Do:* three rules a reader can say aloud. Lie still and you meet. Whoever stays in the chair goes home in the other, until the thing has finished counting. The one it cannot fetch dies in her own bed. Drop "who wakes first" as a variable. Decode the loto riddle in 5, when Lesca first says she will stay. Give the van one line before the vigil: it comes at that minute to whoever has that gate.

**3. Chapter 14: the reversal of the book is done in fifteen lines.**
"Maylis meant to turn the handle. Coralie was under that dyke. Maylis's right hand moved first."
The two women have changed places. The one who never chose reaches for the winch, for Coralie; the one who drowned a man tries to drive to him. That answers the question Lesca cannot answer in 12 and 17 ("I can't find which part of it turned"), and it goes by in 250 words of a 1,449-word chapter, closed by an aphorism: "a van does not go down a track with one woman at the wheel and another at the gear lever." Eight rings should be the longest two minutes in the book and are the shortest. Then 18 does not charge it: Maylis takes "The van in the dip was mine. So was both", Lesca takes two minutes, and nobody takes "Go to the tonne first", the five words that sent her down the track, or the hand that went to the key.
*Do:* one move in the cab per ring, key, clutch, lever, horn, with Lesca recognising the reach for what it was when she made it. In 18, Maylis says what her hand did first and Lesca owns the five words as well as the two minutes.

**4. Chapter 13, 22:41 to 02:49: she has the minute and no plan.**
"She had tried the man twice, at his van window at five and at his hatch at dusk."
She knows the ring, the hour and the ten minutes. For four hours she fights sleep. I am never told what she means to do when the telephone goes, so there is no plan for me to watch fail. I had also been given "twenty-five minutes" on foot twice, and did the sum she does not: there and back by two. At 03:03 she has him out by the collars, one-handed, in a paragraph. The same hole is in the home. Lesca's order in 6 was "'Have them out of their beds by two. Dressed, in the corridor'"; on the night they are in their beds at three, nobody says why, and that is what kills the woman in room twelve.
*Do:* a third attempt at the hatch at one o'clock that fails for a reason on the page. One exchange at the back door for why forty-two people are not got up at two, on an orange, on the word of a woman who cuts verges. And before the roof of the van gives, one sentence of what she intends.

**5. Chapters 9 and 10: the antagonist is given an off-switch at 47%.**
"Inguma had come down at its minute and sat in the middle of the bed, beside her. Nothing had sat on her, and nobody had come home with her at six."
A bandage and lying on her side. With Coralie's two fingers able to tell the women apart (9), the body-theft plot is finished before the halfway mark, and from there Lesca enters only by invitation (10, 11) or by the bent rule (13).
*Do:* no tap. Either the strapped rib cannot be lain on by the second night, or cut the device and let Lesca stay away by her own word. "Friday." is more frightening than the bandage is reassuring. End 9 on it.

**6. Chapters 16 to 18: 8,000 words after the dyke goes, and the order runs backwards.**
"That night Lesca was in the bedroom when Coralie carried her pillow back across the landing" (end of 17, Tuesday), then "At nine minutes to three on Sunday" (start of 18).
The dyke goes at 81%. Chapter 16 is a second climax and holds. Chapter 17 is six scenes, each closing an account, with nothing at risk in any; "I don't know yet what it cost" is suspense I cannot share, having watched the eyes closed two chapters earlier. And the reckoning between the two women, owed since 5, arrives after one of them has had her pillow back.
*Do:* end 17 on Saturday midnight, the pillow not carried and the lamp lit. Give Bayonne and the vote in five sentences in the forward tense the book already uses for "On Monday she would lie awake at that minute to see". Keep the third box. Then the last sitting is the hinge between the two endings, not an appendix to one.

**7. The night of 2014 is told six times.**
Prologue: "She laid her hand flat on the dyke beside the water, and the clay moved under her palm. She had mended that dyke each spring for six years. She gave it ten minutes." Chapter 11: "put her hand flat on the bank beside it, and the clay moved under her palm. She had patched that bank each spring for six years. She put what was left in it at ten minutes."
Also 2 (the speech), 12 (Coralie's morning after), 16 (the speech again), 17 (the statement). "Forty houses" appears 17 times and "forty-two" 21. Chapter 11 is the reveal chapter, on the eve, and two thirds of it is the prologue over again; what is new, the kitchen clock, the crooked reflector, 02:51, the four minutes the reflector burned, is buried in the retelling. By 16 the confession holds nothing I do not have, and lives only on what the square does.
*Do:* 11 gives what the prologue held back, in 400 words. The hand on the clay is not said again until Maylis does it herself in 15, where the rhyme is earned.

**8. Chapters 7 and 8: the river is rising and the stake is a file.**
"She signed under her own name. It was her second paper since nine." (7)
Three desks in a row in 7, a council table in 8. Of the three signings only Jeannot's turns the plot (it loses her his vote in 10). In 8 she sits a whole day in the world where the flood has already happened, with the file open under her finger, pages of it "typed, by the gendarmerie", and reads one line of gauge.
*Do:* let her read the typed pages and carry one hard number home to Friday. A fact brought back across is what this premise exists to deliver, and she brings none. Halve 7: Sarthou's interview as a consequence of Larrère's, not a scene of its own.

**9. Chapter 18: the engine of the plot is declared unexplained.**
"'Why did it rain? There, that year, and not here. And now here.' / 'I don't know.' … 'I don't know either.' / They left it there."
A flood keeping another village's dates twelve years late is the whole clock of the book. Chapter 5 had an answer, and a good one: the winch stuck, the rib put her on her back, the thing sat on two, the weather came across, three letters about a pawl. Then Maylis knocks it down, and the last chapter raises the question again in order to shrug.
*Do:* choose. Let 5's answer stand and be said in 18 in four words, "It's in the minutes", or do not ask.

**10. The two chapters before the night end closed.**
11: "She listened to all of it." 12: "'If I come in and you're lying there with your eyes open,' Coralie said, across the landing, 'I'm ringing Bayonne.'"
Both are right for the people and wrong for the position: Friday is the next page and neither line points at it. 12 also spends its first 700 words on a young man with a roll of tape while a river is over the line next door.
*Do:* 11 ends on what she now knows she will hear at 02:51. 12 opens on Coralie with the speech in her hand and ends with Lesca on her back, the door across the landing open.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 15, whole.** The clock times, a third of a breath then a quarter, "The floor was dry", the doors nine, ten, eleven, twelve, Kévin's five, the count that stops at forty-one in a hall laid out like the hall of the dead.
2. **The newel post (6) and the landing (9).** A right hand as all that is left of a woman, and a wife who reads the pulse and sends the better offer downstairs.
3. **The last 300 words of 13.** The one unbroken sentence from 22:41 to 23:58, five fingers counted, the tin of the roof giving, the telephone. Fault 4 is about what comes before it, not this.
4. **The square after the sentence (16).** The far loudspeaker saying "drowned him" late, the man who claps, the child who is told, "Madame Castagnet will lay the wreath".
5. **Jeannot Castagnet, every line.** Six numbered points and "Bugger off" at five in the morning, "I was in no danger", No. 42 delivered by hand, the second shovel.
