# Critic report: Characters

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "'The gate, or Castagnet? Say which, Maylis. For once.'" (15). The stake is a person asking a person, which is why it works. It is held at 7 because the personal threat, Lesca taking the body and the bed, is defused by Coralie herself as early as chapter 9, and the price of staying is entered by the woman who pays it as "Nil" (10).
- **Tension: 7.** Chapters 13 to 15 are a 9. The middle is not: chapter 9 ends on a schedule, "The rota was on the fridge door, and Coralie was on earlies all week."
- **Progression: 6.** "She had the legs, the left hand and the mouth again, and Maylis had her right hand" (9). That is the fourth statement of the same rule. Chapters 6 to 9 cross and re-cross with the stakes the same height.
- **Comedy: 7.** "It's in the plan now. Staff to wait. Staff not to join in." (3). The comedy comes from people, not from the narrator, and it builds. It rarely goes past wry.
- **Characters: 7.** "Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you, and that was me." (1). Coralie is a 9, Lesca an 8, the village an 8. Maylis is a 6 until chapter 10, and she is the lead: "Maylis had not written a line for that."
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'It's Saturday.' 'Then they'll be wanting the bins out,' he said." (15), said in a flooded corridor. Also Coralie failing her own oven routine in 18: "'I'll be brief,' she said ... 'The—'".
- **Prose: 7.** "Lesca did not knock. Lesca walked Maylis back up the track." (9). Controlled and exact, and in the shared-body chapters it gives up pronouns and reads like a rota.
- **The ending: 7.** Maylis's is an 8.5: "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." Lesca's is a 5: "That night Lesca was in the bedroom when Coralie carried her pillow back across the landing and laid it on the wall side of the bed."
- **Clarity: 6.** Chapter 18 opens on "Sunday the thirteenth of December, the night after the dyke went ... her council's vote was two days off", one page after chapter 17 has shown that vote and its result.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. From chapter 10 I could not have stopped.
**Press it on a friend?** On a friend who reads for people, yes. On a Crouch reader, not without telling them to get to chapter 6.

## 2. Against the comparisons (Characters)

**Above Crouch. Below The Good Place. Short of the bar by one point.**

- **Above Crouch: Coralie.** Daniela in Dark Matter and Helena in Recursion are what the hero is afraid of losing. Coralie is the prize for both women and refuses to be one, twice, in her own words: "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." (9). There are two of her as well, the nurse who is funny and the director who gives orders, and each wants something that is not either Lesca: a floor at Bayonne, her tunic back. Crouch has never written a spouse this alive.
- **Level with The Good Place: the secondary cast.** Each is wrong in their own way and each has a mechanism: Jeannot numbers his grievances, Larrère speaks through a gap, Sarthou "cannot hear a stamp", Tastet writes down what was said first, Daguerre calls "Here". The running gag that turns into grief (Daguerre in the flood, Kévin's five fingers) is exactly what that show does.
- **Below The Good Place: the lead, and the reckoning.** That show takes Chidi's indecision, makes it funny, then makes him look at what it cost. This book makes Maylis's "Either" funny for twelve chapters, lets it kill someone at five o'clock on a Friday, and then lets her double take the blame (fault 1). And the two women never arrive at each other. Their last exchange is advice, traded like terms.

**The lens questions.**

- *Distinct?* Yes, by the fifth line either speaks. "I make it three points" against "Put it in forty-two."
- *Alive?* Lesca from her first page. Maylis from chapter 10. Before that she is a right hand and a word.
- *Softened or excused?* Both, at the end, by each other. See faults 1 and 3.
- *Described from outside as a woman?* No. Neither is given a face or a figure. They are hands, a rib, a seam in a palm, a signature. Nobody in the village finds the couple remarkable except Josiane, who gets a laugh for it.
- *Who would be missed?* Madame Etcheto, Jeannot, Kévin, Daguerre, Maïté, in that order. Bixente and Nadine would not.
- *Which relationship carries it?* Coralie and whichever Lesca is in front of her. It pays in 9, 12 and 18. The relationship the premise promises, Maylis and Lesca, pays once (14, the open palm) and is otherwise a committee.
- *Does the reader care who lives?* Jeannot, yes. Madame Etcheto, yes, and her death is the best-earned thing in the book. Lesca's own death is never a fear, because she prices it at nil and the book lets the figure stand.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The act that kills Madame Etcheto is never owned by the woman who did it. (13, 18)**
"The gate would be all the way up in daylight, on the ebb, while the one man who went out on those meadows was on the dyke behind her." Then: "'Either,' Maylis said." That is the fatal moment of the book. In 18 Maylis's account is "The van in the dip was mine. So was saying both", Lesca answers "A woman is dead of that, and it still isn't what I did", and takes two minutes onto herself. Coralie's charge is also "Both". Only Madame Darrigrand names five o'clock, and Maylis's sole response is to correct the distance in her head. The lead is excused by her double.
*Do:* in the steel room Maylis says it: at five he was on the dyke, the handle was on the shaft, and she said Either. Lesca cannot take that from her and must say so. Move Coralie's charge from the telephone to the dyke, which the Port has already told her about. And in 13 put the motive on the page: Maylis has seen Kévin on a mower in the yellow waterproof after "six months, for eels" (8). One sentence makes the relapse a choice between a boy and a Port that she declines to make, and not a tic.

**2. Lesca's "Nil" is never re-costed after chapter 12. (10, 12, 14)**
In 10 staying costs "Nil". In 12 her own Coralie tells her "You needn't have", leaves her door open, and says "If I come in and you're lying there with your eyes open, I'm ringing Bayonne." Lesca then goes and tries to die in that bed anyway, and when she counts what letting go costs her in 14 the list is "rain on the tiles, and ... a woman four steps off who would have her tomorrow, and ... a man at a counter who owed her for diesel." The woman who will find the body is not in the sum. The best scene in the book changes nothing in the person it happens to.
*Do:* move the statement of the plan ("She meant not to come back") from the rehearsal to after the landing, on the bed with the speech, so that she decides against an open door and the reader watches her do it. Then put her own Coralie in the cost in 14. This makes Lesca harder, not softer, and makes her letting go worth more.

**3. Lesca is let off at home. (17)**
To Maylis she said of the stairs, "I'd have gone up." To her own Coralie she says only "Twice I asked you back across a landing, with the other one's mouth." The Monday night she tried to walk another woman's body up to that bed without asking is left out, and Coralie takes what she is told as late courtship: "It's four steps from your door to mine, Madame le maire. They've taken you eleven years." Three days later the pillow is back, in one sentence. The nurse's standard in 9 is not applied by the director in 17.
*Do:* make the stairs the third thing Lesca confesses. Let the director's hour be spent on that. Keep "I'm not carrying it in to the mayor", and give the pillow's return a scene or end on the lit lamp.

**4. The lead is built out of what she does not say, for too long. (1 to 9)**
"'It's twenty minutes to Bayonne,' she said." "Maylis had not written a line for that." These are right, and there are too many with nothing against them. Through nine chapters the reader's wanting sits with Coralie, against the heroine: when Coralie says "I'd have this one again" the reader agrees. Maylis's love is asserted by a laugh in chapter 1 and by fear on the stairs in 6.
*Do:* give her acts, not words. One thing in chapters 1 to 3 that she does for Coralie unasked and unseen, in her own trade, that Coralie never learns of and the reader does. Another in 9 or 10. She prices everything; let her once do a job for nothing and not say so. Then the handover line with Coralie "as a distance" hurts instead of confirming.

**5. The opening spends the book's one reversal. (00, 2, 11)**
"She looked down that track for as long as the wipers took to cross the glass twice ... It rang once." By the time Maïté asks "Did you know he was out?" in chapter 2 the reader already knows the answer. Chapter 11 then delivers the same night again in nine hundred words, several of them the same ("the clay moved under her palm"), and 12 and 16 give it a third and fourth time. The reader is never wrong about Lesca, so nothing re-reads.
*Do:* end 00 on "She held it while she counted three breaths, and did not fit it to the winch." A question, not the answer. Then 11 is a revelation and chapters 2, 4 and 5 ("That flood is mine") read differently the second time.

**6. The hostage is never told. (7, 13, 18)**
"That's prison, for eels." Kévin's name is why the gate stays shut. He is on the platform with her at dusk in 13 and she asks him "What does your van seat?" In 18 he says "Four winters you looked the other way for nothing. I can do a morning", still not knowing. The book withholds the scene where the person she traded a Port for gets a say.
*Do:* she tells him on the platform, in two lines. Whatever he answers, she then carries his answer as well as her own. If he says open it and she still does not, fault 1 sharpens.

**7. One voice under the tics. (4, 5, 12, 17)**
"I'd never hear the end of it. I wouldn't want to hear the beginning." (Maïté). "Then the commune can sweep it." (Darrigrand). "I said I'd not set foot. I've set it." (Arlette). "That's Either again, with more words." (Coralie). Strip the names and these are one speaker: the short statement, then the cap. The characters who stand apart do so because their speech has a different shape: Jeannot's lists, Daguerre's one word.
*Do:* let two of them be bad at talking. Maïté should be plain and slow until "left". Give Nadine or Bixente a line that trails off. Somebody in this village must be unable to finish a sentence well.

**8. In the shared-body chapters the women become an inventory of limbs. (6, 8, 9, 14)**
"The legs were hers to walk, and the left hand, and the eyes" (6). "She had Lesca's legs, her left hand and her mouth" (8). "She had the legs, the left hand and the mouth again" (9). "Lesca had the legs, the left hand and the mouth" (14). Each crossing re-explains why it happened. The space goes to mechanism and comes out of feeling.
*Do:* state it once, in 5, where Lesca presides. After 6, cut every recap. Use pronouns wherever only one woman can be meant.

**9. Larrère in Maylis's village has no inside. (13, 15, 18)**
In Lesca's village he gets "Nothing came", and his hat coming off. In Maylis's, where he orders a gate shut from the kitchen of the man he is cuckolding, he gets a folder, and when she names the lane he parries without a mark: "And whose word is that?" After the breach: "'They tell me,' Larrère said, and stopped", and then he dates the letter. The one frightened thing about him ("It was the kitchen that frightened him") is told and not shown again.
*Do:* in 13, when she says she saw his car, let the window come down. In 18 put him where he can see the Bayonne bus.

**10. Chapter 17 is a ledger. (17)**
Etcheto, Maïté, the gutter, Larrère at the gate, the landing, the form at Bayonne, the council, the pillow: eight accounts closed in 2,300 words, three of them opening "On Monday at nine", "On Tuesday at six", "That night". After the square, Lesca is never alone with what she said.
*Do:* cut the Bayonne form to a line inside another scene. End the chapter on the landing. Put the vote and the pillow where they can breathe, or let the vote be reported.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie's two fingers on a wrist**, from "A hundred and twelve" to room twelve, where "She kept it for the half minute" and then "put a line through the last name on the list."
2. **The landing in chapter 9.** "That's her ... In this hand. She can't get a word out, can she? I've had sixteen years of her saying nothing, and I know it when I've hold of it."
3. **Chapter 12 from "You left this on the kitchen table" to the end.** "'Why not?' 'Because you'd have told me.'" Reorder around it (fault 2). Do not rewrite it.
4. **Kévin's hand.** "Five," said "as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another", and "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything as much."
5. **The last three pages.** "What do you want, Maylis?" "I want to dig it myself." Letter 42 that "doesn't thank you". The car door shut on a pillow. Jeannot in the grave. And with them Jeannot throughout, unrepentant: "It's Jeannot. Jean's for the stone."
