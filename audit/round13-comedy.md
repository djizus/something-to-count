# Critic's report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

Verdict against the bar: **below The Good Place as comedy, by about a point; level with it on the switch between dark and comic; above it in one respect, which is what the comic flaw is made to cost.** Crouch is not a comic writer, so for this lens he is not the comparison.

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. From the end of chapter 5 I was not going to stop.

Would I press it on a friend? On the friend who likes dry, yes. On the friend who loved The Good Place for laughing out loud, no, not in this state: I laughed aloud nine or ten times in 44,500 words.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, on rate and heat.** The Good Place lands several jokes a minute and plays them loud, with people talking over each other. By my own tally this book has about forty lines that got a laugh from me in chapters 1 to 10 and about twenty in chapters 11 to 18, every one delivered in the same level voice as the drowning. The café in chapter 6 and the loto in chapter 9 are the only scenes with a room full of people being wrong at once. They are the two funniest scenes in the book and they show what the rest withholds.

**Below, on the pair.** The Good Place is four people arguing ethics badly in one room for fifty episodes. This book has a better pair than any of them, two versions of one woman who cannot lie to each other, one of whom tries to chair the meeting, and it puts them in a room together for about 3,500 words out of 44,500. For the rest, one of them is a mute right hand.

**Level, on making the moral problem funny without making it small.** The Good Place does the trolley problem as blood on a tram. This does it as a stone that cannot be given a verb: "'Saved whom? It sits under his name. It reads that she saved him.' / 'Opened.' / 'Opened what? There's a name over it. It reads that she opened him.'" That is the same move, an ethical question nobody can answer turned into a procedural one everybody has an opinion on, and the problem comes out of it larger. Monsieur Darrigrand does it again in two lines: "'It's four when you're the one counting. When it's your house it's one, and they knock it down.'" The two-box form in chapter 17 does it a third time.

**Above, on what the flaw costs.** Chidi's indecision is a joke that kills only Chidi, and then as a joke. Maylis's "Either" is a joke for twelve chapters, is said once on a platform with the handle fitted and a man standing beside her in daylight, and a woman of ninety-four drowns in her bed. "'Both.'" followed, three chapters later, by "'That was Either, with the river in it'" is better built than anything the series did with its own running flaw.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The double act is starved, and its natural high point is missing. (Structural; chapters 5, 7, 10, 14, 18, and the gap after 8.)**
"'Then it's carried, or it's tied, and in a tie the chair decides.' / 'There's nobody in the chair.'" (5)
This is the best comic register in the book and there are five sittings of it, the second under 400 words. The rule that neither can lie on her feet in that room is used once for a laugh ("They were of a height, and neither of them liked it") and otherwise only for gravity. Worse: chapter 7 opens with Maylis's bill of complaint for Lesca's Monday in her body. Chapter 8 then gives Maylis a Wednesday in Lesca's, in which she writes "Either" into the first deputy's diary, laughs in the minute of silence for the dead man in front of his widow, and lets Guy Larrère speak for eleven minutes. Lesca's bill for that day is never presented. Chapter 9 opens with Maylis refusing to lie still and the two do not speak again until chapter 10. The scene the structure has set up, the mayor reading out what the commune's girl did to eleven years of standing, to a woman who cannot lie about why, does not exist.
What I would do: write that sitting, at full length, between 8 and 9, with Lesca presiding and Maylis in possession of the facts. Let the cannot-lie rule do comic work in it: each asks the other one question she would rather not answer about Coralie. Lengthen the sitting in 7. If the word count must hold, take it from chapters 4 and 13 (faults 3 and 5).

**2. The best comic ideas are reported and not played. (Chapters 8, 9, 18.)**
"He began with his grandfather. At three minutes he stopped for the bell, from habit, and got none, and went on like a man who finds a door unlocked. He had eleven years of it saved up, and it lasted eleven minutes." (8)
A man who has been cut off at three minutes for eleven years is given the floor with no bell. That is the funniest situation in the book and it is five sentences of summary. The same at the loto bar: Lesca "gave each customer what he asked for and a sentence he had not." Which sentences? That is the joke, and it is not on the page. The same with Jeannot's letters, the book's longest running gag: the seventeenth, which makes a woman laugh in a minute of silence, is paraphrased; No. 42, the last object in the book, is described from outside ("It's four sides, and it doesn't thank you"). Chapter 11 proves the voice can carry it: "'And six. Bugger off.'" is the hardest I laughed.
What I would do: play Larrère's eleven minutes in speech, with the looks at the timer, a page of it. Give three customers at the bar and the three sentences. Quote the seventeenth letter in Jeannot's numbering, and give the first numbered point of No. 42.

**3. The comic premise is not in play until chapter 6, and the opening does its setup twice. (Structural; chapters 00 to 5.)**
"'Come back across. I've left you on that landing eleven years.'" This sentence closes chapter 3, is spoken again in the middle of chapter 4, and is read back "word for word" at the top of chapter 5.
The engine of the comedy is one woman driving another who has kept one hand. The first full day of it is chapter 6, at 12,000 words, 27 per cent in. Before that, chapters 3 and 4 tell one event from each side with the same beats: envelope opened, card read, looked away, read again, the chair warm, the hand closed over Coralie's fingers. Chapter 4 has three laughs in 2,600 words. Chapter 2 ends on three paragraphs of the narrator working the premise out for me. And 00 is a solemn hook with no comic signal that also spends chapter 11 in advance: after it I knew she looked down the track and rang once, so Maïté's "Did you know he was out?" in chapter 2 was not a question for me.
What I would do: cut the second envelope and the second reading of the card from chapter 4 and start it in Maylis's bed with Coralie's fingers under the wrong hand. That saves about 800 words and puts the first drive a chapter earlier. Stop 00 at "She took the handle of the winch out of its box." The van turned on the crown and the one ring belong to chapter 11.

**4. The farce threads never collide. (Chapters 5, 6, 9, 13.)**
"'A woman carries flowers to the wife because she's after the husband or because she's after the wife, and it's adultery whichever, and that one lives with a nurse.'" (6)
By chapter 9 the village holds four wrong ideas about Maylis: that she has a mayor in her (Lafitte's pad), that she is after one of the Castagnets (Josiane), that she bought Jeannot's complaint off him with the key (Lafitte again), and that she knows about Larrère and Maïté (Larrère). A farce puts those four in one room and lets them feed each other. The loto is that room. It gets twelve lines and is cut off by "'Here'". Josiane's theory is never heard from her again; it comes back once, in chapter 13, as a threat in Larrère's mouth. Each misunderstanding is used once and filed, so the comic scenes stop where they should build.
What I would do: put Larrère and Maïté in the hall at the loto. Let Josiane's theory and Lafitte's theory be argued across the bar, over Maylis's head, while her right hand goes on giving correct change and Lesca's mouth makes each one worse by answering it truthfully. Then Daguerre's "Here".

**5. Chapter 13 plays one refusal four times and buries the hinge of the book in the middle. (Structural; chapter 13.)**
"'Either,' Maylis said. / He waited for the rest of it, and there was none. / 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. 'That's how it was left.'"
That is the moment the running gag kills somebody, and it sits a third of the way through 3,772 words, after Hontaube says no and Larrère says no, and before Jeannot says no at his van and no again at his hatch. The Hontaube call is funny ("'Then it should have gone last Monday'"). The walk to the hatch at dusk is not, is the second of three trips down that track, and uses up the twenty-five minutes and the bolted hatch before the night needs them.
What I would do: cut the dusk walk entire. End a chapter on "'Then it's shut.'" Open the next with the white van's reflector going away down the track, then Kévin, then the wait.

**6. The last sitting is a summing-up, and the moral problem stops being funny at the moment it is settled. (Chapter 18.)**
"'If you had turned that winch first on Friday, you'd have been right about the houses too. You'd have drowned him, and you'd be me.'" And then: "'Sit down when she tells you.'" / "'Answer her.'"
For seventeen chapters the book keeps its problem open and funny by handing it to people with an interest: a council, a man with a broom, a form with two boxes. Here one of the two parties delivers the verdict "at length", and the pair then exchange the lesson each has learned from the other's Coralie. It is the only sitting with no joke in it until the last line. The village has already given both verdicts better: against, "'He'd had the key and gone for a walk'"; for, No. 42.
What I would do: keep the two minutes, "'Madame Etcheto paid for them in hers'", "'I'd sign your sum.'" / "'No.'", the verb, and Inguma going out by the door. Cut the "you'd be me" paragraph to its first three sentences. Cut the exchange of advice.

**7. The narrator explains after the punchline. (Throughout; worst in 6, 9, 10, 18.)**
"Maylis had been able to get the mouth for one word, and the word she had found was Either. It had stopped whatever Lesca had ready, and it had sent Coralie out of the door disappointed." (6)
I had just watched that happen. Also: "It was not Either, and the café heard that it was not." (10) "The man she was trying to keep out of the water had beaten her, with the grudge she had given him herself at that counter." (10) "It was the kitchen that frightened him." (7) "It was weather, and nobody had sent it. They left it there." (18) The costliest is Kévin. In the flood he counts his fingers and "said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another". Three chapters on he explains it: "'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything as much.'" The first is the best silent beat in the book and the second takes it back.
What I would do: cut every one of these. Where a fact is needed for clarity, put it before the line, not after. Keep Kévin's flight in 18 and cut the six.

**8. The rule is recapped in the same sentence four times, and the ridden chapters drum a name. (Chapters 6, 8, 9, 14.)**
"Lesca waited for it. Then Lesca got the left shoulder under the handle and drove with the legs." (6)
"Lesca" appears 46 times in chapter 6, 37 in chapter 8, 39 in chapter 9. The legs, left hand and mouth are inventoried at the top of 6, 8, 9 and again in 14. A comic scene needs its beats to arrive without a label on each, and here every sentence is tagged with who owns the limb.
What I would do: state the division once, in chapter 5, in Lesca's three points, where it is already a joke. After that open each ridden scene with the right hand doing something its owner would do and the rest of the body doing something she would not, and let "she" stand for the driver until the hand moves.

**9. The aftermath in the square is a receiving line. (Chapters 16 and 17.)**
"Monsieur Tastet found her among the chairs." "Her mother had not gone home." "Monsieur Darrigrand had come up from the plots with his broom on his shoulder." "At two the young man from Pau went up the mairie steps with a sheet in his hand."
After the sentence at the microphone, ten people come to Lesca one at a time, each says a piece and leaves, and chapter 17 adds five more. Singly they are among the best things in the book: Marie-Jo's chairs, "'I can't sign a summons to nowhere.'" As a sequence they queue, and a queue has no comic shape.
What I would do: overlap them. Tastet presents his summons while Marie-Jo is counting stacks and corrects his arithmetic. Darrigrand sweeps round the lieutenant. Fold the two o'clock suspension into the return of the medal, so the young man from Pau comes once.

**10. One comic turn stalls the flood. (Chapter 15.)**
"'It's stopped,' he said. 'Your winch. I've three things to say about it.'"
Jeannot then says all three, 130 words, on the sill of a flooding building with wheelchairs waiting and the water rising up a door frame, and nobody stops him. His numbering is the right joke. Letting it run to the end at that moment is the one place a joke weakens the dark.
What I would do: let Coralie cut him off at "Second". He keeps "'I put it in number six.'" The reader fills in the rest, having heard him in chapter 11.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The oven.** Coralie doing Larrère into it in chapter 1 ("'There's the question of the eggs. It isn't settled, and you're not to worry about it.'") and failing to in chapter 18: "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the neck. 'The—'". The second is the dark smothering a joke on purpose, and it is the measure of what the night cost.
2. **Room twelve.** "It's in the plan now. Staff to wait. Staff not to join in." And with it what each person gives Inguma to count: leaves, loads of gravel, verges, signatures, "'my ponies on the commons. That's soon done. They went up a ramp'", nineteen sandbags, "'That's few. It counts quickly.'"
3. **Either, from the eggs to the river.** "'Eggs is what Either gets in this house'", Sarthou's "'I cannot summon Either to the brigade'", Tastet writing it down first, the platform, "'Both.'", "'I know that word. I've cooked it.'" Not one link out of that chain.
4. **The telephone answered.** Nine rings, "'Who's dead?'", and six numbered points ending "'Bugger off.'" (11). It is the same question old Lafitte asked over the chrysanthemums, and it is the proof that this man is alive.
5. **The silent punchlines of 15 and 16.** "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'" "'Then they'll be wanting the bins out.'" Kévin's "'Five.'" "'Forty-one.'" Then, in the square, Tastet turning the sheet over "to see whether it went on", the man who claps, "A child asked what the lady had done, and was told", and "'Somebody has to stack them.'"
