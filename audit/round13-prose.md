# Round 13: Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. 44,500 words. Nothing else opened but the brief.

Verdict against the bar: below it. The sentences are better made than Crouch's. The book is slower than his, and it has one comic register where The Good Place has six. A reader who came for those two would finish it and respect it, and would not say it was as good.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "'Then which do you want? The gate, or Castagnet? Say which, Maylis. For once.' / 'Both.'" (15). The sum is exact and the bill is paid in a named person. Until chapter 12 the forty-two are a number.
- **Tension: 7.** "The telephone began to ring in her hand." (13). Chapters 13 to 15 are as tight as the comparison. Chapters 7, 8 and 10 are tension by committee, and the narrator closes each scene by explaining it.
- **Progression: 6.** "'Four to three,' Larrère said. 'It stays shut.'" (10). The gate is refused in 3, 7, 10 and 13 by the same man, the last three times with the same folder. At half the length of Dark Matter the middle still repeats.
- **Comedy: 6.** "'Then it should have gone last Monday.'" (13). Funny, and always in the same way: procedure applied to an emergency, reported flat. There is no second instrument.
- **Characters: 7.** "'Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you, and that was me.'" (1). Coralie is alive in every line. The other women of the village share one voice (fault 6).
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the neck. 'The—' / She stayed bent for a moment, with her hand on the door." (18). One joke, set in chapter 1, broken in the last. This is the thing the book does as well as the comparison.
- **Prose: 7.** "Then the water came up the door to it and put it out." (11). Checked for filler, stock contrasts, intensifiers and adverbs, the text is almost clean: fewer than thirty words ending in -ly in the whole book, no semicolons, no exclamation marks, dashes only in cut-off speech. What holds it at 7 is structural: one sentence length, one way of ending a scene, one figure of speech, and a narrator who repeats what the scene has shown.
- **The ending: 7.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (18). The last page costs enough. The page before it has the two women state the verdict aloud (fault 5).
- **Clarity: 6.** "what a woman had in her head came to the chair whether she sat in it or not" (11). The crossing rule is told seven times and a new rule is still needed in chapter 11. The village words (loto, mairie, commune, Pau, the brigade) are never glossed, while tonne and pawl are.

**Finished unpaid:** yes, from chapter 12 without stopping. **Pressed on a friend:** on a friend who reads Penelope Fitzgerald or Simenon, yes. On the Crouch reader, no.

## 2. Against the comparisons

Below, on balance. Three things put it there.

1. **Crouch's sentences change with the danger. These do not.** By a rough count the narration averages between 10 and 13 words a sentence in every chapter, kitchen or breaking dyke, with a median of 10. Chapter 15 is the exception: bare clock times as paragraphs, over a hundred sentences of five words or fewer, and one running sentence for the sprint ("She had not run since the rib, and it let her have a third of each breath, and the mud took her boots to the ankle..."). That chapter is level with him. The other seventeen are told at the pace of minutes being read.
2. **The Good Place lets each person be wrong in a separate idiom. Here only the men are.** Larrère has "I'll be brief", Jeannot has "I'll number it", Sarthou has "I cannot summon Either to the brigade". The women all speak the withheld grievance in three clipped clauses and leave on a household order. And the show is loud and fast; this narrator never raises its voice, so the comedy has one volume.
3. **Above both on the object.** Crouch names feelings in italics. This book names none. It has a pad with two boxes, a car window that goes down "all the way, into the door", a half glass left on a counter. "Afterwards Coralie ticked both boxes herself. She wrote *eggs* under them, drew a third box and ticked that." Neither comparison does that.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The narrator explains the scene it has just shown.** Whole book; worst in 6, 7, 9, 10, 14, 15.
"Maylis had been able to get the mouth for one word, and the word she had found was Either. It had stopped whatever Lesca had ready, and it had sent Coralie out of the door disappointed." (6). The scene above it showed exactly that, and Coralie's "For a minute I had somebody" had already landed it. The same coda follows the soup ("At that table it was Coralie who was funny, and Maylis who laughed..."), the folder ("He had asked her twice now about a kitchen... It was the kitchen that frightened him", 7), the loto ("Maylis had shut her own jaw and won, and it had made no difference...", 9), the vote ("It was not Either, and the café heard that it was not", 10), the reach for the key ("Maylis was about to do what Lesca had done, for the same woman, to the same man", 14) and the five words ("The man was to be fetched out first, and the gate raised after", 15). I count eighteen. Each one tells the reader the scene was not trusted.
*Do:* cut every paragraph that opens on "had" and restates the scene before it. Keep the two or three that carry a new plan (the end of 7, the end of 9) and cut those to the plan.

**2. The prologue spends chapter 11.** 00 and 11, and a third time in 15.
00: "It ran down the clay in a sheet the width of a door and crossed the road in her headlamps. She laid her hand flat on the dyke beside the water, and the clay moved under her palm." 11: "Water was going down the clay in a sheet the width of a door, in her headlamps, and across the road. She got out and put her hand flat on the bank beside it, and the clay moved under her palm." Chapter 11 then announces, "Nobody in that village knew that she had looked down the track first." The reader has known since page one, and has watched her turn the van and dial. Half of the central chapter is a reprint, and "Did you know he was out?" (2) was never a question.
*Do:* end 00 at "She took the handle of the winch out of its box." Leave the van, the track and the telephone to chapter 11, where 02:51 on the screen explains Inguma's hour and the call arrives as news.

**3. The crossing rule is recited at every crossing.** 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 (twice), 14.
"Maylis had stayed sitting in the chair, so for that day she had Lesca's legs, her left hand and her mouth, and Lesca had her own right hand and nothing else." (8). The same inventory opens 6, closes 7 as a forecast, returns in 9 ("She had the legs, the left hand and the mouth again, and Maylis had her right hand") and again in 14. Lesca's three-point sitting in chapter 5 states it once, in character, and it is funny there. After that it is a manual. And when the plot needs something the rule does not give, the narrator adds a clause on the spot (11, quoted under Clarity).
*Do:* state it in 5 and never again. From 6 on, the left hand is Lesca and the right is Maylis, and the reader can be left to hold that. Plant the chapter 11 rule in 5 as a fourth point, or have Maylis sit in a warm chair that night.

**4. One temperature.** Whole book.
Sixteen of eighteen numbered chapters open on a day or a clock time; so do 57 of 91 scenes. Chapter 7 runs "At six... At first light... At nine... At three... At four... At dusk... At eleven". 78 sentences begin "Then", 79 "She had", 74 "It was". The pluperfect runs to 25 uses of "had" per thousand words in chapters 2 and 11. This is Lesca's idiom, minutes and items, and it is right for her. The narrator uses it for Maylis too, so the two women differ in what they say and not in how their chapters sound.
*Do:* keep the dateline and the itemising for Lesca's chapters. In Maylis's, open on the object or the speech, and let the sentence run when she is alone, as it does once in 13 ("She said the gauge to herself, and the bags on Sarthou's step, and her mother's ponies by name..."). Keep the weather word near the top of each chapter; rain and frost are the only signpost for which village the reader is in.

**5. The verdict is spoken, and it arrives after its consequences.** 17 and 18.
"If you had turned that winch first on Friday, you'd have been right about the houses too. You'd have drowned him, and you'd be me... A woman is dead of that, and it still isn't what I did." (18). For seventeen chapters the book refuses to solve its sum; its best thought is "She had the sum and nothing else" (12). Here a character adjudicates it in a paragraph of nearly two hundred words. Then "It was weather, and nobody had sent it. They left it there" closes the rain by narrator's ruling. And the scene comes late: 17 has already run Lesca to Tuesday and put the pillow back, so 18 has to say "her council's vote was two days off" to find its place.
*Do:* keep the two minutes ("Those two minutes are mine") and "Don't hand me your sum to sit behind." Cut the rest of that speech and the weather ruling. Move the steel-room scene ahead of 17, so that both women leave the room and then pay.

**6. The women speak one sentence.** 2, 4, 12, 16, 17, 18.
Six hard conversations end on a chore. Maïté: "There's a tile gone over the back bedroom" (2), "That back gutter's coming over again" (17). Marie-Jo: "Somebody has to stack them" (16). Arlette: "Get that side strapped, Maylis" (18). Coralie: "Wash your hands, Madame le maire" (17). Arlette says "I'm not asking why once" (16) and "I'm not asking where you were" (18). "I'll not" comes out of five mouths: Arlette, Madame Etcheto, Jeannot, Lesca, Maylis. "Don't give me the minutes", "Don't give me the measurement", "Don't give it me now", "Don't give me him for that". Any of these lines could be moved to another woman without a reader noticing.
*Do:* the chore exit belongs to Maïté, who has a roof, and the strapping to Coralie, who is a nurse. Take it from Arlette and Marie-Jo and give each a way of leaving that is hers. Keep "I'll not" for the two oldest speakers.

**7. The name grind in the shared-body chapters.** 6, 8, 9, 14.
"Lesca waited for it. Then Lesca got the left shoulder under the handle and drove with the legs." "Lesca did not answer her. Lesca pulled." (6). "Lesca moved the right hand. Lesca got it across the oilcloth" (8). "Lesca did not knock. Lesca walked Maylis back up the track." (9). Chapter 6 has "Lesca" 46 times and "Maylis" 43 in 2,570 words, a proper name every 29 words, and 22 sentences that begin "Lesca" and a verb. The pronoun is avoided for safety, and the price is a stammer in the four chapters that should move fastest.
*Do:* "she" for whoever holds the point of view in that chapter, the name only for the visitor, and the hands to carry the rest.

**8. The number the dilemma stands on does not hold.** 9, 11, 15.
"It was twenty-five minutes on foot to that hatch. The bank had ten." (11). The track is a kilometre (13, 15). Chapter 9 earns twenty-five by the cracked rib, "at the pace the rib allowed, which was a walk", and then says "It was twenty-five in 2014", when she had every rib. A kilometre is twelve minutes at a walk. Worse, 15 does it by van: "Three minutes out. Two to get him up a ladder. Three back. That was eight." Eight is inside ten, and in 2014 the van was already pointing down the track. Either that is the book's charge against Lesca, and nobody ever makes it, or it is a slip that a reader with a watch will catch at the worst moment.
*Do:* decide. If it is the charge, Maylis says it in the steel room in one line. If not, put the dip under water in the 2014 night so no van passes, and lengthen the track.

**9. One device for emphasis, and one number for everything.** Whole book.
"In sixteen years she had never ticked one of those boxes and left the other." (6). "In eighteen years beside that car Maylis had never seen it done." (6). "In thirty years at that counter Jeannot Castagnet had not left one." (7). "That chair had not asked the table a question in eleven years." (8). "For N years" occurs 27 times. Each is exact; by chapter 8 the reader hears it coming. Separately, "twelve" or "twelfth" appears 53 times and means the years, the twelfth of December, the twelfth commemoration, room twelve, the twelfth of June and a loto call: "The committee for the twelfth commemoration... would renew both lines before the twelfth." (2).
*Do:* keep the count of years for the three that turn something (the window, the tick, the half glass). In Lesca's village call the date "the Saturday" or "the ceremony".

**10. One simile.** Whole book; densest in 15.
"as a dog gets up" (1), "as a door is held" (6), "as a man votes" (10), "as things do that are filed" (11), "as a child is carried", "as a stage is", "as he would have carried a sack of maize to a hide", "as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another" (all 15). Twenty-seven comparisons of the form comma, "as", a customary action. It is the narrator's only figure.
*Do:* keep those where the custom belongs to the character ("as she priced a job", "as the cost of a motion is read out before the vote"). Cut the generic ones ("as people did", "as a stage is").

**Usage and consistency, one line each.**

- Chapter 11 calls the dyke "the bank" six times; every other chapter, and the same sentences in 00, say "dyke". 00 has "mended that dyke each spring", 11 "patched that bank each spring".
- Spelling is consistent British throughout (metre, grey, towards, learned, leaned). "Telephone" 67 times against one "phone", in Kévin's speech; leave it.
- "Madame le maire" is lower case everywhere, which the joke in 6 needs. No slips.
- "loto" first appears in 3 ("plays loto with the rest of the dead") and is not shown until 9. Gloss it at first use.
- "two fingers on the inside of the wrist" is given in full on nearly every occasion, fifteen "two fingers" in all. After 4, "two fingers" is enough.
- Coralie enters "in her tunic" seven times and "in her dressing gown" seven times.
- "the stroke that came back and crossed itself" appears word for word in 16 and 17.
- Lesca knows "forty registered letters" (2) a page after the reader has seen No. 41 (1). Correct, and it reads as an error.
- Eleven years (the landing) and twelve years (the flood) alternate inside Coralie's speeches in 12. Correct, and it reads as an error.
- The vote in 17: fifteen seats, the mayor suspended, eleven in favour and one abstention. Two councillors are unaccounted for.
- 1: "It came down, with the sound of a sack let down off a trailer." The comma splits the verb from its manner.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The shopping pad and Either**, from "I don't cook Either. There's no recipe for it" (1) through the two ticked boxes (5) and the one ticked box (6) to "That was Either, with the river in it" (18).
2. **Coralie's call log.** "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." (12), and the whole landing scene around it.
3. **Chapter 15 as it stands**: the clock times as paragraphs, "She got out, and went on", "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'", Kévin's "Five", and "Forty-one".
4. **The sentence at the microphone and what the square does with it.** "Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him." through "A child asked what the lady had done, and was told." (16).
5. **Larrère in the oven, both times**: the full impression in 1 and the one Coralie cannot finish in 18.
