# Critic report: Reader

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else opened but the brief.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** By the end of the second chapter I had a date, a place and a man. "If it kept its dates, the flood would be there on the night of the eleventh of December, with Jeannot Castagnet out at his tonne."
- **Tension: 7.** Chapters 13 to 15 are a 9. Chapters 7, 8 and 10 are a 5, because the pressure there is paper. "It was her second paper since nine."
- **Progression: 6.** A whole chapter in the other village brings back what chapter 1 told me. "She had had her day at Lesca's desk, and it had told her that the gate was Guy Larrère's twice over."
- **Comedy: 7.** A scene per chapter made me smile and three made me laugh aloud. "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade."
- **Characters: 8.** Coralie carries the book. "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them."
- **The switch between dark and comic: 9.** The running joke is tried the morning after the flood and dies in her mouth. "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the neck. 'The—'"
- **Prose: 7.** Exact, and stiff where two women share a body. "Lesca did not answer her. Lesca pulled."
- **The ending: 8.** It costs a death, a job, a marriage and a name in the village, and closes on an act, not a statement. "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it."
- **Clarity: 6.** I reread to keep the rules straight, and one rule arrives in the sentence that needs it. "what a woman had in her head came to the chair whether she sat in it or not."

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. I would have put it down once, between chapters 7 and 8, and picked it up again for Coralie.

**Pressed on a friend?** Not on the friend the bar describes. That reader stops in chapter 7. I would press it on a reader who will take slow going for a last third this good, and I would warn them about the middle.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below the bar, for this lens.** From chapter 11 to the end it is level or above. Before that it asks more of an ordinary reader than either comparison does.

**Below Crouch**

- *Ease.* A Crouch reader never rereads a sentence to find out what is physically happening. Here I held seven rules (lie still or push, who wakes first, who stays sitting, what Inguma counts, the right hand, the jaw for one word, no lying on your feet) and needed the recap that opens chapters 6, 8, 9 and 14.
- *The middle.* Crouch moves the premise every chapter. Between 30 November (chapter 6) and 8 December (chapter 10) the flood plot stands still: the gate is still Larrère's and Jeannot is still going down.
- *The world outside.* Crouch's premise has consequences beyond the room. This village has a mayor, an association and a prefect reached by post. No fire brigade, no neighbour who simply drives the children up the hill.

**Level with or above Crouch**

- *Fear for the marriage.* Coralie tells the two women apart with two fingers on a wrist, in one page. That is faster and better than the equivalent in Dark Matter.
- *The cost of the ending.* Higher than Dark Matter's and more exact.

**Against The Good Place**

- *Below* on how often I laughed. The comedy is dry and local. It made me smile for most of a chapter and laugh once.
- *Level* on turning an ethics problem into furniture: the shopping pad with two boxes, the gendarme's form that "needs a third box", Darrigrand's "It's four when you're the one counting."
- *Level in kind, smaller in size* on reversals that re-read what came before. There are two: Coralie has had the minute for twelve years, and the kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice.
- *Above* on the switch. Monsieur Daguerre saying "Here" to the roof of the van, and Kévin counting five fingers in the flood, are better than anything comparable in the series.

**What I did not understand**

- Why the rain comes twelve years late. The book asks twice and answers "It was weather".
- What the experiment did, and whether Dr Kessler matters. She never returns.
- Why Maylis pulls her wrist away from Coralie in chapters 4 and 5. "Maylis wanted it out from under those fingers." I was never told why.
- Why Larrère had the gate raised on the 23rd, the act the complaint and half of chapter 7 hang on.
- How Inguma keeps Lesca's minute in a village where nothing happened in 2014. I accepted it; I did not understand it.
- "Two more of Lesca's list." I did not know a council is elected from a list.
- On first reading, what was happening in the cab in chapter 14, and what day it was at the top of chapter 18.

**Where I was bored or skimming**

- Chapter 3, the association on the dyke.
- Chapter 7, from the dictated letter through Sarthou's form.
- Chapter 8, the council after Inguma has fetched Maylis.
- Chapter 11, the first page, which I had already read in 00.
- Chapter 17, the lieutenant and the vote.

**Where I could not stop**

- Chapter 4, from 02:51 to the motion.
- Chapter 6, from the soup to the stairs.
- Chapter 9, from the loto to the landing.
- Chapter 12, from the speech in Coralie's hand to the end.
- Chapter 13 from five o'clock, straight through 15 to "Forty-one", and on through 16 to the chairs.

**What I will remember in a month**

Kévin making five. "Here." Coralie taking a dead woman's pulse for the full half minute. "Eggs is what Either gets in this house." Larrère's window going all the way down. "Still in the box" becoming "gone for a walk". "And six. Bugger off." The grave.

I will not remember the rules of the chair, the left and right cards, Tastet, Hontaube or the association.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The middle runs the same ride three times and brings nothing back. Chapters 7 to 9.**

> "She had had her day at Lesca's desk, and it had told her that the gate was Guy Larrère's twice over."

Chapter 1 told me that: "Guy Larrère was also president of the landowners' association, which owned the sluice gate". Lesca rides (6), Maylis undoes it (7), Maylis rides (8), Lesca rides again (9). Chapter 9 disposes of three days in three sentences so the ride can restart. There are seven sittings of one body or another in the book and four of them fall here.

*What I would do:* one ride each way. Fold 7 and 8 into one chapter. Cut the steel-room recrimination that opens 7, which recaps 6. Make the day across yield the thing the flood plot then uses: the resolution Madame Ducasse reads names the prefect, so let Maylis come home with that and write to Pau herself, in her own hand. At present Lesca writes it and Maylis crosses a t.

**2. The hinge of the plot is one word with one sentence of motive. Chapter 13.**

> "'Either,' Maylis said. He waited for the rest of it, and there was none. 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said."

Jeannot is on the dyke, it is daylight, her hand is on the handle. The reason given is the sheet with Kévin's name on it. Twenty lines later she plans to do the same thing at three in the morning regardless: "If the dyke was going she would raise the gate with her own arms." The sheet costs the same at either hour. So I read the five o'clock refusal as the book needing the gate shut.

*What I would do:* give the true difference between the two hours. At five the bank has not moved, and she would be opening a river onto maize on the word of a woman in a steel room. Put that doubt on the page, and put the folder at the glass as chapter 10 does, so that I feel the weight before the word.

**3. The climax of the two-women story is explained, not shown. Chapter 14.**

> "Maylis was about to do what Lesca had done, for the same woman, to the same man."

This is the reversal the book has been building: each has become the other. It arrives as narrator's gloss inside an action sequence that already stacks four rules (sitting on Inguma counts as sitting, the ponies are counted, first to wake, the right hand). I followed it on the second reading.

*What I would do:* have the argument on their feet in the steel room, where neither can lie. Maylis says there that she will raise it with him in his cellar; let Lesca answer that to her face. Then the cab is a wheel, a gear lever and eight rings, with no paragraph of meaning between them.

**4. The Port was warned, saw the warning come true, and went to bed. Chapters 10, 13 and 15.**

> "*Tuesday 8th. At the line, on her day.*"

Nine people sign a petition on the strength of that. Three nights later every shutter is closed, a baby is asleep under the dyke, and Lafitte, who wrote the prophecy down, is "at his upstairs window". Lesca told Coralie "Have them out of their beds by two" and at four minutes to three they are in them. Larrère's "Red is when people are moved" covers the officials. It does not cover a mother.

*What I would do:* one paragraph at dusk on the Friday. Who took a child up the hill, who stayed, and what each said about the pad at the café. And one line that puts the fire brigade somewhere else that night.

**5. I did not know what Maylis wanted until chapter 13.**

> "She no longer wanted Lesca's Monday undone. She wanted a day at Lesca's desk, to learn how a gate was ordered over that man's head."

Lesca's want is in her own writing by chapter 4, with a bed and a name in it. Maylis's is announced by the narrator in chapter 7 and is procedural. For twelve chapters her acts are refusals made with one hand. I was with Coralie, and then with Lesca, before I was with her.

*What I would do:* answer the question chapter 5 raises and drops. Why does she want her wrist out from under Coralie's fingers? One scene inside that, early, and she has a want with an object.

**6. The secret is told seven times, and chapter 11 repeats the opening nearly word for word.**

> 00: "She laid her hand flat on the dyke beside the water, and the clay moved under her palm."
> 11: "She got out and put her hand flat on the bank beside it, and the clay moved under her palm."

The third use, in 15, is Maylis's own night and it lands. The second spends it. After 11 the ring is told again in 12, 14, 16, 17 (twice) and 18.

*What I would do:* start the memory in 11 at Larrère's call and cut to the van turning. Keep only what 00 did not have: the kitchen clock, the car in the yard of the home, the reflector. Drop the ring from Lesca's speech in 18.

**7. Rules are amended in the scene that needs them. Chapter 11.**

> "what a woman had in her head came to the chair whether she sat in it or not"

Chapter 5 gave me the contract in three points. This clause rewrites point one so that the whole backstory can be delivered while Lesca is absent.

*What I would do:* put her in the chair. She has pushed for two nights; on the third she has no push left, exactly as happened to Maylis in chapter 9. No new rule, and she knows it has been seen.

**8. The last steel-room scene hands out the verdict and the lessons, and steps backwards in time. Chapter 18.**

> "'Your Coralie gives orders,' Maylis said. '... Sit down when she tells you.'" / "'Answer her.'"

Chapter 17 ends on the Tuesday with the pillow carried back. Chapter 18 opens two days earlier, with "her council's vote was two days off", and I had to stop and work out the day. The two women then tell each other what each has learned, and Lesca tells Maylis what her night meant: "A woman is dead of that, and it still isn't what I did."

*What I would do:* keep the two minutes and "Drowned". Cut the exchange of advice and the question about the rain. Place the scene before the Tuesday material of 17.

**9. Too many named people, most of them twice. Chapter 8 above all.**

> "Monsieur Tastet, the first deputy, had the chair by the range, with his hat on his knee. Two more of Lesca's list sat beside him over bowls of coffee."

About twenty-five names in a short book, in two villages with different jobs in each. In chapter 8 I lost which Marie-Jo, which Kévin and which Sarthou I was looking at.

*What I would do:* give each double one fixed tag on re-entry in each village, as the weather does for the villages themselves. Take the names off Josiane, Nadine and two of the front row.

**10. The date says 2026 and the village runs on envelopes.**

> "The envelope from Pau said orange, and she read it through its window at eleven, on the mairie step, in the rain."

The same chapter has a text message and a screen that shows missed calls. A reader in 2026 asks why a flood warning comes by post and why nobody rings an emergency number.

*What I would do:* let Pau's warning reach Larrère's telephone and have him sit on it. It is in character and it closes the hole.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 15, whole.** The clock times, "Here", the man who wants the bins out, Kévin's five, the pulse, "Forty-one".
2. **Coralie's two fingers on the wrist**, and the landing in chapter 9 where she finds Maylis in one hand and sends Lesca down the stairs.
3. **"Still in the box, were you?"** and what the village replaces it with in chapter 18.
4. **The oven door.** Coralie doing Larrère into it in chapter 1, and failing to in chapter 18.
5. **Jeannot Castagnet's numbered answers**: the five o'clock call ending "And six. Bugger off", letter No. 42 brought by hand, and the second shovel.
