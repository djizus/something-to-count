## 2. Against the comparisons (Characters)

Above Crouch. Below The Good Place. Short of the bar by half a point.

**Above Crouch.** In Dark Matter the wife is what the hero is afraid of losing, and she chooses at the end because he lets her. Here Coralie refuses to be the thing two women compete for. She has her own want, kept behind a fruit bowl since March, and she acts on it: "I posted the form today, and I posted it for me." (9) Neither Coralie is a prize, and one of them leaves. Crouch has nothing like that. His villagers and colleagues are furniture; this book has six people outside the triangle that a reader would know by one line of speech (Jeannot, Madame Etcheto, Kévin, Larrère, Sarthou, Arlette).

**Below The Good Place.** That series lets you inside Eleanor's wanting from the first minute, and her being wrong is the comedy. This book's heroine is defined by not saying what she wants, and the book mostly keeps the reader out with everybody else. The consequence is plain on the page: the reader, like Coralie, "would have this one again", meaning Lesca. Lesca is the better company in every scene they share. In a book of two versions of one person, the one who holds eleven chapters of nineteen is the one the reader would trade.

**Level with The Good Place** on people each being wrong in their own way. Maylis's Either, Lesca's sum, Larrère's window, Jeannot going down "because you've said it", Coralie not asking "because you'd have told me". Five distinct ways of failing, none borrowed from another.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. The central choice has nobody in it (15, 18). Structural.

> "If she went back she turned the handle on him, as Lesca had. If she went on, the river had the Port.
>
> She got out, and went on."

This is the moment the book exists for, and it is two geographical sentences and a verb. Maylis is walking away from a bank with her wife under it, towards a man who has insulted her twice that day. Coralie appears in the paragraph as a clause of location ("the home, with Coralie in it"). What Maylis wants at that second, whether she is obeying Lesca's five words, refusing to be Lesca, or simply unable to turn a handle on a man she has heard swear at her, is not on the page.

It is worse because the only moment where Maylis chooses Coralie is narrated from inside Lesca's head: "Maylis wanted the gate. She meant to raise it now, with the man still in his cellar at the end of the track, because Coralie was under that bank." (14) And the meaning of what she did at the dip is then supplied by Lesca: "You rang nine times. You walked his track at dusk, and you ran it in the night. A woman is dead of that, and it still isn't what I did." (18) Maylis's own reading is "I'd sign your sum."

So the heroine's decisive act is prompted by the other woman, performed without interior, and interpreted afterwards by the other woman.

Do: write the dip from inside. Four to six sentences in her pricing idiom, with Coralie named as what she is leaving, and the reason she goes on stated as hers, even if the reason is a bad one. Then in chapter 18 let Maylis say why she went on, and let Lesca answer it, not the reverse. Cut "I'd sign your sum".

### 2. Maylis is excused (13, 18). Structural.

> "She had her weight on the handle, and she could have turned it." (13)
>
> "'The van in the dip was mine. So was saying both.'" (18)

Madame Etcheto dies of five o'clock on Friday. At that hour Jeannot was on the bank, the handle was on the shaft, it was daylight, and Maylis said Either. In the one room where the book says nobody can lie, she lists her faults and leaves that one out. The only person who says it to her is a neighbour with a pram: "At five on Friday it was light, and Castagnet was up on the bank at your van window... You had it on your belt then." Coralie does not say it. Lesca does not say it. Maylis does not say it.

Against that single accusation the last chapter gives her three absolutions: Lesca's speech, Jeannot's letter 42, and Jeannot's shovel. That is the softening the brief asks about. Lesca is made to say "I drowned him" into a microphone. Maylis is never made to say what she did.

Do: Maylis says it in the steel room, first, before the van and before "both": at five she had him on the bank and her weight on the handle, and she said Either, for the boy. Lesca does not absolve it. Let Coralie's charge in the kitchen be that hour, not the telephone call.

### 3. The reader is shown Maylis swallowing an answer about a winch, never about her wife (whole book). Structural.

> "She had her answer ready, and it was the pawl. Six men turned to hear.
>
> 'Either,' she said." (3)

That is how Either should work on the page: we see the answer, and then we see it not said. It is done once more, in chapter 5: "The getting out was hers. Maylis could hear what that would be, said across a table to a woman in a tunic." After that the book stops. For the rest of it we get Either from outside, as Coralie gets it. Why her wrist pulls away from Coralie's two fingers is only ever told from Lesca's side ("Maylis wanted it out from under those fingers", 4). What she feels on the stairs with her hand on the newel post is "more afraid of her own stairs" and no more. She spends an hour locking a house with one door and the reader is not let in on the hour.

A woman who conceals her wants from other people is a character. A woman whose wants are concealed from the reader is a gap, and the other woman fills it.

Do: three more places, each two sentences, where the reader has the answer she does not give Coralie. The soup tick (6), the box room (9), "It's twenty minutes to Bayonne" (9). Do not give her speeches. Give the reader what she holds back.

### 4. The rule that nobody can lie in the steel room does the women's honesty for them (5, 7, 10).

> "On our feet we can talk, and neither of us can lie." (5)
>
> "Neither of them could lie, standing in that room." (7)

"I'd have gone up" is the hardest line Lesca has, and the rule takes it from her: she says it because the room makes her. The same in chapter 10: "I can't stand here and say that I'll go, because in here it would have to be true." The two leads never once decide to tell each other the truth. And the rule is not kept: Maylis says "Either" in that room in chapter 5 and Lesca lets a question go by unanswered in the same scene.

It also has Lesca announcing a rule she has had no chance to learn. She has stood up in that room for ninety seconds.

Do: delete the rule. Lesca is the one who answers; that is her character, and it needs no physics. "I'd have gone up" then belongs to her.

### 5. Lesca's Coralie offers the pillow for a bowl of soup (8).

> "Do you know what I'd give for a woman at this table who sat when she was told? I'd carry my pillow back across that landing tonight. I'd keep that one in the house."

Four chapters later the same woman explains the landing: "I thought I could have the one without the other, if I kept a landing between." (12) And in 17: "That's eleven years of wanting a thing that wasn't you." Three reasons for one landing, and the first of them is the weakest: eleven years of a call log on a telephone do not lift because somebody sat down to eat. The speech is there so that each Coralie prefers the visitor. It is the pattern speaking, and it cheapens the scene in chapter 12 and the hour she takes in 17.

Do: keep "I'd keep that one in the house". Cut the pillow from chapter 8. It is offered once in the book, in 17, and then it costs what it should.

### 6. The boy she said Either for is never accounted for (13, 18).

> "If she turned it now, she sent the boy to prison herself, for a flood that had not come." (13)

Larrère's terms were that if the gate moved, the letter was dated and Kévin's sheet went to the prosecutor. The gate moved a hand. The letter is dated. The sheet is not mentioned again. Kévin tows her van: "Four winters you looked the other way for nothing. I can do a morning." He does not know she traded the daylight opening for him, and the reader does not know whether the trade held. This is the one fact that decides whether five o'clock was cowardice or a price, and it is dropped.

Do: Larrère, in the silt, shows or pockets the second sheet. One line. And decide whether Kévin is to know.

### 7. Coralie is never seen and never in danger (15, and throughout).

> "Coralie stood in the doorway with the list on its cord and a torch in her fist. Her tunic was dark to the hip."

Both women's nights turn on Coralie being under that bank. Lesca cannot say whether she drowned a man for her. Yet in the flood Coralie is the most competent person in the building and the water is at her shins. The reader is afraid for Jeannot, who is in a dry hole, and not for her.

She also has no body. In nineteen chapters she has a tunic, two fingers, and once "her hair flat on one side". The single sensuous fact about her is Lesca's: "They were warm, and rough at the tips from the soap at the home." (4) Maylis, who has slept beside her for sixteen years, never notices anything about her. Nobody in this book is looked at as a woman from outside, and that is to its credit; but a reader cannot fear for a marriage to someone they cannot picture.

Do: one moment in the old wing where the water has her (the door of twelve, the ramp). And two physical facts about Coralie that are Maylis's.

### 8. Maylis has no mother until the last chapter (18).

> "'I'm not asking where you were,' she said. 'I've been told.'"

Arlette has three scenes in Lesca's village and is one of the best people in the book ("I never voted for her to be in here"). In Maylis's village, where she still has her ponies and her grazing and is the one person plainly better off, she walks through once with a mare. Maylis says the ponies' names to herself to stay awake, and we have never seen her with the woman who owns them. Lesca is given a mother, a council, a file and a Coralie. Maylis is given Coralie. It is part of why she is thinner.

Do: one scene with Arlette on the commons before the flood. The Sunday of the chrysanthemums has room.

### 9. After the confession, the village queues (16).

> "Monsieur Tastet found her among the chairs."
>
> "Monsieur Darrigrand had come up from the plots with his broom on his shoulder."

The sentence is said. Then the young man from Pau, the lieutenant, Marie-Jo, Tastet, Arlette, Darrigrand, the young man again and Madame Etcheto come up one at a time and each delivers a verdict. Eight in a row, and Lesca receives each standing still. The villagers stop being people and become a chorus. Meanwhile the one person whose reaction matters gets: "Coralie did not look down. Her hands stayed on the handles of the chair."

Do: keep Marie-Jo's chairs, Darrigrand's counting and Madame Etcheto's question. Cut the lieutenant to two lines and move Tastet's summons to the Tuesday.

### 10. Everybody's feeling arrives as a count of years (throughout).

> "I've held hands for a living since I was nineteen." (5, Coralie)
>
> "I've kept this counter thirty years." (6, Josiane)
>
> "I've stood at that stone for twelve years asking who was meant to go round the tonnes." (17, Maïté)
>
> "In thirty years at that counter Jeannot Castagnet had not left a glass." (7, narrator)
>
> "In eighteen years beside that car Maylis had never seen it done." (6, narrator)

The men are told apart by a habit of speech: Jeannot numbers, Larrère is brief, Sarthou requires, Kévin asks three questions to a sentence. The women (both Coralies, Maïté, Arlette, Marie-Jo, Josiane) share one sentence, a tenure followed by a verdict, and the narrator uses it too. By chapter 12 it is the author's voice in every mouth at the moment of most feeling.

Do: leave it to Coralie, whose sixteen years it is, and to Lesca's files. Take it from Josiane, Maïté and the narrator.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie on the landing (9).** "That's her. In this hand." through "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." through "You'll go down those stairs." It is where she stops being what two women want and becomes the person who decides.
2. **The call log (12).** "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." and "Because you'd have told me." Every word of that scene, including that Coralie does not touch her.
3. **Jeannot's voice, end to end.** "And six. Bugger off." (11), "It's Jeannot. Jean's for the stone." (9), and letter 42 brought by hand: "It's four sides, and it doesn't thank you."
4. **Kévin's hand.** "Five." in the yard (15), "as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another", and "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything as much." (18)
5. **The oven.** The impersonation through a gap the width of two fingers in chapter 1, and the same joke begun and abandoned in chapter 18. It is the marriage, and then the end of it, in one prop.
