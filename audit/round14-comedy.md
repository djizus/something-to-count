# Round 14: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** A whole village against one man, and a marriage on top of it, put in one line: "'The gate, or Castagnet? Say which, Maylis. For once.'"
- **Tension: 7.5.** Chapters 13 to 15 hold; the middle leaks it through repetition. "The bank had six minutes, and on foot she could reach one of the two."
- **Progression: 6.5.** The crossing is run five times and announced each time. "It went as it had on Saturday."
- **Comedy: 6.5.** Dry, exact, and thin on the page: about twenty real laughs in the book, nearly all in one register. "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade.'"
- **Characters: 7.5.** Coralie is the best thing in it. "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'"
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** This is where the book meets the bar. After the sole of a wader under black water, a living man on the telephone: "'And six. Bugger off.'"
- **Prose: 7.** Clean and controlled, and stiff as a logbook whenever two women share a body. "Lesca waited for it. Then Lesca got the left shoulder under the handle and drove with the legs."
- **The ending: 7.5.** It costs: a death, a job, a marriage. The running gag becomes the verdict. "'That was Either, with the river in it.'"
- **Clarity: 6.** The rules of the crossing have to be re-read. "'If the other is still sitting in this chair when that happens, the sitter goes home with the waker, and wakes inside the waker's body.'"

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. Would I press it on a friend? Yes, with a warning that chapters 4 and 5 are spent on rules and chapter 6 is where it starts being funny.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below The Good Place as comedy. Level with it on the switch, and on making the moral problem funny without shrinking it.** Crouch is not a comic writer, so he sets no bar here.

What puts it below:

1. **One cadence.** The Good Place has four kinds of wrongness in a room and four kinds of noise: crude, anxious, grand, stupid. Here the people are each wrong in their own way (Larrère: nothing is ever his department; Jeannot: everything is a numbered grievance; Maylis: Either; Lesca: the sum), but almost all of them speak the same way, in a flat short sentence that shuts the paragraph. It earns a smile every time and a laugh rarely.
2. **Set pieces stop at the first laugh.** A Good Place episode takes its comic situation up three steps. This book takes its best one, a verge-cutter driving the mayor's body through a working day, and tells it in a hundred lines of summary. The village-hall loto gets thirty.
3. **The narrator explains the rules straight.** The Good Place makes rule-giving the joke: somebody is told how the place works and gets it wrong. This book does that once, superbly, in chapter 5 ("'Those in favour.' 'There's two of us.'"), and then has the narrator restate the same rules six more times with no joke attached.

What puts it level:

- The trolley problem as paperwork. The stone that needs a verb ("'It reads that she opened him'"), the timer that rings on the widow, the form that "needs a third box", the motion "That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it." None of these makes the drowned man smaller.
- The running gag that turns into the cause of death. "The council has taken note of the winch" in chapter 3 is "'I know you did. They took note'" in a flooded doorway in chapter 15. "Either" on a shopping pad is "Both" down a telephone with the water at the back door.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Chapter 8. The best comic situation in the book is told in summary.** Chapter 6 gives Lesca a full day in Maylis's body and it is the funniest chapter. The reverse ride, the commune's girl in the mayor's sash, is the richer half, and it gets seven beats in a hundred lines. Its own promise is not kept: "She had one hand to keep that with, and in her mouth a woman who said Either." Maylis never says Either with the mayor's mouth. The darkest joke in the book, a laugh at a dead man's complaint during his minute of silence, is explained before it happens ("In the seventeenth he billed the commune for two ruts its tractor had left in his track") although chapter 6 already planted it ("'That's my seventeenth. The commune was billed.'"), and then reported in one line: "Maylis laughed, with Lesca's mouth, a table's width from the widow."
*Do:* give chapter 8 the length of chapter 6. Play the minute of silence in scene and cut the explanation. Have the council put a question to the chair and get "Either", and have Madame Ducasse minute it. Keep the paragraph where Larrère speaks without a bell word for word. Pay for the length out of faults 3 and 9.

**2. Chapter 9. The loto is thrown away, and three later scenes echo a scene the reader never had.** The dead play loto "as in the village hall"; their caller gives "the numbers, whole and then in parts"; the survivors are counted in the same hall under the same strip lights. The live loto that all of this rests on is thirty lines, and its caller is never heard. "Madame Etcheto had won a duck and kept both hands on it" is all the comedy it is given. Chapter 13 then leans on a moment that was never shown: "'He's been one off the strimmer since the loto.'"
*Do:* play the loto. Let the caller call a number whole and then in its parts. Let Monsieur Daguerre be one off the strimmer on the page and call "Here" for the first time there. Then the hall of the dead in 10 and 14, and "Forty-one" in 15, land on something the reader sat through and laughed at.

**3. Chapters 6, 7, 9, 10, 14. The farce stops to check its paperwork.** Who drives whom is laid down in chapter 5 and then restated by the narrator at the head or foot of nearly every chapter after. Chapter 6 opens with four paragraphs of it: "Lesca had stayed sitting in the chair, so she had come home in Maylis's body, and she had nearly all of it." Chapter 9 closes on it: "If Lesca was still sitting in the chair on any of those mornings, Lesca would come home in her." Worst is chapter 14, where the best image in the book, "Then she sat down on Inguma", is footnoted at once: "While she kept Inguma down, it could not get up to fetch her home. And with Inguma under her she was still the one sitting in the chair, so that when Maylis woke, Lesca would wake in her." Chapter 17 does the same job in nine words and gets the laugh: "'You sat on it. It's the one that does the sitting.'"
*Do:* state the rules once, in the chair meeting of chapter 5, and cut every later restatement. Where a reminder is needed, let a character get the rule wrong and be corrected.

**4. Throughout. Everybody is funny in the same sentence.** "'Those are my boots.'" "'It'll have sunk.'" "'He can be asked.'" "'I've set it.'" "'I'm trained. I know what a coincidence is.'" "'It's four when you're the one counting.'" Coralie, Arlette, Maïté, Marie-Jo, Madame Etcheto and Monsieur Darrigrand could trade these lines. Only two people in the book are funny by excess: Jeannot when he numbers, and Josiane in her one speech ("'and it's adultery whichever, and that one lives with a nurse'"). Nobody laughs aloud after chapter 8.
*Do:* keep the dry closer for Coralie and Lesca, who own it. Give two others a different music and let them run: Josiane at the counter in every café scene, and one talker at the home (Bixente and Nadine have no lines to speak of). Three sentences of somebody who cannot stop is worth ten more closers.

**5. Chapters 11 to 18. The café goes off stage and the last third lives on callbacks.** The chorus of chapters 1, 5, 6 and 10 (Lafitte's pad headed *Madame le maire*, the dealer, Josiane) has its last scene in chapter 10. After the flood it is a voice through a door: "Somebody inside said that the commune's girl had gone for a walk." The village that laughed never has to face the reader. Josiane, who was sure the flowers meant adultery in the Castagnet kitchen, was right about the kitchen and wrong about the visitor, and never learns it; Maïté walks past with her suitcase and "nobody came out of it". The pad has no last entry.
*Do:* one short scene at the café door in chapter 18, as the bus leaves. Put the "gone for a walk" line in the dealer's mouth, and show what Lafitte does with the page.

**6. Chapters 6, 7, 13, 17, 18. Ironies explained after the reader has them.** The folder and the two bowls on the drainer are planted cleanly in chapter 5. Then: "Lesca asked because she did not know whom he meant. Maylis knew. On Sunday she had seen his folder on Maïté Castagnet's dresser" (6), and "It was the kitchen that frightened him" (7). Kévin's five in the flood is perfect in chapter 15 and is glossed in 18: "'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything as much.'" Also "She had a word for that" (13) and "He was left out there" (17).
*Do:* cut all six. Each joke or blow is complete without them.

**7. Chapters 00 and 1. The book does not say it is funny for nine hundred words.** A solemn prologue, then a paralysed woman and a demon, then the steel room, then a glossary: "She was the one outdoor employee of the village of Peyrebarthe, which on paper was a commune", with the mairie, the mayor, the landowners' association, the sluice gate and the winch key in the next four sentences. The first laugh, the postman who cannot find the mayor, comes after all of it and does the same introductions better.
*Do:* let the postman scene introduce Larrère, the mairie and the yellow waterproof, and move the association and the winch to where Larrère says "The winch." Get one small laugh into the bedroom before the scene break: Dr Kessler's "counted one of her, paid her, given her a sandwich" is already there and is buried mid-paragraph.

**8. Chapter 15. One gag sits on the fact the reader most needs.** Jeannot arrives chest-deep, out of breath, and delivers a numbered speech: "'I've three things to say about it. First, I counted, as you do, by the click'". This is the moment the book reveals that the winch everyone took note of has failed and the gate is up a hand. The numbering was spent at full strength four chapters earlier, and here it turns the fact into a turn. A second "Here" from Monsieur Daguerre ten lines after the first has the same effect.
*Do:* strip the numbering from this speech and keep its last two lines ("'I told them in 2011 that handle wanted looking at. I put it in number six.'"). Cut the second "Here" in the yard.

**9. Chapters 7, 10, 13. The folder threat is staged three times without variation.** Running gags in this book change at each use; the threat does not. Blank sheet, undated letter, Kévin's name, in an office, against a car window, on a passenger seat: "'I have a letter in here in your writing, with a gap where the date goes.'" The night of 2014 is likewise recited in chapters 2, 5, 11, 12 and 16. The third folder scene and the chapter 5 recital are where the room for faults 1 and 2 is found.
*Do:* cut the chapter 13 threat to its last exchange ("'Is that advice?' 'No. That's the file.'") and cut Lesca's chapter 5 account of the flood to the stone, the date and the nets.

**10. Chapters 12, 13, 16. Beats that misfire.** The Hontaube call peaks at "'Then it should have gone last Monday'" and "'You'll be ours'", then runs four exchanges on to a kicker that does not connect: "'It's a long way to bring it.'" "He did not count the ninth" (12): no ninth strip has been laid. "At eleven he had shaken her hand, and he left without it" (16) reads as the prefect leaving a hand behind.
*Do:* end the Hontaube call on "You'll be ours." Fix or cut the other two.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The end of chapter 11.** The cellar, the wader, then nine rings and Jeannot's six points down the telephone. "She listened to all of it."
2. **The verb for the stone, chapter 2.** "Opened what? There's a name over it. It reads that she opened him", and the timer ringing as the widow opens her mouth.
3. **The sitting in the steel room, chapter 5.** "'Then it's carried, or it's tied, and in a tie the chair decides.' 'There's nobody in the chair.'" And then what gets up into it.
4. **The whole chain of Either.** Eggs, the pawl she had ready and did not say, both boxes ticked, the jaw seized to say it, Sarthou's summons, the handle lifted off the shaft, "Both", and Coralie's last word on it. Every use changes it. Leave all of them.
5. **Coralie at the oven, chapter 1 and chapter 18.** The impression through a two-finger gap, and the morning she bends to do it again and gets as far as "The—".
