# Critic report: Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. About 42,400 words, 87 scenes. Nothing else was opened.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.5.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." (2) The sum is stated early and never blurs.
- **Tension: 7.** "She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it went." (15) Chapters 11 to 15 earn an 8. Chapters 7 to 10 run on procedure and earn a 6.
- **Progression: 6.5.** "It went as it had on Saturday." (6) The narrator says it for me: chapters 5 to 9 run the same loop five times, one woman riding the other and being fetched.
- **Comedy: 7.5.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade.'" (7)
- **Characters: 7.5.** "'Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you, and that was me.'" (1)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'" (15) The running joke walks into the flood and is answered straight.
- **Prose: 7.** The sentence is an 8: "The loudspeaker on the bank road said drowned him when the square had finished hearing it." (16) The paragraph is a 6: "The body was shared out as it had been on the other days." (14)
- **The ending: 7.5.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (18)
- **Clarity: 6.** "By Lesca's own first point, what a woman had in her head came to the chair whether she sat in it or not, and Maylis was given that night entire." (11) A book that has to cite its own clause numbers has a rule problem.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. The one place I would have put it down is the paragraph in chapter 2 that begins "So there were two Peyrebarthes."

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who reads for sentences, yes. On the Crouch reader the bar names, not yet.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch, by about a point, on what his prose is for.**

- Crouch states a rule once and then breaks it in action. This book states its rule in chapter 5 and restates it in the narrator's voice in chapters 4, 6, 9, 11, 12, 14 (three times) and 15. The phrase "stayed sitting" or "still sitting" occurs 13 times.
- A Crouch chapter opens inside a sensation. Here 16 of 19 chapters and 57 of 87 scenes open on a clock or calendar stamp. It reads as a log.
- Crouch makes a reader afraid for a marriage because the narrator says what the husband feels. Here the narrator never says what Maylis feels for Coralie except as an inventory: "In sixteen years Maylis had never got that laugh out of her." (6) The withholding is a style, and its price is the fear.

**Above Crouch on the sentence and the object.** The reflector "screwed on crooked, burning red" (11, 13), the shopping pad's boxes returning as the gendarme's form with "two boxes" (17), and the call log read "as she would have read it off a chart" (12) are things his books do not have.

**Level with The Good Place in talk, below it in trust.** Each villager is wrong in his own syntax: Larrère through a few centimetres of window, Jeannot numbering his grievances, Sarthou billing the commune for his own labour, Madame Etcheto reporting a care assistant for doubling the stars. The reversal re-reads what came before: the minute on the clock radio is the minute of the call (11). But that series never explains a joke or a reversal after making it, and this book does both (faults 7 and 8).

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. The rule book is read aloud again every time it applies (4, 6, 9, 11, 12, 14, 15)

> "It went as it had on Saturday. Lesca had stayed sitting in the chair, so she had come home in Maylis's body, and she had nearly all of it." (6)
>
> "While she kept Inguma down, it could not get up to fetch her home. And with Inguma under her she was still the one sitting in the chair, so that when Maylis woke, Lesca would wake in her." (14)
>
> "Lesca had kept Inguma from fetching her, and the one it cannot fetch belongs at that table. If she sat down, she would be found dead in her bed." (14)

Lesca's three points in chapter 5 are the rule, said in character and funny. Everything after is the narrator repeating her, often in the same words ("had stayed sitting, and came home in her", 9). The cost is highest in chapter 14, where the climax stops three times for a clause of the rules while a telephone is ringing.

**Do:** keep chapter 5's three points, the alarm plan in chapter 7 (it is a plan, not a recap) and Lesca's "Nil" speech in chapter 10 (it adds the price). Cut or reduce to one clause: the motion paragraph's "If Lesca was still sitting" sentence (4), the second paragraph of chapter 6, "Anybody could send Inguma off to count" through "a second pair of hands" (9), the last paragraph of chapter 9, "By Lesca's own first point" (11), "Her plan was made" through "stand on that cross" (12), the three passages in chapter 14 quoted above and "The body was shared out", and the paragraph in chapter 15 beginning "Maylis had woken meaning to raise the gate", which re-explains five words the reader heard two pages earlier.

### 2. The three decisive moments are argued by the narrator instead of happening (13, 14, 18)

> "If she turned it now, she sent the boy to prison herself, for a flood that had not come. If she waited until the bank moved, she would know, and by then this man would be under his hatch. She could not do the one, and she would not look at the other. She had a word for that." (13)
>
> "But for as long as she stayed there were two of them in the cab, and they wanted different things. One had the wheel and the other had the gear lever, and the van would stand where it was." (14)
>
> "'No. You won't sign it, and I'll say why at length.'" … "'A woman is dead of that, and it still isn't what I did.'" (18)

Everywhere else the book decides by gesture: a hand locked on a newel post, a spoon tipped back into a bowl. At the platform, in the cab and in the last meeting it switches to a written brief. The first is the "Either" that the whole ending is charged to, and it arrives with a label on it. The second explains a stalled van the reader has just watched stall. The third has one character deliver the book's verdict on the other.

**Do:** at the platform, go from Kévin's cap ("waited to be told what he had done wrong") straight to "'Either,' Maylis said." In the cab, keep "She felt it lift, and kept it down" and "Not one of them would drive a van", and cut between them to the ring count. In 18, keep "No embankment made me ring once" and "You rang nine times", and cut the rest of Lesca's paragraph.

### 3. The last meeting of the two women is out of place and opens the final chapter with an apology for the calendar (17, 18)

> "For Lesca it was the night after the speech. Her Monday at Bayonne and her Tuesday before the council were still to come, and the pillow was still in the box room." (18)

Chapter 17 carries Lesca through to Tuesday night and closes her story on the pillow. Chapter 18 then rewinds to Sunday at 02:51 and the narrator has to explain where in chapter 17 we are. The scene the book has been building towards starts with bookkeeping.

**Do:** move the chair scene to its own place, between the landing scene and "On Monday at nine" in chapter 17, told from Lesca's side. The quoted gloss is deleted, chapter 17 still ends on the pillow, and chapter 18 opens on "At first light on Sunday Maylis walked down into the Port", which is a better opening.

### 4. Chapter 2 hands over the premise as a deduction, in a briefing voice (2; also 1)

> "So there were two Peyrebarthes. They had been one village until the afternoon in June 2014 when she sat in the steel room and one lamp of two came on. In the other village the other lamp had come on." … "The forty letters were the proof." (2)

This is the one thing a high-concept book should let the reader find. Lesca reaches it from one half-dream, eight paragraphs into her first chapter, and the narrator states it as settled. The scene that proves it, the card that reads *left* in her own hand, comes two chapters later, after the conclusion it ought to produce. The voice changes with it: the narrator stops reporting and starts summarising, and does so again at the chapter's end ("She had been wrong. It was 2026 there as well").

Chapter 1 has the same voice in small: "Duck come down at night only to standing water, and a hand of it, the depth of a palm laid flat, is enough." The narrator steps into the present tense to give a natural-history note.

**Do:** in chapter 2, stop at "he had been dead for nearly twelve years" and cut the next two paragraphs. Let "the roof was in the other village" on the ladder be the first time the phrase appears. The card in chapter 4 then does the proving. In chapter 1, give the duck sentence to Jeannot, who would say it.

### 5. After the confession, eight callers queue up to give a verdict each (16)

> "Monsieur Tastet found her among the chairs." … "Her mother had not gone home." … "Monsieur Darrigrand had come up from the plots with his broom on his shoulder."

The confession and the square's silence are the best pages in the book. Then the young man from Pau, the lieutenant, Marie-Jo, Tastet, Arlette, Darrigrand, the young man again and Madame Etcheto each arrive, speak in their idiom and leave. Eight scenes of one shape and one length turn the silence into a reception line. Before it, the reaction shot is a roll-call of a dozen named people, one sentence each.

**Do:** merge the medal case into the suspension at two o'clock. Move the lieutenant's lines to the head of the Bayonne scene in 17. Keep Marie-Jo, Tastet, Arlette and Darrigrand. In the roll-call, keep the front row, Coralie, Tastet turning the sheet over, and Larrère's hat; cut the camera operator, Kévin, Sarthou and the priest.

### 6. Scenes open like log entries (throughout)

> "At eight Lesca stood Maylis on the platform" / "At nine Guy Larrère drew up" / "At noon Lesca walked Maylis into the café" / "At seven there was soup" (6)

57 of 87 scenes open on a time or a date, and 83 narrative sentences begin "At". The weather tag is useful, since rain means one village and frost the other. The clock is habit. The exceptions show what is being lost: "Somebody was on the gate platform with a torch in his teeth." (13)

**Do:** keep the weather. In two scenes out of three, open on the person or the thing and put the hour in the second sentence, or leave it out when the previous scene fixed the day.

### 7. The narrator explains a line the scene has just delivered (6, 7, 8, 9, 11, 15)

> "He had asked her twice now about a kitchen, and not once about a river. It was the kitchen that frightened him." (7)
>
> "It was true, word for word, and it was meant to pass for Maylis." (9)
>
> "Lesca understood it. At home that man held Maylis's letter and a boy's name, and she could not put a bell on him even here." (8)
>
> "It would leave Coralie with the one who said Either, and Coralie had just said which of them she would have back." (6)
>
> "He had come out of a dry cellar half an hour before, by the neck, and had been told that the gate was to be opened on him." (15, before "I was at school with his son")

Each follows a line of dialogue or a gesture that had already done the work.

The heaviest case is the central doubt. Whether she opened the gate for forty houses or for Coralie is put by the narrator in 11 ("nothing in the night as it came told Maylis for which of the two she had turned the handle"), by Coralie in 12, by Lesca trying both sentences aloud in 12, and by Maïté in 17. The narrator's version is the flattest and comes first, so Coralie's arrives second-hand. Likewise the minute on the telephone screen is shown in 11 ("The screen said 02:51"), explained in 11 ("she knew now why it kept the hour it did"), and said again by Coralie in 12.

**Do:** cut the five quoted glosses. In 11, cut the "for which of the two" sentence so that Coralie is the first to ask it, and reduce the 02:51 paragraph to its last two sentences: "It was not her hour that it came at. She had never had one."

### 8. The book points at its own echoes (13, 15, 16, 18)

> "She put her hand flat on the clay beside the sheet, as she had seen it done" (15)
>
> "It buzzed, twice, against his chest, as it had on the dashboard of a tractor under the plane trees." (15)
>
> "He did it without asking, as he had once got into her bucket." (15)
>
> "Nobody had given her this one. It was her own night, and it was happening." (13)
>
> "She went back to the landing of the Saturday before, and the woman in Maylis's coat who had asked her across and been sent down the stairs." (18)

The echoes are the craft: the hand on the clay, the reflector, the timer, the two fingers on the wrist. Each "as it had" tells the reader he was not trusted to hear them. The last quotation also misreads on first pass as Coralie walking to the landing. The corridor recap in 15 ("She had stood in this corridor once before" through "been heard") is the same fault at paragraph length, placed one door before the body.

**Do:** delete the pointers. Cut the corridor recap to "The two of them had got the giggles here, and been heard."

### 9. A small set of constructions does too much of the work (throughout)

- **The ledger of years.** "years" appears 59 times. "In sixteen years she had never ticked one of those boxes", "In eighteen years beside that car Maylis had never seen it done", "In sixteen years Maylis had never got that laugh", "In eighteen years Maylis had put three letters", "In thirty years at that counter Jeannot Castagnet had not left a glass". Every reversal is announced by the same drum. Keep two.
- **The name as subject.** "Lesca" appears 44 times in chapter 6 and 29 times in chapter 14, mostly as the subject: "Lesca waited for it. Then Lesca got the left shoulder under the handle" … "Lesca kept the eyes on it" … "Lesca let the gate down". "The left hand" and "the mouth" already say who is driving, so "she" would serve in half of these.
- **Sentence openings.** Of about 2,100 narrative sentences, 987 begin with She, He, It, They or a woman's name. Chapter 15 has 19 sentences that begin "It was": "It was not loud. It was the sound of a lorry" … "It was black. It was still in there".
- **The retort "That's".** 37 times, from every mouth: "That's Either again", "That's the door", "That's Inguma", "That's her", "That's the file", "That's few", "That's what you'd keep". It is Coralie's and Jeannot's by right. When Kévin, Madame Etcheto and Larrère use it too, the voices close up.
- **Measuring by objects.** "the width of" 14 times (a door four times, a house twice, two fingers twice, a finger, a hand, a date, a barn), "the height of" 12, "a hand" as a measure about 15. "A hand of water" is the book's own unit and should stay; the rest dilute it.
- **"The one" plus noun, 49 times.** "the one outdoor employee", "the one chair", "the one seat", "the one form", "the one man", "the one track".
- **Plain verbs worn smooth.** "stood" 144, "took" 106, "looked" 85. People "look at" each other in place of reacting. In the roll-call in 16 it carries most of the sentences.
- **Full names.** "Guy Larrère" in full 36 times out of 68, "Jeannot Castagnet" 21, with no rule for which form is used.

### 10. Consistency

- **How often it comes.** "on most nights for twelve years" (1, 2, 4) against "on every night since" (11).
- **The council.** Seven and seven vote in chapter 8, which is fourteen. Chapter 17 has eleven in favour, one abstention and Lesca not voting, which is thirteen.
- **The same sentence, two verbs.** "She had mended that bank each spring for six years. She judged that it would hold for ten minutes more." (00) "She had patched that bank each spring for six years. She put what was left in it at ten minutes." (11) If the echo is meant, use the same words.
- **Said to whom.** "I said then that I was coming to stay" (9, to Coralie). She said it to Maylis in the steel room (7). On the stairs, "Lesca did not answer her."
- **The arithmetic of the ending.** "I held it for eight rings" … "That was two minutes." (18) Eight rings is under a minute, and no telephone rings unanswered for two. The whole accounting in 18 rests on that figure.
- **A supper that vanishes.** Arlette: "I want you at my table at seven" (12). At a quarter to seven Lesca is at home with Coralie and the supper is not mentioned again.
- **Eight hours.** Maylis prices the verges at eight hours and writes "Back at two" (7). She is fetched after thirteen (8), and nobody says why the count ran over.
- **Clock style.** Digits belong to screens ("The screen said 02:51"). Chapter 15 also uses them as free-standing paragraphs with no screen in hand ("02:59."), then goes back to "It said 03:09."
- **What gets glossed.** The mairie, the commune, the tonne and the pawl are explained. "Trafic" (first in the handover, 10), "Irun" (13), "adjudant" (7) and "Clio" (18) are not.
- **A tangled sentence.** "In the afternoon that had come into her head in the night there had been grass past the bank." (2)
- **Clean.** British spelling holds throughout. "Telephone" is the narrator's word everywhere, and "phone" appears once, in Kévin's mouth. The dialect participles ("while you're sat", "I'm stood in it") stay inside speech. Dashes appear only where speech is cut off.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie at the oven, both times.** "'In my tray,' she said, and shut the door on him." (1) and "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the neck. 'The—'" (18)
2. **The confession and its delay.** "Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him." … "The loudspeaker on the bank road said drowned him when the square had finished hearing it." (16)
3. **Nine rings against one.** Jeannot's six numbered points down the telephone, ending "'And six. Bugger off.'" and "She listened to all of it." (11)
4. **Kévin's hand.** "'Five,' he said." in the water at four o'clock (15), and "'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything as much.'" (18)
5. **Room twelve and the count.** From "Twelve was shut" to the line through the last name, then the hall laid out as the loto of the dead and "'Forty-one,' Coralie said." (15)
