# Round 14 — Thriller

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Below the bank were Sarthou, and the Darrigrands' baby, and old Lafitte, and Coralie with forty-two in their beds." (15) The forty houses have names by the time they matter. Maylis herself is never in danger of anything worse than a day in one hand.
- **Tension: 6.5.** "Lesca took the body up one stair, and Maylis's arm brought it back." (6) Chapters 3 to 6 and 13 to 15 are tight. Chapters 8, 12 and 17 have none, because nothing can be lost in a village whose flood is twelve years old.
- **Progression: 6.** "'Four to three,' Larrère said. 'It stays shut.'" (10) and "'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. 'That's how it was left.'" (13) The gauge climbs every chapter and that works. The plot under it asks for the gate four times and gets the same answer four times, and the two women trade bodies four times on the same three rules.
- **Comedy: 8.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade.'" (7)
- **Characters: 8.** "'So I'll go down on Friday the eleventh because you've said it.'" (6) The man the book needs me to fear for is the one I would least want drowned.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 7.** Best: "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the neck. 'The—'" (18). Worst: Jeannot's numbered speech about the winch with the water at the residents' shins (15), which is funny and costs the yard forty seconds it has not got.
- **Prose: 7.** "…and when her head went forward she drove her thumbnail into the weal and kept it there." (13) That sentence is the only place the prose runs, and it is right. Against it: "a hand" as a measure (of water, of gate, of window, of door, of roof, from her chin) is past counting by chapter 10.
- **The ending: 8.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (18)
- **Clarity: 6.** "If the other is still sitting in this chair when that happens, the sitter goes home with the waker, and wakes inside the waker's body." (5) The rule is sound and has to be said again in 6, 9, 14 and 15, each time at the moment I want to be moving.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. From the last line of 3 I did not stop before the end of 6, and 13 to 15 went in one sitting. I would have skimmed 8 and the first third of 12.

**Press it on a friend?** On a friend who watches The Good Place, yes. On a friend who reads Crouch for the engine, no, not yet.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch as a thriller. Level with him in two places. Above him at the ending.**

- **Below: nobody is hunting her.** In Dark Matter the man is taken at gunpoint in chapter one and is in bodily danger on most pages after. Maylis's body is borrowed at six and returned by nine at night. The one threat to her person, "I'm coming to stay" (7), is withdrawn by the woman who made it, after eight rings of a telephone (14). All the fear in this book is for other people. That is why its tension depends so heavily on Jeannot and on Coralie, and why the chapters without either go slack.
- **Below: the premise never gets worse.** Crouch's box produces more Jasons; his chair collapses whole timelines. Here the rules are complete at "Those in favour" (5). The two later turns are good and small: "Anybody could send Inguma off to count" (9), and Lesca sitting on its back (14). Meanwhile the thing that drives the clock, weather repeating to the day twelve years late, is raised and put down: "It was weather, and nobody had sent it. They left it there." (18)
- **Level: fear for the marriage.** "'I'd have this one again.'" / "Under the tea towel Maylis shut her fingers on the cut." (6) That is Daniela at the dinner table with the wrong Jason, and it is as good. It is also better judged than Crouch in one respect: Coralie finds both of them out by the wrist in chapter 9, so the book cannot coast on a deceived spouse.
- **Level: the timed climax.** 02:53 to 04:16 in chapter 15, with the clock on the page and the distances in metres, is as well-engineered as anything in Recursion.
- **Above: the cost.** Crouch's endings let the family walk through a door. This one flood-marks forty houses, kills the woman who taught everyone the words, dates the resignation, and puts the pillow in a car. And the saved man says "I was in no danger, and I'll say so to anyone."
- **The Good Place reversal** is there: nine minutes to three is the minute on a telephone screen, and Coralie has had it for twelve years (11, 12). It re-reads the landing, the wardrobe and the pulse-taking. The prologue spends part of it in advance; see fault 8.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The choice at the dip is stated in one sentence, not felt, and then the book denies it was made. (15, 18) — structural**

> "The bank had six minutes, and on foot she could reach one of the two. If she went back she turned the handle on him, as Lesca had. If she went on, the river had the Port. She got out, and went on."

This is the centre of the book: the woman who says Either knowingly gives forty houses and Coralie to the river for one man. It gets six words, no look back at the lit home, and nobody ever mentions it again. Coralie's verdict is "You said both… That was Either, with the river in it." Maylis's own account is "The van in the dip was mine. So was saying both." Madame Darrigrand's is "fetching a man out of a dry hole." All three treat her as a woman who failed to choose. The narrator at 02:59 says she chose.

It also kills the two best beats that follow. Because I have been told at 02:59 that the Port is lost, the Trafic stopped a metre short of her own van at 03:06 is not a blow, and the bank going at 03:11 is not a surprise.

The book's own arithmetic says "both" was still alive. Four minutes to the hatch, two back in a Trafic whose driver "knew the ruts", and the bank in fact held until 03:11. What killed it was her van across the track.

What I would do: cut the three sentences quoted above. At 02:59 let her do the sum she would do: his van is four hundred metres on, and it has already been through this dip twice today. She goes on still meaning both. Then 03:06, "There was no way round", is the moment the sum dies, in front of the reader, on her own abandoned van with its door hanging open. Nothing in 18 needs to change; every reckoning there already assumes this version.

If the makers want the other book, the one where she chooses the man, then the dip needs the home's light in her mirror and Coralie's name, and chapter 18 needs Coralie to say "you left me under it." One or the other. At present it is both, which is the fault the book is about.

**2. Five chapters run on no clock, and the one hook they are given is never paid. (2, 8, 12, 16, 17) — structural**

> "'She's voted herself onto the platform. A metre from that stone, with a camera on her. I'll have my answer there.'" (8)

Everything urgent is in Maylis's village. In Lesca's the flood is on a stone. What is at stake there is a speech, and the question of whether she will say a sentence aloud. Chapter 8's stakes are a laugh in a minute's silence and a kitchen timer. Chapter 12, placed between the five o'clock call and the Friday it was all for, opens with 600 words of white tape: "'Monsieur le maire stands here,' he read."

The one threat that could have put a clock in Lesca's village is the line above: Larrère will ask her, on camera, at the stone. In 16 he does not. "Guy Larrère was at the back, under the last tree, and kept his hat on." She confesses with nobody pushing. The confession is the better for being unforced. But then the end of 8 promised a confrontation the book does not hold, and a Crouch reader who is carrying that promise through 9 to 15 has been lied to.

What I would do. In 8: end on Maylis's finger under the word *prefect* and Inguma coming along under the table, and cut Larrère's motion to four lines without the threat, or keep the threat and let him open his mouth in 16 a second after she has answered it. In 12: begin at "At four the mairie was open". Keep from the tape scene only what 16 needs (the cross, "if the mayor is prevented", the eight in the front row, Madame Etcheto nearest the stone) and give it as Coralie's forty lines, not the young man's. The chapter is then Arlette, the telephone on the charger, and the two sentences tried aloud, and it earns its place at the peak.

**3. The winch is planted five times and pays off in somebody else's hands, reported. (1, 3, 6, 10 → 15)**

> "*Eighteen clicks lifts it a hand. It binds at nineteen. Left shoulder under, both arms. You can have the right.*" (10)
> "'First, I counted, as you do, by the click, and at nineteen it stopped as if it had come to a wall.'" (15)

The nineteenth turn is the book's most carefully hung gun. It cracks the rib, it defeats six men and four umbrellas, it takes the skin off the palm, it is the reason Lesca is hired. At the crisis Maylis throws the key to Jeannot and I hear about nineteen in a three-point speech at the home's front door.

It also leaves chapter 18's accounting unsound. Lesca says "two minutes would have covered them." Two minutes covers two hundred metres of track. It does not cover eighteen clicks and a shoulder under the nineteenth with "something gone in her side", on a winch that has stopped everybody who has touched it.

What I would do: at 03:11 put her on the platform. She has the key; it is on the way. Give her the eighteen clicks and the wall at nineteen, with the lamps of the Port going out behind her. Then she throws the handle's key to Jeannot and runs. It costs 150 words and pays the plant on stage. In 18, let Maylis answer Lesca's two minutes with one line: it stops at nineteen. That takes nothing from either woman and puts the weight where Jeannot's number six and her three letters have always put it.

**4. The antagonist is beaten by a paragraph of her own reasoning. (14)**

> "But for as long as she stayed there were two of them in the cab, and they wanted different things. One had the wheel and the other had the gear lever, and the van would stand where it was. Nobody would reach the handle, and nobody would reach the man."

"I'm coming to stay" has been the threat since chapter 7 and the motion since chapter 4. It ends here, between ring seven and ring eight, in an explanation of what I have just watched happen: the lever knocked out, the stall, the horn. Then a second paragraph explains what letting go will do, and a third costs it. Three paragraphs of reckoning between "Seven." and "She let go."

What I would do: cut from "She could hold it" to "nobody would reach the man", and all of "If she let go… on the way out." Keep "He sat under a hatch at the end of that track, and he would be left there a second time, by the two of them together." Keep the cost, because "Not one of them would drive a van" is the best line in the chapter. The sequence is then: horn, seven, Inguma straining under her, the man left a second time, eight, the three things she came for, she lets go. It reads as an act.

**5. The five o'clock "Either" is the hinge of the plot, and the reason given for it is one the reader cannot share. (13)**

> "For three o'clock she had only the word of a woman in a steel room, and that morning she had struck that woman off the pad."

If she turns the handle here, in daylight, with Jeannot standing on the planks, everybody lives. The book needs her not to. Three chapters earlier the café read that woman's word back to the mayor from a pad, "'I wrote that eight days ago, Guy'", and the gauge has matched the file day for day since chapter 5. Doubt of the forecast is not available to her, and I do not believe she has it.

Kévin is the true reason and is enough: "she sent the boy to prison herself, for a flood that had not come."

What I would do: cut the sentence quoted. If a counterweight to her foreknowledge is wanted, the book has one and uses it forty minutes too late, through the hatch: "'Pau gives less than that for the top of the tide. I had it on the radio.'" Move that to the platform at five, in Jeannot's mouth, where it can do the work.

**6. 8,200 words follow "Forty-one", and they run out of order. (16, 17, 18)**

> "Her Monday at Bayonne and her Tuesday before the council were still to come, and the pillow was still in the box room." (18)

That sentence is an apology for the structure. Chapter 17 runs Lesca forward to Tuesday night and the pillow; chapter 18 then steps back to Sunday at 02:51 for the last meeting in the chair. And I wait two chapters and 5,100 words after the best chapter-ending in the book to return to the village where the bank went.

Chapter 17 is where a Crouch reader stops. Etcheto's question, Maïté's kitchen, the gutter, the landing, Bayonne, the council: six scenes of accounts being settled, none in doubt.

What I would do: put the chair scene where it falls, after "Go to bed, Maylis" and the lamp left lit. Then Bayonne and the council together in one page; "ticked *No*" and "'I abstain,' he said, and that was all he said" are the two things worth keeping. End Lesca on the pillow. Chapter 18 then opens at first light in the silt, with Larrère on foot, and the apology sentence is gone.

**7. Nine hours in a van, waiting for the appointed minute. (13)**

> "She had tried the man twice, at his van window at five and at his hatch at dusk, and a third try would take her an hour off the gate."
> "The man in the cellar had been told twice."

What works at 03:03, a fist in two collars, was available at dusk, when the hatch stood open "the width of a hand", and again at two. The reader asks why she does not go at two and be back by ten to three. The only answer on the page is that it would take her off the gate, and she has already been off it an hour and been caught.

The second sentence quoted is worse than it looks. It says that at 22:41 her plan is to raise the gate on him: Lesca's act, with more warnings. Only Lesca's five words change it. So the woman who goes down the track is following an instruction.

What I would do: one beat in those hours that shuts the door. She starts down the crown at one; lamps come along it from the terrace and sit a hundred metres back with the engine running, as they did at five. And change "The man in the cellar had been told twice" to something that leaves it open, so that the decision at 02:57 is partly hers.

**8. The prologue spends chapter 11, and chapter 11 repeats it. (00, 11)**

> "She laid her hand flat on the bank beside the water, and the clay moved under her palm." (00)
> "She got out and put her hand flat on the bank beside it, and the clay moved under her palm." (11)

The prologue is right to exist. It puts a river and a decision on page one, which chapter 1's postman cannot. But from it I know she turned the van, looked down the track and rang once, so Maïté's "Did you know he was out?" (2) and Maylis's (10) are questions I have had answered since before chapter 1. Chapter 11 then gives me 300 words I have read: the shutters, the stair light, the sheet the width of a door, the hand on the clay, six springs, ten minutes, the torch on the river.

What is new in 11 is four things: Larrère's order with a kitchen clock behind it, Coralie's car in the yard, the reflector, and the four minutes it burns before the water puts it out.

What I would do: keep the prologue as it is. In 11, go from the telephone and the ticking clock to the home's yard to the platform in six lines, and spend the room on the reflector and the wader. The third telling, Maylis's own hand on the clay in 15, then echoes the first and not the second.

**9. The rules are re-explained at the moments I most want to be moving. (6, 9, 15)**

> "It went as it had on Saturday. Lesca had stayed sitting in the chair, so she had come home in Maylis's body, and she had nearly all of it." (6)
> "If Lesca was still sitting in the chair on any of those mornings, Lesca would come home in her." (9)
> "The man was to be fetched out first, and the gate raised after." (15)

Chapter 9 has a perfect ending, "'It's twenty minutes to Bayonne,' she said. Coralie got up and took her bowl to the sink." It is followed by a paragraph of rota and rule. The threat is already in one word, said round a hand: "'Friday.'" In 15, the third paragraph glosses five words the reader heard a page ago, at 02:53 with a telephone ringing.

What I would do: end 9 at the sink. Cut 15's third paragraph. Cut the second paragraph of 6 to its last two sentences, which are new (the right hand, the jaw for a word or two).

**10. The book asks why it rained, and then declines. (18)**

> "'Why did it rain? There, that year, and not here. And now here.'" / "It was weather, and nobody had sent it. They left it there."

Chapter 5 dealt with this properly, by argument: "'Then it had rained two days before you did anything'", "'Nobody was to blame for a dry one.'" Lesca's guilt wanted the flood to be hers and Maylis's dates took it away. That was the answer. Reopening the question on the last night, only to shrug, points at the one hole a Crouch reader has been willing to step over.

What I would do: cut the six lines.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 6 from the soup to the stairs.** "'I'd have this one again.'" The spoon tipped back. The newel post. Inguma's leaves going past Coralie's ankle. "'Are you coming up?'"
2. **The reflector.** "low on its left-hand door a round reflector, screwed on crooked, burning red" (11), and its return in 13 with the dream check turned on waking life: "She read it, looked away at the gauge and read it again. She was awake."
3. **Chapter 15 from the breach to "Forty-one."** The street lamps going out one after another. The corridor lit by one van, and dark while it is gone. Kévin's telephone buzzing at four and "'Five,'" said "as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." Room twelve, the two fingers, the pencil through the last name.
4. **The sentence at the microphone and what the square does with it.** "'Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him.'" The loudspeaker on the bank road saying it late. The man beyond the last loudspeaker who claps.
5. **The last page.** Letter 42, "It's four sides, and it doesn't thank you", and Jeannot getting down into the grave in his waders.
