**Finished unpaid?** Yes. From chapter 5 I would not have stopped.

**Pressed on a friend?** No. I would tell the friend three scenes from it (the oven, the café in chapter 6, the phone call in chapter 11) and keep the book until chapter 8 and the last three chapters are rebuilt.

## 2. Against the comparisons

Crouch writes almost no comedy, so against him the book is above by default and the comparison is worth nothing. The Good Place is the test. Against it: **below**. A 7 against an 8.

**Level: the flaw is the engine.** The Good Place makes Chidi's inability to choose the joke and then the thing that damns him. This book does the same with one word. "Either" starts as dinner ("I don't cook Either. There's no recipe for it."), becomes a repair, an identity, a police form ("Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station."), and then the gate with a man standing on the bank. When she finally chooses, it is "Both", and Coralie hears it for what it is: "That was Either, with the river in it." The physical farce follows the same line. Two women fighting over a spoon in chapter 6 become two women fighting over a gear lever in chapter 14, and that fight costs two minutes and a life. The comic mechanism and the moral mechanism are one mechanism. That is exactly what the series does and it is done as well here.

**Level: the switch.** The series turns from joke to grief on a prop it has already made funny. So does this book: the oven door, the white timer, "Here", "I'll be brief", the bingo card. Nothing has to be changed between the two tones, because the same object does both jobs.

**Below: rate and range.** The Good Place has four or five comic registers in every scene: vulgar, anxious, grand, stupid, literal. This book has one, a dry procedural understatement, and the narrator and most of the cast speak it. There are distinct comic men (Larrère evades, Jeannot numbers, Sarthou files, Kévin asks). The women, except Coralie in the first half, deliver verdicts in the same clipped sentence. And the book plays only one set piece at full length (chapter 6). Its other two best premises, the village's girl chairing a council and a village bingo night, are summarised.

**Below: the reversals.** A Good Place reversal re-reads the whole season. The reversals here each re-read one object: the minute 02:51, the kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice, the landing Coralie has kept. They are well planted and they pay. The one that could re-read the whole book, that the better choice kills a woman and saves a man who "was in no danger, and I'll say so to anyone", arrives as aftermath across a chapter and is never a single turn.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. Chapter 8 throws away the book's second-best comic premise.**
"He went on, like a man who finds a door unlocked. He had eleven years of it saved up, and it lasted eleven minutes. Near the end he was saying things twice, and looking at the timer in between for help."
That is the funniest idea in the book and it is four sentences of report. We do not hear one word of the eleven minutes. The same chapter gives the laugh during the minute of silence as "Maylis laughed, with Lesca's mouth, across the table from the widow." Chapter 6, where the mayor rides the handyman, runs five played scenes with the trapped right hand busy in every one. Chapter 8, where the handyman rides the mayor, runs four short scenes in half the length, mostly in summary, and Lesca's one hand does one thing. Maylis spends thirteen hours as mayor and never looks at the stone that opened her story in chapter 1, never sees the lake, never enters the café. The reader has waited three chapters for this exchange and gets a bridge.
*Do:* rebuild chapter 8 to the length and construction of chapter 6. Play the minute of silence. Play the eleven minutes as speech, with the table's faces and Larrère circling back to "hydraulic". Give Maylis a public scene to match the café: a mayor who says "Either" to a room that minutes it as a ruling. Put her in front of the stone. Give Lesca's right hand a job in every scene.

**2. The aftermath is two receiving lines, and the café is missing from the last eight chapters.**
Chapter 16, after the speech: Marie-Jo, then Tastet, then Arlette, then Darrigrand, then the young man from Pau, each walking up, saying a piece, and walking off. Chapter 18 does it again on the other side: Larrère and Sarthou, Kévin, Arlette, Madame Darrigrand, Marie-Jo, then Coralie, the priest, Jeannot. "I'm saying it to your face. I'd rather you heard it from me than from the café." These are fifteen small scenes with a closing line each. Nothing builds from one to the next. Meanwhile the book's chorus, the café with its card game and Lafitte's pad, is last seen in chapter 10. Its final joke is heard through a door and then explained by a third party: "That's what late means now. They've retired the other one."
*Do:* one scene on each side. In chapter 18 put Maylis inside the café on Sunday morning, with everyone present, the new line said to her face, and Lafitte's pad headed *Madame le maire* getting its last entry. In chapter 16 keep the chair-stacking and let the visitors overlap and interrupt across the rows, so it is one scene with a shape.

**3. The last meeting of the two women drops the meeting.**
"'No. You won't sign it, and I'll take my time telling you why. I was right about the houses. I can show you that in a file, to the centimetre...'" (18)
The double act was founded in chapter 5 on a form: items, a vote, a tie, an empty chair. The final meeting has no items and no vote. It has a hundred-and-fifty-word speech in which one character tells the other the moral of the book, a retelling of chapter 16 that the reader has just read ("I said it at eleven. Into a microphone, in front of the square"), and a thread closed by the narrator's say-so: "It was weather, and nobody had sent it. They left it there." It also sits in the wrong place. The chapter opens by apologising for its own order.
*Do:* run it as the last meeting. Matters arising, one item each, the first vote that is not a tie, the meeting closed. Cut Lesca's speech to its three sentences of fact ("You rang nine times. You walked his track at dusk, and you ran it in the night. A woman is dead because of that, and it still isn't what I did."). Move the scene to sit between chapters 16 and 17, where it happens, and delete the time-sorting paragraph.

**4. Bingo night has no bingo in it.**
"At eight that evening it was bingo night in the village hall, and the keyholder was serving at the bar." (9)
The hall of the dead is the book's best invention, and its best line is the caller: "It called her the way it called the numbers, first whole and then in parts," then "Twelve. One, two." But the living bingo night never lets us hear a number called. Monsieur Daguerre's "Here", the grass trimmer he is "one number short of", Madame Etcheto's duck: all are reported after the fact. The dead are calling back to a scene the book did not write.
*Do:* play the bingo. Let the caller call in the living hall the way he will call in the dead one. Let Daguerre's "Here" start there. Let twelve be called.

**5. The women speak in one voice, and it is the verdict.**
Coralie: "Don't give me the minutes." "Don't give me the measurement." "Don't tell me what he was." "I'll say what it is to me, and then I won't say it again." Arlette: "I'm not asking you why. Not once." "I'm not asking where you were. I've been told." Maïté: "Don't do it in the square." "Don't kiss me after it." Marie-Jo: "Then you can have the rest from me." Madame Darrigrand: "I'm saying it to your face." Lesca herself: "Don't give me him for that." "Don't give me your sum to hide behind."
Six women, one sentence. Each is a stern instruction, clipped, with the feeling withheld. In chapter 1 Coralie is a comic performer who talks into an oven. By chapter 15 she is issuing the same refusals as everyone else. The Good Place's people are each wrong in their own way and each funny in their own way. Here the men are (Larrère evades, Jeannot numbers, Sarthou files) and the women are not.
*Do:* take "Don't give me" and "I'm not asking" away from all but one speaker each. Give Arlette, Maïté and Marie-Jo a comic habit as fixed as Jeannot's numbering. Marie-Jo has one in chapter 2 (she speaks to the wall, "through the chair") and loses it by chapter 16.

**6. The narrator labels the joke and the feeling.**
"In eighteen years beside that car Maylis had never seen it do that." "In sixteen years she had never ticked one of those boxes and left the other empty." "In sixteen years Maylis had never got that laugh out of her." "In thirty years at that counter Jeannot Castagnet had never left a glass unfinished." "In sixteen years Maylis had never asked her for anything." Five uses of one construction to tell the reader that a beat mattered. Beside them: "It hurt to hear." "Maylis was ashamed." "She was scared." (four times) "Five meant he was awake. He said it like a man who turns over a card and finds it isn't the one he hoped for." "He meant the medal." "The chair was Lesca, and her name was on the stone." "At that table Coralie was the funny one, and Maylis was the one who laughed."
Deadpan works by withholding. Each of these hands over what the scene had already delivered. In chapter 15 the narrator also reminds us of the laughing fit in the corridor one paragraph before the body in room twelve, to make sure the switch is felt.
*Do:* keep one "In N years" (the car window). Cut every sentence that names an emotion after a scene has shown it. Cut the corridor reminder in 15. Kévin's "Five" needs nothing after it.

**7. Chapter 7 is where the dark smothers the comedy.**
"'I don't want it back,' Guy Larrère said. 'I want it written down. Sit.'"
After the book's comic peak comes a 110-line chapter with one joke in it. The cliffhanger of chapter 6 ("Are you coming up?") is answered in retrospect, in a fine line that stands in for a scene: "It had taken her an hour to lock a house with one door." Larrère, whom we have only ever seen through "a few centimetres" of car window, is suddenly behind a desk, and the narration does not notice. Chapter 18 notices ("This time the mayor had no car around him"), so the idea exists. He also drops his own idiom. This is the one time in the book he is actually brief, and nobody marks it. Then Maylis blackmails Jeannot, and the narrator tells us how she feels.
*Do:* let Larrère open with "I'll be brief" and, for once, be. Stage him through a gap (a door on a chain, the secretary's hatch). The menace is worse when it comes in the voice that has been funny. Play the stairs.

**8. Recaps and glosses break the timing.**
Chapter 6 spends its first three paragraphs re-teaching the rule that chapter 5 taught as a joke: "Lesca had stayed sitting in the chair, so she had come home inside Maylis's body, and she controlled nearly all of it." A version of that sentence appears in chapters 5, 6, 7, 9 (twice) and 14. The pawl is defined four times in almost the same words ("the pawl, the steel catch that held each turn", chapters 3, 6, 11, 13). The blind is re-explained in chapter 10 and again in 15. Chapter 15, at 02:53 with a bank about to go, stops to say "Jeannot was a kilometre away on the flood meadows, in his duck-hunting blind, under a hatch."
*Do:* define each thing once. Open chapter 6 on "Coralie came down in her tunic at ten past six. Lesca spoke first."

**9. The double act is one-sided.**
"'I'll chair this one,' she said. 'One item.'" (10)
In the steel room Lesca gets every joke: "All those in favour", "Next item", "Nil", "apologies received". When Maylis takes the lead she borrows Lesca's form. But Maylis has an idiom of her own, and it is just as funny: she prices and measures. "Maylis counted it out on her fingers, the way she worked out the price of a job." "That's a working day. Eight hours." "*Paid.*" "It's twenty minutes to Bayonne." The book never sets the estimate against the motion.
*Do:* in chapters 10 and 18, let Maylis quote for the job while Lesca moves it. An invoice against a resolution. Each woman is then wrong in her own form, and the scene has two engines.

**10. The first laugh is late and the premise is announced.**
"So there were two Peyrebarthes." (2)
Chapter 1 runs sixty lines of night terror and backstory before the postman. The café's line "Still in the box, were you?" arrives before the reader can get it. Chapter 2 then delivers the premise as a deduction made lying in bed, in nine paragraphs of statement, before the scene that would have delivered it as comedy: a committee arguing about a verb for a stone with her name on it.
*Do:* cut the deduction to the two facts she cannot explain (forty letters, a cracked rib she does not have) and let the stone and the committee carry the rest. Move the postman up.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **"Either", from the shopping pad to the gate to "Both".** Every use, in its present order, including Sarthou's "Either is not a person."
2. **The oven door.** Coralie doing Larrère into it in chapter 1, and failing to in chapter 18. Do not add a word to either.
3. **Chapter 11's phone call.** One ring in 2014, nine rings now, "Who's dead?", and "And six. Bugger off." followed by "She listened to all of it." The best switch in the book.
4. **Room twelve.** Bixente, "Staff to wait. Staff not to join in", "A rib doesn't laugh", the dead at bingo with maize, and "Twelve. One, two."
5. **The café in chapter 6.** "She's got a mayor in her." "It takes a small m." Josiane's "It's adultery either way, and that one lives with a nurse." "Which do I put?" And the car window going all the way down.

Also leave alone: "There's nobody in the chair" (5), "apologies received" (14), "Then they'll want the bins out" (15), Tastet turning the sheet over (16), and Larrère's "I'll be brief" followed by nothing (17).
