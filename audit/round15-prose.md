## 2. Against the comparisons

Below Crouch through chapter 10. Level or above from 11 to 16. Below overall, narrowly.

- **Below: Crouch states a rule once, inside an event, and afterwards shows only what it costs.** This narrator re-briefs. "Stayed sitting" or "still sitting" appears thirteen times. The division of the shared body is set out in 5, 6, 8 and 14. The penalty, dead in her own bed, is stated in 3, 5, 10, 12 and 14. A Crouch reader has the rule after Lesca's three points in 5 and reads every later statement as the book doubting him.
- **Below: Crouch changes gear.** Here the narration averages between ten and twelve words a sentence in every one of the nineteen chapters, at breakfast and in the flood. 15 proves the point against the rest: it breaks the page with bare clock times ("02:59.") and is the best chapter in the book.
- **Above: the comic sentence and the prop.** "The rule applied to every member and had been needed for one." (2) Crouch has no sentence like it. The moral problem is run as procedure (a motion, a timer, two boxes on a form), which is The Good Place's method, and it is done as well here. There is no ornament: no semicolons, eight dashes and all of them cut-off speech, every comparison taken from her work (sacks, doors, a hand of water).

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The premise is deduced in narration, not staged. (2)**
"So there were two Peyrebarthes. They had been one village until an afternoon in June 2014." And at the end: "She was wrong. It was 2026 there as well, and that village had not had its flood yet."
Lesca derives the whole cosmology, the split, the twelve-year delay and the date, from one bad night, and the narrator certifies each step. The book's idea reaches the reader as nine statements. The staged version already exists two chapters later and is far better: "The river over there was following this file, one line a day." (4)
Do: end 2 on the ladder with the two facts that do not fit (a dead man's forty letters, rain on a dry roof, Maïté's "It was raining by this date, that year"). Move the deduction of the date to the gauge sheets in 4, where there is evidence on the desk. Cut the certified paragraphs.

**2. The central choice, and every plan before it, is argued by the narrator ahead of the act. (14, also 4, 12, 13)**
"Letting go would cost Lesca everything she had come for. She had come for the sound of rain on the roof. She had come for a woman four steps away across a landing... She had come for a man at a café counter..." (14). "This was her plan." (14) "So her plan was simple." (13) "She did not mean to come back. She had made her plan." (12) "She could not do the first thing. She did not want to look at the second. She had a word for that." (13) "The rest had a bed in it, and a name." (4)
The book's hinge, a woman giving up a life in two minutes while a phone rings, is delivered as four paragraphs of if-then. The ring count, the horn and "None of those things would drive a van" do the work alone. In 4 the motion's second clause is the best chapter ending in the first half, and it is described thirty lines before it is shown. In 13 "Either" is the punchline and the narrator sets it up.
Do: in 14 keep the gear lever, the wheel, the lift under her in the steel room, the rings and the van line; cut the two paragraphs that begin "But as long as she stayed" and "If Lesca let go", and the triple "She had come for". Cut every plan paragraph named above. In 4 cut from "She had come back wanting that" to "and a name".

**3. In the last chapter the narrator leaves its post. (17, 18)**
"Her Monday in Bayonne and her Tuesday in front of the council had not happened yet." "On Monday night she would lie awake at that minute to see whether it came, and she would find nothing on the wardrobe." "She was thinking of the landing, the Saturday before." (Coralie's head, the only time the book enters a third one.) "It was weather, and nobody had sent it. They left it there."
For eighteen chapters the voice stands inside one woman in one minute. In 18 it manages a calendar, looks ahead, enters Coralie and closes the book's open question by decree. The cause is the order: 17 runs Lesca on to Tuesday, so 18 has to rewind to Sunday.
Do: end 17 on the landing, lamp on. Open 18 in the steel room with no note. Put Bayonne, the vote and the pillow as a short Lesca section before the cemetery, so the book still ends on the second shovel. Cut the look-ahead and the Coralie sentence; her "I chose you, Maylis, over somebody who asked" needs no gloss. Let the two women's "I don't know" stand as the last word on the rain.

**4. The mechanism is re-briefed each time it is used. (6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 14)**
"It was the same as on Saturday. Lesca had stayed sitting in the chair, so she had come home inside Maylis's body, and she controlled nearly all of it." (6) "It did not sit on her, so she did not go to the chair, and nobody came home in her body when the alarm rang at six." (10) "They shared the body the way they had on the other days. Lesca had the legs, the left hand and the mouth." (14) "Anybody could send Inguma away to count." (9)
The second paragraph of 6 announces that Maylis can take the jaw for a word, and so spends "Maylis threw herself at the jaw, and got it" before it happens. 9 does the same to the bingo scene after the fact.
Do: Lesca's three points in 5 are the one statement, and they are funny. After them show the consequence only: the legs swing out of bed. Delete the rest. This needs a check with a reader who has not seen the book before.

**5. The narrator footnotes its own echoes and labels the feeling. (throughout, densest 13 to 18)**

Each setup is good enough to be remembered, and each is then explained at the moment it pays. The Kévin line in 15 also spends his "I wanted six, Maylis" three chapters early.

**6. The night of 2014 is told three times, twice in the same sentences. (0, 2, 11)**
Prologue: "It ran down the clay in a sheet as wide as a door and crossed the road in her headlights. She put her hand flat on the bank beside the water. The clay moved under her palm." Chapter 11 has the same three sentences. The prologue also shows the van turned and the call made, and Maïté asks "Did you know he was out?" in 2, so "So Lesca had known" (11) tells the reader nothing. The echo that matters is Maylis's hand on the clay in 15, and it arrives third.
Do: keep the prologue. In 11 enter at the headlights finding the reflector, which is the new fact; cut the drive and the bank. Cut the three paragraphs after the flashback that restate it ("Now she knew why...", "So Lesca had known...").

**7. Two scenes in three open on the clock, the day or the weather.**

By my count 57 of 87 scenes open this way. The countdown is worth having. Used at every break it reads as a duty log, and nine minutes to three (sixteen uses) loses its edge.
Do: date the chapter head and the scenes where the count bites. Open the others as 13 does: "Somebody was on the platform by the gate, with a flashlight in his teeth."
