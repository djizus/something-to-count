# Round 15 — Reader

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. About 45,000 words. Nothing else opened.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.5.** The stake I felt most was a bed, and it is stated in one line: "*That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*" (4). The river is clear and dated but I was never as afraid of it as of that sentence.
- **Tension: 7.** At its height: "She had been afraid of that winch for a week. Now she was more afraid of her own stairs." (6). It leaves the book between those stairs and the landing in 9, and comes back for good at 12.
- **Progression: 6.5.** The middle moves by rule and by form, and one chapter closes on a rota: "Her alarm would ring at six on each of the five mornings before Friday, and each time it would wake Maylis first." (9, last paragraph).
- **Comedy: 7.5.** "'The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies,' she said, in the voice she used for the prefecture. 'She has a ceremony at eleven and a third sheet to put right, and she will not be taking this seat.'" (14). I laughed out loud eight or nine times in the book. The Good Place gets that in an episode.
- **Characters: 8.** "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." (9). Coralie is the person I read for, in both villages.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'It's Saturday.' / 'Then they'll want the bins out,' he said." (15), said by a man being wheeled through flood water. Not one joke in 15 costs the chapter anything.
- **Prose: 6.5.** "The embankment was a bank of clay built between them and the river." (1), three pages after the opening told me exactly that. It reads without effort and it tells me most things twice.
- **The ending: 7.5.** "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much." (18). The cost is real. The order in which I was given it is wrong (fault 2).
- **Clarity: 6.5.** "If the other one is still sitting in this chair when that happens, she goes home with the one who woke." (5). I read it three times. The rule is then restated at the top of 6, 8 and 14, and I still cannot say why eight hours of verges took thirteen.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. I was at risk twice: in the middle of 3 and all through 8.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On one friend, the one who likes village comedy and long marriages. Not on the friend who lent me *Dark Matter*. He would stop in 8 and ask what the box was for.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Overall: below the bar.** It is a good book (7). It is not yet as good as Crouch at being unable to stop, or as The Good Place at the reversal that re-reads everything. It is above Crouch at one thing.

**Below Crouch on drive.** A Crouch chapter ends on a turn that makes the next page compulsory. Here I count seven chapter endings of that strength (00, 3, 4, 6, 12, 13, 15). Chapter 9 ends on a shift pattern and 8 on a councillor promising to ask a question later. Crouch also explains his box by the midpoint and then spends the second half exploiting it. This book stops mentioning Dr Kessler after chapter 4 and answers its own "Why did it rain?" with "I don't know either."

**Above Crouch on the marriage.** *Dark Matter* makes me afraid the wife will not tell the difference. This book has the wife tell the difference by a pulse, "That's her. In this hand", and then say to the impostor, "I'd have you tomorrow." That is worse, and better. The stairs in 6 and the landing in 9 frightened me more than the river did.

**Level with The Good Place on people each wrong in their own way; below it on the reversal.** Maylis has Either, Lesca has the sum, Coralie never asked, Jeannot numbers his grievances, Larrère talks through a gap. That is the show's method and it works. But the show's reversals change what I thought I had watched. This book's secret, that Lesca knew the man was out there, is shown in 00 and confirmed in 2 ("That isn't what I asked"), so chapter 11 told me what I had known for nine chapters. The turns that did change things for me were smaller: 02:51 is the minute of the phone call, Coralie has had the call log for twelve years, and the screen says "Castagnet, house".

**What I did not understand**

- Why Maylis went on at the dip instead of back to the winch (fault 1).
- Why Inguma took thirteen hours over a job she priced at eight (7, 8, 9).
- Whether the chair pulled in at "Twelve. One, two." is the place set for LESCA, MAYLIS. Chapter 14 says "One chair was empty". If so, Madame Etcheto took Lesca's seat, and that is a much bigger claim than two lost minutes.
- Why water let out over the meadows does not run into the Port. Both lie on the land side of the same bank, two hundred metres apart.
- Who was ever going to ask for the envelope, and what the university learned.
- Which landing I was on. From 9 onward both Coralies sleep across one, and in 12 and 17 I had to check the village.
- Marie-Jo and Maïté, every time they were in one scene.

**Where I was bored or skimming.** The winch inspection in 3. All of 8 after the minute of silence. The first half of 11, until the reflector. Bayonne and the council vote in 17. Every paragraph that explained the gate, the blind or the pawl again.

**Where I could not stop.** From the motion at the end of 4 to "Are you coming up?" at the end of 6. The landing and the kitchen table in 9. Coralie and the phone in 12. Then 13 to 16 in one sitting.

**What I will remember in a month.** A right hand clamped over a mouth and a woman leaning across a table to hold the other wrist down. Two fingers on the inside of a wrist. "Eggs is what Either gets in this house." A car window that goes all the way down. Kévin counting five in his own headlights. Monsieur Daguerre's "Here". "Forty-one." A man in waders stepping over a string. I will not remember the resignation letter, the Hontaube form, the council register or anything about Monsieur Tastet.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The central choice is one sentence, and it is told from outside on both sides. (14, 15)**

> "If she went back, she would turn the handle on him, as Lesca had. If she went on, the river would take the Port.
>
> She got out and went on."

This is the act the book is about. The Port is Coralie and forty-two beds. Two pages earlier, in Lesca's chapter, I was told that "Maylis wanted the gate. She meant to raise it now, with the man still in his cellar at the end of the track, because Coralie was under that bank." So in the space of ten minutes Maylis wants the gate for Coralie, is told five words by a woman she crossed off a pad that morning, and walks away from the gate and from Coralie. I am not inside her for either turn. The first is reported by her rival. The second gets four words. At dusk, when she said Either at the winch, I had a full page of her reasons.

Everybody else then tells me what the act was. Lesca: "it still isn't what I did." Coralie: "That was Either, with the river in it." Madame Darrigrand: "fetching a man out of a dry hole." The café: gone for a walk. Maylis herself says only "The van in the dip was mine. So was saying both." Nobody in 18 asks her why she went on, and I wanted to.

What I would do: stay at the dip for one short paragraph in her head, with Coralie's name in it. Either she decides, or her legs go before she has decided, which would be Either with legs. I need to see which, so that Coralie's verdict in 18 is a thing I watched and not a ruling. And give Maylis her own reversal at 02:53 in 15, in her own chapter, instead of hearing it from Lesca in 14.

**2. The last three chapters are in the wrong order. (16, 17, 18)**

> "For Lesca it was the night after the speech. Her Monday in Bayonne and her Tuesday in front of the council had not happened yet, and the pillow was still in the spare room."

After "Forty-one" I wanted Maylis and Coralie. I was given two chapters of Lesca, through to a Tuesday council vote, and then the narrator had to stop and tell me to forget half of what I had just read. The last meeting of the two women, where the book says what it thinks, arrives after one of them has already been settled. And the best mirror in the ending is thrown away: Lesca's Coralie carries a pillow in on the last line of 17, Maylis's Coralie shuts a car door on one in 18, and I only put the two together after I had finished.

What I would do: 16 as it is. Then 17 as far as midnight on the landing ("The pillow stays where it is"). Then the steel room. Then Bayonne and the council, cut to a page, ending on the pillow crossing. Then Maylis's Sunday, so that the pillow going out follows the pillow coming in, and the grave stays last. The apologetic paragraph quoted above disappears.

**3. The day the village's girl is mayor is a timetable. (8)**

> "At two Maylis stood Lesca at the window until the church clock had finished striking. Nothing came for her."

Chapter 6, Lesca loose in Maylis's village, is 2,668 words of scene. Its mirror is 1,474 words of clock times: ten, two, six, half past six, seven. Since chapter 1 I had been waiting for Maylis to stand in front of the stone with her name on it ("She knew what a name on a stone meant"). She crosses that square in 8 and never looks. Her wanting to be mayor is what the bargain in 10 and the shame in 11 stand on, and it rests on two lines: "She wanted that" (3) and "I want that" (8).

What I would do: add no events. Play two that are already there as scenes. Stand her in front of the stone and let her read the top line she could not read in chapter 1. Let the table stay standing for her a beat longer before the timer joke. Take the room from Larrère's motion about the prefect, which is Lesca's business and could be half its length.

**4. The opening shows the secret, and chapter 11 still unveils it. (00, 2, 10, 11)**

> "So Lesca had known. She had seen his van before she opened the gate."

The opening has her turn the van, look down the track and ring a number once. Chapter 2 has the widow say "That isn't what I asked." I knew from there. Chapter 10 then stages "Did you know he was out there?" as a question, and 11 answers it at length, reusing the opening's sentences ("It ran down the clay in a sheet as wide as a door and crossed the road in her headlights"). By chapter 15 I had read that half minute four times: 00, the speech in 2, 11, and Maylis's own in 15. Only the fourth earns the echo.

What I would do: keep the opening whole. It is why I understood the bank, the gate and the track at all, and "It rang once" is the best hook in the book. Cut from 11 everything the opening already showed and keep what it did not: the reflector, the time on the screen, Coralie's car in the yard, the four minutes watching the red light until the water covered it. Cut "So Lesca had known."

**5. The book explains its own furniture again in almost every chapter. (throughout; worst in 13 and 15)**

> "Larrère was in Jeannot's kitchen, on Jeannot's phone, and Jeannot was a kilometre away on the flood meadows, in his duck-hunting blind, under a hatch." (15, at 02:53)

"The pawl, the steel catch that held each turn" is glossed four times (3, 6, 11, 13). "Duck-hunting blind" is written out twelve times, with "a cellar under a hatch" attached again in 10 and 15. "The Adour was their river" arrives in 13. Madame Ducasse is introduced as the town hall secretary in 8 and again in 12. The riding rule is laid out in 5 and then again at the top of 6 ("It was the same as on Saturday…"), 8, and in the middle of the climax in 14 ("This was her plan. While she kept Inguma down…"). By 13 I felt I was being read to slowly.

What I would do: gloss each thing once, where it first appears, and trust me after. One sentence of rule at the top of 6 and 8, not a paragraph. Nothing glossed after 12.

**6. The machine is dropped. (1, 4, 5, 18)**

> "Dr Kessler had handed her the card and the envelope together, and had told her to write one word, seal it and keep it with her until someone asked." (4)

Nobody asks. Kessler is named three times and gone by chapter 5. The rain question is raised in 5 and again in 18 and both times set down: "It was weather, and nobody had sent it. They left it there." I do not need physics. I do need the book not to promise me a caller for the envelope. The card itself is carried to the last page, buttoned in with letter No. 42, and that is enough of a fate for it.

What I would do: cut "until someone asked" and "Nobody had asked her for the envelope since." Then nothing is owed. Keep one "I don't know" about the rain, in 18, and cut the exchange in 5 down to Maylis's count on her fingers.

**7. Too many women beginning with M at one table. (2, 5, 16)**

> "'Through the chair,' said Marie-Jo. 'Saved who? It sits under his name.'" … "'I have a verb,' Maïté Castagnet said."

Maylis, Maïté and Marie-Jo, plus Madame le maire, Madame Etcheto, Madame Ducasse and both Darrigrands. In chapter 2 I meet Maïté and Marie-Jo in the same scene, in my first ten minutes in that village. In 5 Marie-Jo stops Maylis on the church steps and Maïté opens the door a paragraph later. I mixed them up both times.

What I would do: rename Marie-Jo. She is the smaller part.

**8. Two rules slip and nobody on the page notices. (7–9, 14–16)**

> "'That's a working day. Eight hours.'" (7) … "the mayor has been absent since six this morning,' Lesca said. 'Thirteen hours.'" (8)

Coralie says "Your pad said two. It's seven" and the matter is left there. In a book where every count is exact I took the missing five hours for a clue, and it was not one. The second slip is the seat at the bingo: 14 says one chair is empty and the card in front of it says LESCA, MAYLIS; 16 says "A chair was pulled in to a table" on the call of twelve. I cannot tell whether Madame Etcheto sat in Lesca's place.

What I would do: one line in 9 that accounts for the five hours, or change eight to the true figure. One clause in 16 that says whose place the chair was at.

**9. Chapter 2 asks me to carry too much before I care. (2)**

> "So there were two Peyrebarthes."

In 1,873 words I am given a second heroine, a stone, a committee, a three-minute rule, a kitchen timer, a deputy who returned her sash in a plastic bag in 2018, a former mayor, a widow, a gravel lake, a lease and the twelve-year delay. Lesca gets from a dream to a second village in one paragraph and never doubts it. I believed her only because chapter 1 had already shown me the other side.

What I would do: move the sash and the plastic bag to where they pay, in 16. Move the lake to 4, where Arlette makes it hurt. Chapter 2 then holds the dream, the stone, the verb and the date.

**10. Feelings are named after the scene has already shown them. (6, 7, 16)**

> "In thirty years at that counter Jeannot Castagnet had never left a glass unfinished.
>
> Maylis was ashamed."

The glass has said it. The same in 6, after Coralie's laugh: "It hurt to hear." And in 16, before the sentence that needs no help: "She was afraid." "She was scared" appears four times (00, 1, 13, 15); it works in 00 and at the reflector in 13 because the second answers the first.

What I would do: cut the three quoted above. Keep "She was scared" in 00 and 13 only.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The wrist.** Two fingers on the inside of it from chapter 1, through "That's her. In this hand" on the landing in 9, to Coralie holding the left wrist flat on the kitchen table, to the half minute on Madame Etcheto in room twelve.
2. **Chapter 6 entire.** The tick with a time on it, the window that goes down into the door, "It takes a small m", Josiane's ruling on flowers, the spoon tipped back into the bowl, the post at the bottom of the stairs.
3. **The evacuation and room twelve in 15**, from Kévin's van in the yard to "Forty-one": the times, Daguerre's "Here", the bins, Jeannot carrying Castéra, Kévin's five, the hatbox on the wardrobe.
4. **Either.** The shopping pad, the eggs, both boxes ticked, "Either is not a person", Either at the winch, "Both" down the phone, and the oven door in 18 that Coralie opens and cannot finish.
5. **Jeannot Castagnet's numbering**, all of it: "And six. Bugger off", "Jean's for the stone", the hand that stays up after the vote, No. 42 brought by hand, and the second shovel.
