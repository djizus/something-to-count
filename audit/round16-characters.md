# Round 16 — Characters

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

Names used below: **Maylis** is the keyholder in the village where the flood comes in 2026. **Lesca** is the mayor in the village where it came in 2014. Each has her own Coralie.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the care home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." Countable and never fudged. The private stake, the marriage, is set by one evening at an oven door.
- **Tension: 6.5.** "In her hand, the phone began to ring." Chapters 11 to 15 hold. Chapters 7 to 10 are petitions, votes and a folder.
- **Progression: 6.** Larrère makes the same threat three times: "That's prison, for eels" (7), "*Labat, Kévin.*" (10), "'No,' Larrère said. 'That's the file.'" (13). Nothing changes between the first and the third.
- **Comedy: 7.5.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" The jokes come out of what each person is.
- **Characters: 7.** The village is an 8, Lesca an 8, the Coralies 7.5, Maylis 6, Larrère 5. "They were the same height, and neither of them liked it" is the level the two women reach when they are in a room together, and they are in a room together five times.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Five,' he said. He said it like a man who turns over a card and finds it isn't the one he hoped for." A running joke about lucid dreaming becomes the worst moment of the flood without a word changed.
- **Prose: 7.** "She was scared." Plain to the point of refusing the women an inside. That refusal suits Lesca and starves Maylis.
- **The ending: 7.** "Then he stepped over the string in his waders and began to dig." The last page is right. The two scenes that should carry it there, the landing in 17 and the steel room in 18, are underweight.
- **Clarity: 6.** "One of us always wakes first, and she wakes at home, in her own bed. If the other one is still sitting in this chair at that moment, she is carried along." The mechanism needs a three-point lecture in chapter 5 and I still had to stop at 02:59 in chapter 15 to work out what Maylis thought she was doing.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. From the last line of chapter 4 I was not going to stop.

**Would I press it on a friend?** I would lend it. I would not press it, because I would have to say "push through 7 to 10" when I handed it over.

## 2. Against the comparisons — Characters

**Against Crouch: level.** Above him in two places, below in one.

- Above, on the double. Crouch's second Jason is a threat with the hero's face. Lesca is a person with a public record, a rule about three minutes, a mother who votes against her, and a sentence she has read into a microphone for eleven years. She states what she wants in the form of a council motion: "*That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*" Crouch has nothing like that sentence.
- Above, on the people around the leads. Crouch's secondary cast is furniture. Jeannot, Kévin, Madame Etcheto and Maïté would each be missed.
- Below, on wanting. By page twenty of Dark Matter the reader has seen one ordinary family evening and is afraid for it for three hundred pages, because Jason wants it out loud. Maylis's want for Coralie is never on the page in her own terms until "Come back across the landing" in chapter 13. The reader fears for that marriage on Coralie's behalf, not on Maylis's.

**Against The Good Place: below.**

- Level on "each wrong in their own way": Jeannot numbers, Larrère is brief, Sarthou files, Marie-Jo speaks through the chair, Lesca does the sum, Maylis says Either. Six distinct ways of not answering a question. That is the show's method and the book has it.
- Level on the reversal that re-reads what came before: "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." Coralie has known for twelve years, and every earlier scene on that landing changes.
- Below on the thing the show never fails at: its people own what they do. Eleanor's selfishness is hers, with no co-signer, and it is funny because nobody takes it off her. This book takes each woman's worst moment and hands part of it to somebody else. Faults 1, 2, 5 and 8 are all that one fault.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

### 1. The central choice is not Maylis's, and the book says it is — ch. 14, 15, 18

> "On foot she could reach the winch or the man. She could not reach both. If she went back, she would turn the handle on him, as Lesca had. If she went on, the river would take the Port.
> She got out and went on."

No reason is given. Four minutes earlier the book told me what Maylis would have done by herself: "Maylis wanted the gate. She meant to raise it now, with the man still in his cellar at the end of the track, because Coralie was under that bank." Her hand was on the key. Lesca saw it: "Maylis was about to do what Lesca had done. She would do it for the same woman, and to the same man." That is the best character turn in the book, the woman who cannot choose turning out to be a killer for love the moment she is alone, and it lasts four lines, is seen only by Lesca, and is never known by Maylis.

What turns her is Lesca's "Go to the blind first." So the woman who is always told ("You're told," Jeannot says in chapter 1) obeys the last order she was given. Then chapter 15 claims the choice for her ("Maylis thought she could do better"), chapter 18 has Lesca claim the delay ("Those two minutes are mine") and credit Maylis with the rest ("You rang nine times. You walked his track at dusk, and you ran it in the night"), and nobody in chapter 18 mentions the five words at all, though chapter 17 trailed them: "I don't know yet what that cost."

Three authors for one act, plus a winch that Jeannot cannot turn either, so that going back might not have worked. Nobody owns Madame Etcheto.

**What to do.** Decide whose choice 02:59 is and remove the others.
- If it is Maylis's: cut "Go to the blind first," or have her hear it and set off for her own reason. Put on the page, at 02:59, the thing she tells herself, which is still Both: his van is at the blind, it knows the ruts, three minutes back. Let the reader watch her do the arithmetic that lets her not choose. Then "That was Either, with the river in it" lands on something the reader saw.
- Make her own the hand on the key. In chapter 18 she says "I'd sign your sum" from hindsight. She should say it from memory: her hand was on the key before Lesca had the wheel.
- Drop "Those two minutes are mine," or have Maylis refuse the gift. As written it is one woman excusing the other, and they agree too fast.
- Have the winch turn for Jeannot. If the gate could not have been raised, nothing anyone chose mattered.

### 2. At five o'clock, Either is given an alibi — ch. 13

> "On Tuesday, when she said open, the sheet in the folder had been blank. Now it had Kévin Labat's name on it. [...] 'Either,' Maylis said."

This is the real hinge, and Madame Darrigrand knows it: "At five on Friday it was light, and Castagnet was up on the bank at your van window. You had the key on your belt then." The handle is on the winch, the man is above ground, it is daylight. Twelve chapters have built Either as her fault. At the one moment it kills somebody, the book supplies a decent motive: she is keeping a boy out of prison. The flaw is excused in the act.

The sentence that makes it hers is already in the scene: "The only thing missing was the order." A woman who cannot turn a handle without being told is the character. A woman being blackmailed is a plot.

And the debt is never paid. In chapter 18 Kévin says "For four winters you looked the other way for nothing. I can do a morning." He does not know that his name on a sheet is why the gate stayed shut, and Maylis does not think it. "I wanted six, Maylis" is the best line in the book and would be unbearable if he knew.

**What to do.** Take the folder out of the five o'clock scene. Leave her with the handle on, Jeannot asking "Shut, or open?", and no order. If the folder stays, pay it: one of them has to know in chapter 18 what Kévin's freedom cost.

### 3. The two women's last meeting is half a page of advice — ch. 18

> "'Your Coralie gives orders,' Maylis said. '[...] Sit down when she tells you.'
> 'Yours said she'd have the one who answers. She had two fingers on your wrist when she said it. Answer her.'"

This is the relationship the premise promises, and it gets five scenes, of which this is the last and shortest. A week earlier Lesca announced she was taking Maylis's body for good and tried to walk it up the stairs to Coralie. Maylis has one line of anger about it ("Two minutes, because you wanted my bed") and then offers to sign Lesca's sum. They swap tips on each other's wives and the thing leaves. Neither asks the other what she makes of her. "Why did it rain?" gets "I don't know" twice.

The room has a rule that is set in chapter 5 and used once: "When we're on our feet we can talk, and neither of us can lie." Chapter 10 uses it ("I can't stand here and say I'll go, because in here it would have to be true") and it is the best exchange they have.

**What to do.** Give this scene the length of the landing scene in chapter 12 and build it on that rule. Each should have to say one thing she would lie about anywhere else. Lesca: whether she still wants to stay. Maylis: whether she is glad Jeannot is alive at that price. Cut the exchange of advice; neither woman takes it anyway.

### 4. Maylis's sixteen years have no past, and her want has no root — ch. 1, 3, 10

> "'Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you, and that was me.'"

That is the whole history of the marriage the reader is asked to fear for. Lesca's has a wall outside a barn and a sandwich, a week of shouting in February, a phone on a chest of drawers. Lesca also has a mother across the yard with ponies and a grudge. Maylis's mother is "her mother's ponies" and never appears, in the one village where the pasture was never sold.

Her other want arrives in a sentence: "The square in front of her was full of people, and they were waiting for her to begin. She wanted that." It returns once, well ("I want the timer, and a table of people who won't sit down until I do"), is spent in thirteen hours that chapter 8 covers in a hundred lines, and is gone. By the end she wants to dig a grave. Nothing connects the three.

**What to do.** Show the one straight answer. Lesca receives Maylis's afternoons in the chair; let that one arrive, in chapter 4, as the thing that makes her write the second half of the motion. One scene with Maylis's mother, who still has her grass, would tell the reader where Either comes from or at least who has had to live with it longest.

### 5. Lesca is let off for the bed — ch. 17

> "Coralie asked nothing about the gate."

Lesca tells her wife two things: that she held on for two minutes and an old woman Coralie wheels every day is dead of it in the other village, and that she twice asked the other Coralie into bed with a body that was not hers. The book has already shown the second one cold: "By now she had the voice nearly right, higher, the way Maylis used it." The other Coralie named it exactly: "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them."

This Coralie answers with a joke about four steps and a timetable: "The pillow stays where it is. After Tuesday, when they've voted." On Tuesday it comes back. The woman who concealed a death, sent Kévin to prison and tried to take another woman's life ends the book with the pillow on the wall side. The woman who tried to save everybody ends alone. That inversion is worth having. It has to be paid for, and "an hour" is not a price.

**What to do.** Coralie has to answer the mouth, not the landing. Either the pillow does not cross inside the book, or she says what it costs her to carry it. She is the one character with the standing to say it.

### 6. Lesca's Coralie is given a reason that the book then contradicts — ch. 8, 12

> "'Do you know what I'd give for a woman at this table who sat down when she was told? I'd carry my pillow back across that landing tonight.'"

Four chapters later the true reason for the landing arrives: "I wanted you in the house. I thought I could have you without it, if I kept a landing between us." That is about a phone and a question. It has nothing to do with obedience at a soup plate. The chapter 8 speech exists so that each Coralie prefers the other woman, a neat cross, and one half of it is false. Chapter 17 then adds a third reason ("I'm not carrying it in to the mayor").

**What to do.** Keep the cross only if both halves are true. What this Coralie should notice at the table is something that touches the secret: a woman across from her who can laugh at Jean Castagnet, who has nothing at nine minutes to three.

### 7. Larrère has a window and a folder and no inside — ch. 7, 10, 13, 17, 18

> "'So there's the question of the winch,' he said. 'It isn't settled, and you're not to worry about it.'"

He is a very good joke for six chapters. As the antagonist of a book about a moral choice he is an obstacle, and the folder turns a village farce into a blackmail plot. The most interesting fact about him is buried: in 2014 he gave the order to keep the gate shut from the kitchen of the man who would drown if it opened. Was he protecting Jean? Maïté says not: "You said it made no difference where he was." He answers nothing. His silence at the garden gate pays off "I'll be brief." It does not make him a person.

He is Either with a title. "Touch nothing until I ring" is the same refusal as hers, made by a man who can make other people carry it. The book never lets Maylis see that.

**What to do.** One threat with the folder, not three. And one moment where Maylis, or the reader, recognises her own word coming out of his window.

### 8. The one clean cost of Either is never collected — ch. 3, 15

> "She had her answer ready. It was the pawl. Six men turned to hear it.
> 'Either,' she said.
> 'The keyholder has no preference,' Larrère said, and the member who kept the minute book wrote that down."

Eight days later the winch sticks at nineteen with the river in the Port, and this is the exchange:

> "'I told them in 2011 that handle needed looking at. I put it in number six.'
> 'I wrote to them too.'
> 'I know you did. They took note.'"

She was asked which repair she wanted and said Either. The book forgets and lets her take the credit for two letters. This is the only place where her word, with no file and no second woman in it, has a direct consequence.

**What to do.** One line. Somebody remembers the minute book.

### 9. Maylis's Coralie is never frightened — ch. 9

> "'That's her,' she said. 'In this hand. I've had sixteen years of her saying nothing, and I know it when I've got hold of it.' She looked up at the face. 'And you're the one I had soup with.'"

Twelve days after a cracked rib, a trained carer is addressing two people in one body, one of them by the wrist, without having rung anyone or doubted her own mind. She has the doctor's number on the screen in chapter 9 and her thumb never comes down. Her wit is the best in the book and it stands in for a reaction. Crouch makes the reader afraid for the marriage through the wife's unease. This wife is never uneasy, so the reader is never afraid for her, only for what she will decide.

**What to do.** One beat where the method fails her: she cannot find the pulse, or she makes the call.

### 10. The first meeting is spent on rules the reader has already watched — ch. 5

> "'The first item is how this works. I'll say it once, so we both have it the same. There are three points.'"

Chapters 3 and 4 showed all three points happening. Chairing the meeting is Lesca to the life, and "There's nobody in the chair" is funny. But the scene the premise sells, what a woman says to herself, begins only at "You held her hand" and has eight lines before the flood briefing takes over.

**What to do.** Keep the one rule that is new, that the unfetched one is found dead. Give the room to "I held yours. She put two fingers on it, and I took them."

**Asked directly by the brief, and not a fault:** neither woman is described from outside as a woman. Neither has a face, a body or an age on the page. "The village's girl," "little Lesca" and "Monsieur le maire" on the prefect's sheet are the village's diminishments and the book uses them against the village. Maylis is described from outside in a different sense: as a silence that other people talk into.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie at the oven door**, chapter 1, and the same business broken off in chapter 18: "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'". The marriage is made in the first and ended in the second.
2. **The phone log on the landing**, chapter 12: "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." and "'Because you'd have told me.'" The best scene in the book.
3. **Kévin's fingers.** The count under the plane trees, the count at four in the yard with a wheelchair half up on the van, and "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much."
4. **Jeannot whole.** Nine rings and "And six. Bugger off." "It's Jeannot. Jean's for the stone." Letter forty-two, which "doesn't thank you," and the second shovel.
5. **The hand against the mouth.** "Maylis threw herself at the jaw, and got it. 'Either,' Maylis said." The spoon tipped back into the bowl. The post at the bottom of the stairs. The palm clapped over her own teeth while Coralie pins the other wrist. This is how the two women fight, it is new, and it is funny and frightening in the same gesture.

Also leave alone: the Kévin of Lesca's village taking his cap off to be told what he has done wrong; "Staff to wait. Staff not to join in."; "And then there's me, as a distance."
