**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. From chapter 11 on I could not have stopped.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On the friend who preferred the trolley-problem episode to the chases, yes. On a Crouch reader, no: not until chapters 7 to 9 are scenes.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below The Good Place on comedy, by one point. Level with it on the switch. Crouch does not compete on this lens.**

What puts it below:

- **Rate and range.** The series gets a laugh every half minute and gets it four ways: verbal, visual, absurd, and character. This book has one way: the official form applied to a private matter (a motion to take another woman's bed, a verb put to the vote, a registered letter that is not a complaint). It is a good joke. It is nearly the only one. Six scenes made me laugh out loud in 42,400 words: the oven (1), Bixente and the stars (3), the chrysanthemums (5), the café (6), "And six. Bugger off." (11), the Hontaube telephone call (13). The last 11,600 words have wry beats and no laughs.
- **The premise's comic engine runs once.** The series would have taken "two women in one body with one hand each" and "a room where neither can lie" and built an episode on each. The book builds one chapter (6) on the first and one scene (5) on the second, then summarises both.
- **The comic reversal is delivered secondhand.** The series' great reversal re-reads everything before it and is itself a laugh. This book has four reversals that re-read (she saw the van; Coralie has known since the morning after; the kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice; the cellar was dry). Only the last is comic, and it reaches the reader as a paraphrase by Marie-Jo.

What puts it level:

- **The people.** Larrère through his gap, Jeannot numbering, Marie-Jo through the chair, Sarthou billing the village for his own sandbags, Daguerre's "Here", Kévin's questions, Coralie's boxes. Each has one fault of their own and each fault is broken exactly once, late, for effect (the window goes down; "I'll be brief" is followed by nothing; Marie-Jo says "Maylis"; nobody calls out at nineteen; letter 42 is not a complaint).
- **The moral problem is funny without being small.** The trolley problem is put through a kitchen timer, a shopping pad and a police form: "'One ring is an attempt,' Lesca said. 'It's also all there was of it. The form needs a third box.'" (17) That is the series' method, done in paperwork.
- **Every running joke is collected as a debt.** The series cashes its callbacks in sentiment. This book cashes them in consequence: the café's joke on Monday is why Jeannot is in his blind on Friday ("So I'll go down on Friday the eleventh, because you've said it"); "Either" at the winch in chapter 3 is the minutes, in chapter 7 a police file, in chapter 13 the gate.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Chapter 8: the mirror of the best chapter is half its length and told in summary. (Structural.)**

> "He went on, like a man who finds a door unlocked. He had eleven years of it saved up, and it lasted eleven minutes. Near the end he was saying things twice, and looking at the timer in between for help."

Chapter 6 (Lesca driving the village's girl) is 2,457 words in five played scenes, each one worse for Maylis than the last, and it is the comic peak of the book. Chapter 8 (the village's girl driving the mayor) is the same idea turned over and should be funnier: a woman who says Either has a sash, a timer, and a table that will not sit until she does. It gets 1,355 words. The eleven minutes are three sentences of report. Chapters 7, 8 and 9 together are 4,165 words, fewer than chapter 15 alone. The premise is spent before the reader has had it.

Do: make 8 the length and shape of 6. Play Larrère's eleven minutes in his own words until he is begging the timer. Give Maylis a morning of being the mayor wrong in her own way: asked to rule and ruling Either, signing with the tall L, the council treating it as policy. "I want that." "The timer? You have it, Madame le maire. It's in the inventory." is the one played exchange and it shows what the rest should be.

**2. The room where neither can lie is funny once. (Structural.)**

> "When we're on our feet we can talk, and neither of us can lie." (5)

Chapter 5's meeting ("'Then it's carried, or it's a tie, and in a tie the chair decides.' 'There's nobody in the chair.'") is the scene the whole book is for: two versions of one person, each wrong, arguing procedure about a body. After it the steel room is a place for handing over plot. Chapter 7's meeting is eight lines ("'Take Monday back.' 'I can't, and I wouldn't.'"). Chapter 10's is a negotiation with one joke in it ("Nil."). The no-lying rule is used once, for pathos ("I can't stand here and say I'll go, because in here it would have to be true"), and never for a laugh. The two women are the series' Eleanor and Chidi and they are funny together in one scene out of five.

Do: make the rule bite. In 7 and 10 each of them should start a sentence that would work on a council or on Coralie and be unable to finish it. Lesca has twelve years of true sentences that mislead ("No one came to tell him"); the room should be the one place they do not come out. That is a comic scene and it is also the confession arriving early, in pieces.

**3. The café is the book's chorus and it is dropped after chapter 10. Its last joke is reported and then explained. (Structural.)**

> "'This morning a man came in late, and they told him he'd had the key and gone for a walk.' She looked at the way Maylis held her side. 'That's what late means now. They've retired the other one.'" (18)

The village's joke about the box, replaced overnight by a crueller joke about Maylis, is the best dark-comic idea in the ending. In chapter 1 it is heard through a door. In chapter 6 it is said to her face and Lesca answers it. In chapter 18 it should be played a third time, and it is paraphrased by a bystander who then tells the reader what it means. Lafitte's pad headed *Madame le maire* is never closed. Josiane's theory has attached the adultery to the wrong woman since chapter 6, and the café never learns whose car was in the back lane, though Jeannot's letter 42 now says it in writing.

Do: one scene at the café door in 18. A man comes in late. Somebody says the new line. Lafitte turns the pad to a page. Maïté's bus leaves in front of the window and Josiane does the arithmetic out loud. Cut Marie-Jo's explanation to "They've retired the other one."

**4. Bingo night is thrown away, and its best gag is about a moment the reader never saw. (Chapter 9.)**

> "The ham was the last prize, and he won it." (9)
> "He's been one number short of the grass trimmer ever since bingo night." (13)

The dead play bingo. The living play it once, in thirty lines, and nobody calls a number. The hall of the dead in chapters 10 and 14 is meant to be a village evening gone wrong, and the reader has not had the village evening. Daguerre's "Here" at every number, the duck, the grass trimmer, the caller giving each number whole and then in parts: all of it is set up in other chapters and none of it is on the page where it happens.

Do: play the bingo before Inguma comes across the hall. Then "Twelve. One, two." in chapter 16 is the caller the reader laughed at.

**5. Maylis is the straight woman of her own book for eight chapters.**

> "Maylis had not written a line for that. She did not know what to say." (10)

"Either" is one word, and it is always somebody else who makes it funny (Coralie: "I don't cook Either"; Sarthou: "I cannot summon Either to the station"). Chidi's indecision is funny because he can talk. Maylis has a comic voice of her own, measurement in place of feeling, and it turns up late: "It's twenty minutes to Bayonne." (9) "She took more than that to the tip in a morning." (18) "It was closer to a kilometre and two hundred metres. She didn't correct her." (18) Before chapter 9 she has it twice ("It had taken her an hour to lock a house with one door" is the narrator's, not hers).

Do: give her that voice from chapter 1. When she cannot say what she wants she should say what it weighs, how far it is, or what the job costs. Then "Both" on the telephone in 15 is a woman who has measured everything and measured this wrong.

**6. The laugh in the minute of silence is set up by explanation and over in twelve lines. (Chapter 8.)**

> "What Maylis knew of him was forty-one registered letters, and Lesca had been given some of them in the chair. Letter seventeen was his bill to the village for two ruts in his track. Lesca felt the laugh start under the ribs."

This is the cruellest joke in the book and its best idea about the two villages: to one of them the dead man is a saint on a stone, to the other he is a nuisance at a counter. The narrator tells the reader why it is funny and then the laugh happens. The reader does not laugh with her, so Maïté's "What is there to laugh at in him, Madame le maire? Tell me. In twelve years I haven't found it" lands on Maylis alone.

Do: have the committee supply the trigger. Somebody reads a line of tribute into the silence ("a man who asked nothing of this village") and the reader, who has heard about forty-one registered letters, laughs half a second before Maylis does.

**7. Chapter 4 replays chapter 3's machinery, and chapter 2 ends by explaining the premise three times.**

> "If she were dreaming the card, the word would have changed. If she were remembering it, it would have said *right*, because she had never in her life written the other word." (4)
> "The rain was falling on the other village. That village had not had its flood yet. The rain that had brought the flood here in 2014 had started to fall there that morning, twelve years late." (2)

The reader has just watched Maylis open the envelope, lie still, find the chair warm and read the card twice. Chapter 4 does it again from the other seat for thirty-seven lines before anything new happens. It is the longest chapter in the first seven and has three comic beats.

Do: cut the envelope and the first visit to the chair to a third. Open on the crossing, which is the scene: "Where did you get eleven?" Cut the last paragraph of chapter 2 to its final sentence.

**8. Payoffs explained after they have landed.**

> "'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much.'" (18)

Chapter 15 already did this without a word of gloss: "'Five,' he said. He said it like a man who turns over a card and finds it isn't the one he hoped for." The same fault in 17: "who had kept a gate shut against him for twelve years and had now spoken to him across it". And in 15: "Half an hour earlier he had been dragged out of a dry cellar by the neck and told that the gate was going to be opened on him."

Do: cut all three. In 18 keep Kévin flying and not liking it ("You can see what's under you") and stop there.

**9. One joke that costs the dark: Jeannot's three points at the care-home door. (Chapter 15.)**

> "'Two. I got my shoulder under it, and my back, and my boots slid out from under me on your planks, and I've done something to my knee. Three. It never got past nineteen, so the gate is up by the width of a hand and no more.'"

The water has gone from shins to knees, there is nobody to carry Castéra, and the clock stops for 130 words of a routine the reader has had three times (6, 10, 11) and had at its best in 11. The news (the winch stuck at nineteen) is needed. The numbering is not, here.

Do: "It's stuck. Nineteen. A hand." Then "I put it in number six", then Castéra. He numbers again in letter 42, where it belongs.

**10. Punchline tics and one cadence.**

> "In eighteen years beside that car Maylis had never seen it do that." (6)
> "In thirty years at that counter Jeannot Castagnet had never left a glass unfinished." (7)
> "Until tonight it had never been off her belt." (15)

The same button on three scenes, two of them in consecutive chapters. "Wrote that down" closes a beat six times (3, 6, 10 twice, 16, 17). "A hand" as a measure appears 29 times. The narrator can be witty ("The rule applied to every member and had been needed for one"; "They were the same height, and neither of them liked it") and does it about once a chapter. Every other joke is carried by dialogue in short sentences of one length, so the timing never changes.

Do: keep the window, cut the glass and the belt. Keep the minute book in 3 and Lafitte's pad in 6, cut the rest. Give the narrator one dry sentence per scene and let some of them be long.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The oven, both times.** Coralie doing Larrère through the oven door in chapter 1 ("'In my tray,' she said, and shut the door on him"), and in chapter 18 getting as far as "The—" and shutting it.
2. **The end of chapter 11.** The whole of the drowning, then nine rings, then "'Who's dead?'" and six numbered points ending "Bugger off." "She listened to all of it."
3. **The verb for the stone.** "It reads that she opened him." The timer ringing on Maïté's verb in 2. Her verb in 17 being the word on Maylis's card. Lesca's in 18.
4. **The square after the sentence in chapter 16.** Tastet turning the sheet over to see whether there is more on the back. The man beyond the last loudspeaker who claps because he thinks she has finished. "Somebody has to stack them. You can't do it in that."
5. **Lesca in the hall of the dead in chapter 14.** "'I saw your van,' she said. 'I rang once.' He put a kernel on a card." Then: "The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies … You can put that in the minutes as apologies received."
