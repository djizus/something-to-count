# Critic report: Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

Counts below are taken across all nineteen files.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.5.** "The hatch was twenty-five minutes away on foot. The bank had ten." Two numbers carry the whole dilemma, and the book sets them twice.
- **Tension: 7.** Chapter 15 runs on a stamped clock ("02:59." then "The bank had six minutes left"), and it works. Chapters 7 to 9 have none: "For two nights Maylis pushed up at Inguma with all the breath she had, and stayed out of the chair."
- **Progression: 6.** The middle repeats one move (ride, counter-ride, vote lost) in summary: "He had eleven years of it saved up, and it lasted eleven minutes."
- **Comedy: 7.5.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'"
- **Characters: 7.** Coralie is the best thing in the book: "I've held hands for a living since I was nineteen. I know when a hand is holding on, and I know when it wants to be let go." The other women speak with her syntax.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 7.5.** In the flooded yard Kévin counts his fingers: "'Five,' he said. He said it like a man who turns over a card and finds it isn't the one he hoped for." The joke planted in chapter 3 comes back as grief.
- **Prose: 6.5.** Clean and easy to follow at its best, but one pulse for every scene, and it clots when two women share a body: "Lesca kept the eyes on it for longer than a cut needed. 'I have that,' Lesca said. […] Lesca let the gate down one click at a time".
- **The ending: 7.5.** "Then he stepped over the string in his waders and began to dig." The last page is right. The first scene of the last chapter talks the cost through before the reader is allowed to feel it.
- **Clarity: 6.** "She wanted Monday taken back. Lesca could take back her share of it now, in the steel room. Guy Larrère could have the rest at nine, as an apology." I read that three times.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. From chapter 11 I did not stop. Chapters 7 to 9 I read on credit earned by chapter 6.

**Would I press it on a friend?** Not in this state. I would tell a friend about room twelve and the shopping pad. I would hand over the book once chapters 7 to 9 are scenes.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch as a whole, level with him from chapter 11 to chapter 16, above him in one respect. Below The Good Place on voices.**

1. **Crouch's narrator sits inside one skull.** Dark Matter is first person, present tense, and when Jason is afraid the sentence says so and the paragraph shrinks to a line. This narrator stands outside and reports hands, coats and clock times. That restraint pays in room twelve, where nothing is said and nothing needs to be. It fails in the chapters where two women drive one body, because the narrator then stands outside two skulls at once and has to name each of them in nearly every sentence. Chapter 6 uses "Lesca" 40 times and "Maylis" 38 times in 2,457 words, a proper name every 31 words.

2. **Crouch changes rhythm when the danger changes.** His calm chapters have long paragraphs and his chases have fragments. Here the sentence that rakes leaves and the sentence that watches a bank fail are the same length and the same shape, and about two in three sections open on a clock time, a date or the weather. Only chapter 15 breaks the pattern, with bare stamps ("03:11."), and that chapter is as good as his.

3. **The Good Place passes a blind test on any line.** Take away the name and you still know Eleanor from Chidi from Tahani. Here the men are told apart by a device (Jeannot numbers his points, Larrère says "I'll be brief", Sarthou and the priest do not contract). Take the device away and Coralie, Maïté, Arlette, Marie-Jo and Madame Darrigrand speak one sentence: a count of years, then an order.

**Where it is above Crouch:** objects. His props are generic. This book's shopping pad, kitchen timer, crooked reflector, tan suitcase and two fingers on a wrist are each planted, reused and paid. No object is wasted.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. Chapters 7, 8 and 9 are synopsis (structural)

They run 1,233, 1,355 and 1,577 words. The chapters that work run 2,400 to 4,500. The premise of the book is that one woman lives a day in the other's life, and the second and third of those days are told in outline.

Chapter 8 is the only time Maylis is mayor. In 1,355 words she meets a broken Kévin, laughs in the widow's face, is offered Coralie's pillow, chairs a council and finds the rule that drives the next five chapters. None of it is a scene. The best comic idea in the chapter gets four sentences and no dialogue:

> He spoke, and no bell rang. At three minutes he stopped for the bell, out of habit. None came. He went on, like a man who finds a door unlocked. He had eleven years of it saved up, and it lasted eleven minutes.

Chapter 9 summarises a struggle and a takeover in three sentences:

> For two nights Maylis pushed up at Inguma with all the breath she had, and stayed out of the chair. On the Friday night the cracked rib had no push left in it. […] At six an alarm rang across the landing, and Lesca came home in her.

Chapter 7 opens with a paragraph of intentions that cannot be parsed without rereading (quoted under Clarity).

**What I would do.** Write chapter 8 at the length of chapter 6. Give Larrère three of his unstopped sentences and let the table look at the timer. Play the meeting with Kévin as an exchange. Put Maylis in front of the stone that has her name on it, which the chapter never does. In chapter 9, cut the two nights of pushing to one line or play one of them. In chapter 7, replace the first paragraph with the line it is hiding: she stands up in the steel room and says "Take Monday back."

### 2. The narrator is in both heads at once, and worst at the climax (chapters 6, 8, 9, 14)

Chapter 14 is the moment the two women fight for the van. It is told as two statements of intention and then explained:

> Maylis wanted the gate. She meant to raise it now, with the man still in his cellar at the end of the track, because Coralie was under that bank. […] Lesca knew that movement, because she had made it herself. Maylis was about to do what Lesca had done. She would do it for the same woman, and to the same man. Lesca wanted the man.

The chapter is Lesca's. The first two sentences are inside Maylis. The fourth and fifth tell the reader what the third already showed. The same happens in chapter 8 ("Lesca felt the laugh start under the ribs" and, two lines on, "Maylis had the mouth, and she put nothing in it") and in chapter 11, a Maylis chapter that reports Lesca's motive from her own bed: "She didn't want to stand in that room and be asked again whether she knew the man was out on the meadows."

Chapter 6 shows how to do it. It stays with Maylis, and Lesca is known only through what the body does: "Maylis felt her take a breath for it, as deep as the cracked rib allowed, and the mouth opened."

**What I would do.** One head per chapter. In chapter 14 stay in Lesca: the right hand goes to the key, then "Lesca knew that movement, because she had made it herself." Cut the two sentences before it and the two after. In chapter 8 choose Lesca, who has one hand and watches her own body misbehave. In chapter 11 cut the paragraph that explains why the chair is cold. Cold arms are enough.

### 3. The last chapter opens with a debrief (structural)

Chapter 18 begins in the steel room, where the two women add up the night aloud and exchange advice:

> 'That was two minutes. You were two hundred metres short of the bank when it broke, and two minutes would have covered them. Those two minutes are mine.'

> 'Don't sign my sum for me. I've hidden behind it for twelve years.'

> 'Sit down when she tells you.' […] 'Answer her.'

The reader did this sum in chapter 15 from the clock stamps. Chapter 16 has already told us who died ("'Twelve. One, two.' A chair was pulled in to a table"). Chapter 17 has Lesca recite it to Coralie on the landing ("I held on to it while a phone rang eight times. When I let go I said five words with her mouth"). Chapter 18 is the third account of one death, and it is the one where the characters state the theme.

There is a second cost. Chapter 17 ends on Tuesday with the pillow. Chapter 18 then goes back to Sunday at 02:51 and has Lesca report "they've taken back the medal, and the keys", which we watched.

**What I would do.** Cut the scene to six lines: "Forty-one." "I heard her chair pulled in." "Those two minutes are mine." "The van in the dip was mine. So was saying both." "What was the verb?" "Drowned." Then Inguma goes out by the door. Cut the advice. Cut the landing recap in chapter 17 down to the one fact Coralie does not have, which is room twelve.

### 4. The narrator explains what the scene has just shown

Each of these follows a scene that made the point without help:

- Ch 6: "Maylis had never got that laugh out of her. Lesca had got it in ten minutes, with Maylis's mouth."
- Ch 6: "It would leave Coralie with the one who said Either. And Coralie had just said which of them she wanted back."
- Ch 9: "It was what Maylis had brought home and had not dared to write. […] It also asked for the one thing she wanted, and it asked out loud."
- Ch 11: "It wasn't Maylis's minute. She had never had a time of her own."
- Ch 13: "Nobody had given her this night. It was her own, and it was happening."
- Ch 15: "Lesca's choice was in front of her now, all of it."
- Ch 15: "Half an hour earlier he had been dragged out of a dry cellar by the neck and told that the gate was going to be opened on him."
- Ch 16: "His sheet had a line for a mayor who could not attend. It had no line for a mayor who stood on her cross of tape with nothing more to read."
- Ch 17: "the woman at the bottom of the ladder, who had kept a gate shut against him for twelve years and had now spoken to him across it."
- Ch 18: "'That's what late means now. They've retired the other one.'"

A cousin of this is the certificate of rarity, where the narrator vouches for a gesture: "In thirty years at that counter Jeannot Castagnet had never left a glass unfinished." "In eighteen years beside that car Maylis had never seen it do that." "Until tonight it had never been off her belt." There are fifteen "had never" and "never once" in the book. The half-full glass and the window going down into the door do not need a witness statement.

**What I would do.** Delete every sentence in the list. Keep one certificate (the window) and cut the rest.

### 5. One voice in every mouth

More than a dozen lines of dialogue are built "I've [done this] for [N] years", spoken by six different people:

- Coralie: "I've had sixteen years of her saying nothing, and I know it when I've got hold of it."
- Maïté: "I've had silence from you for twelve Decembers, and I won't have it in my own kitchen."
- Josiane: "I've kept this counter thirty years."
- Lesca: "I've carried that night for twelve years."
- Arlette: "I've listened to the lake for you, and the four houses, over my own coffee."
- Jeannot: "I've got forty-one of them standing, and I've never taken one back."

The narrator joins in. "Twelve years" appears 25 times and "eleven years" 13.

The other shared shape is the two-beat closer. "I'd never hear the end of it. I wouldn't want to hear the beginning." "I'm not a visitor. I'm her mother." "It's Jeannot. Jean's for the stone." "'Is that advice?' 'No. That's the file.'" "It's four when you're the one counting. When it's your house it's one." Coralie alone has "Don't give me the minutes", "Don't give me the measurement" and "Don't tell me what he was". Each line is good. Together they tell the reader that one hand wrote every speaker, and a villager who exists to deliver one quotable line (Darrigrand in chapter 4) reads as a device.

**What I would do.** The count of years belongs to Coralie, who reads charts for a living. Leave it with her and take it from the rest. Maïté already has her own way of talking, which is to change the subject to the house ("There's a tile gone over the back bedroom", "Winter things"). Use it in chapter 17 in place of her aphorisms. Give Arlette food and animals. Keep one two-beat closer per chapter.

### 6. The premise and the rules are announced, not found (chapters 2 and 5)

Chapter 2 ends with the narrator stating the hook as settled fact, four sentences after a dream:

> The rain was falling on the other village. That village had not had its flood yet. The rain that had brought the flood here in 2014 had started to fall there that morning, twelve years late. If it kept to the same dates, the flood would come there on the night of the eleventh of December.

Lesca has no evidence yet. The evidence arrives in chapter 4, in a far better form: "*26 November 2014. Under the line. Rain.*" By then the reader has been told the answer.

Chapter 5 gives the rules of the device as a numbered agenda of nearly three hundred words ("There are three points. One. When Inguma sits on two people at the same time, it puts them in one seat…"). The meeting-procedure joke carries point one. Point two restates what chapter 4 showed, and Maylis then restates it again: "So the one who stays sitting rides the one who wakes." One rule is asserted and never tested until chapter 10: "neither of us can lie."

**What I would do.** End chapter 2 on "the last one was about low water" and cut the four sentences after it. Let the file do the deduction in chapter 4. In chapter 5 cut point two and keep Maylis's one-line version.

### 7. The metronome

Of 87 chapter and section openings, 56 begin with a clock time, a date or the weather. Chapter 6 has five sections: "It rained on Monday the thirtieth of November", "At eight Lesca stood Maylis on the platform", "At nine Guy Larrère stopped his car", "At noon Lesca walked Maylis into the café", "At seven there was soup". Inside the sections, 90 sentences begin "Then" and 69 begin "It was".

The cost is paid in chapter 15. The bare stamp ("02:57.") is the best device in the book, and by the time it arrives the reader has been given the time of day fifty times for scenes where it did not matter.

**What I would do.** Keep the day and date at the head of each chapter, because the countdown needs them. Before chapter 13, take the clock time out of every section opening where the hour is not the point, and open on the action: "Guy Larrère stopped his car beside the village van." Leave 02:51 alone wherever it appears. From chapter 13 on, change nothing.

### 8. Everything is measured against a hand, a door or a house

Seventeen measurements by the hand, 24 uses of "as wide as", "the size of", "the height of" or "as high as", and 47 of "the way".

"A hand of water" is a planted term and it pays: "the gate is up by the width of a hand and no more. A hand." But the hand has also measured a care home door ("open about the width of a hand"), a hatch ("lifted a hand's width"), a van roof ("a hand above her head") and Inguma ("a hand's width from her chin"). The leak is "as wide as a door" three times, the breach "as wide as a house" twice in one chapter and then "as wide as a barn". Things are the size of a sheep, a garden shed, a postcard and a plate.

**What I would do.** Keep the hand for water and for the gate, and nothing else. Give the leak its door once. Halve "the way", keeping the comparisons that come from a trade ("the way she worked out the price of a job").

### 9. The stock entrance

People arrive in a garment, holding an object. "Coralie came to the doorway in her dressing gown and stood with one hand on the door frame." "Coralie came to the bedroom door in her work tunic, with her keys in her hand." "Coralie was standing in the bedroom doorway with the speech in her hand." Arlette is "in her coat, with her bag on her arm" three times. Maïté holds her bag in front of her three times. "Jeannot Castagnet turned on his stool with his glass" appears word for word in chapters 6 and 10. The tunic appears 12 times, the dressing gown 7, a bag 9.

The two fingers on the wrist appear 12 times. That gesture has a payoff, on a dead woman's wrist in chapter 15, and the payoff is blunted by the count.

**What I would do.** Tunic four times, bag three. Keep the two fingers in chapters 1, 4, 9, 15 and 16 and cut the others.

### 10. Spelling, usage and small slips

- **Mixed English.** The book is British (colour, metres, judgement, windscreen, pavement, tarmac, wheelie bin, pyjamas, "rang" 36 times). Against that: "flashlight" 25 times, "dish towel" 7, "truck" in chapters 4 and 12 but "lorry" in chapter 15, "phoned" 3 times. Pick one.
- **Prepositions.** "the prosecutor in Bayonne" (ch 7), "the prosecutor at Bayonne" (ch 13, twice); "the prefect's office in Pau" (ch 6), "the prefecture at Pau" (elsewhere).
- **One piece of ground, seven names.** "flood meadows", "low meadows", "the meadows", "common pasture", "the common", "common land", "grazing land". From the text alone I cannot be sure that Lesca's gravel lake covers the same grass the river went out over.
- **Two gates in one scene.** Chapter 13 has "the gate to the platform" and "the gate" within a page ("'That platform's locked.' 'The gate to it is locked.'").
- **Narrator's contractions.** "did not" 12 times and "didn't" 46, with no pattern: "He did not look up from his numbers. She did not stop." against "Lesca didn't answer."
- **Ch 10.** "'Today I asked for the gate'" is said at nine minutes to three on Wednesday about Tuesday afternoon.
- **Ch 2.** Lesca has "forty registered letters" in her head. Chapter 1 delivers "*No. 41*" the same morning. The night-time count is defensible, but the chapter repeats "forty" that afternoon and it reads as an error.
- **Ch 13.** "'It's a long way to bring it,' Maylis said." I do not know what this line means or why it makes the woman post the form.
- **Ch 12, 14, 16.** One idea in three places: "It's true, every word of it"; "every one of them was true"; "Every word of it was true, and it was typed." Keep the last.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 15 from "02:55." to "'Forty-one,' Coralie said."** The clock, "'Here.' 'I know you are.'", "'Then they'll want the bins out'", the three open doors before the shut one, the hatbox on the wardrobe, the pencil line through the last name. Not a word.
2. **The shopping pad and "Either".** From "'I don't cook Either. There's no recipe for it'" through the two ticked boxes to "'That was Either, with the river in it.'" It is the comedy and the marriage in one object.
3. **The nine rings and Jeannot's answer in chapter 11.** "'Who's dead?'" and the numbered tirade down to "'And six. Bugger off.'" followed by "She listened to all of it." The whole book turns on a living man being rude.
4. **The confession and its aftermath in chapter 16.** "Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him", the loudspeaker saying "drowned him" late, the sentence walking back through the square, Tastet turning the sheet over, and "'Somebody has to stack them.'"
5. **Coralie on the phone log in chapter 12.** "'Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one.'" and "'Because you'd have told me.'" This is where the reader becomes afraid for the marriage.
