# Critic report — lens: Reader

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

Verdict against the bar: **7. Good, and below the comparison.** The last six chapters are at the bar or over it. Chapters 7 to 10 are under it, and they are where an unpaid reader stops.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes — 7.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the care home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." Stated once in chapter 2 and never needs restating. The second stake is better: "*That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*"
- **Tension — 6.5.** At its best: "02:59. The phone on the seat began to ring. The screen said G. Larrère." At its slackest, three days before the flood: "'The association has no opinion on sand,' Larrère said through the window."
- **Progression — 6.** "'Four to three,' Larrère said. 'It stays shut.'" That is the third time the gate has been asked for and refused, and the situation on the eighth of December is the situation of the thirtieth of November.
- **Comedy — 7.** "She said it had been told twice and given two lots of stars, and it got through them early and came at one." I laughed out loud there, and at the oven, and at the car window. From chapter 7 to chapter 12 I did not laugh.
- **Characters — 7.** "I've held hands for a living since I was nineteen. I know when a hand is holding on, and I know when it wants to be let go." Coralie is an 8 or better. Maylis is defined by what she does not say, and for half the book I had no purchase on her.
- **The switch between dark and comic — 8.** "'Here,' said Monsieur Daguerre, to the roof of the van." The running joke goes into the flood and comes out sadder without being dropped.
- **Prose — 7.** "Lesca had gone over it every night for twelve years, and it was worn as smooth as a stone step." I never had to read a sentence twice. I had to read several scenes twice, which is a different fault and is scored under clarity. The even, short cadence does not vary for nineteen chapters.
- **The ending — 8.** "He went and got it. Then he stepped over the string in his waders and began to dig." It costs a death, a job, an office and a marriage, and nobody is let off.
- **Clarity — 6.** "In the steel room Lesca was still sitting on Inguma. It had finished counting her mother's ponies, and it was pushing up under her, trying to fetch her home." She is at that moment also driving a van in another woman's body. Sentence by sentence the book is a 9. Mechanism by mechanism it is a 5.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes, but on credit. Chapter 4 bought it; chapters 7 and 8 nearly spent it; chapter 11 bought it back.

**Pressed on a friend?** Lent, with a warning to get past the committee meetings. Not pressed. I would press chapters 11 to 16 on anyone.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch, for a reader.**

1. *His premise never needs a rules meeting.* In Dark Matter the box is one idea and by a third of the way through you can explain it to someone else. Here chapter 5 stops so that Lesca can recite three numbered points, and I still needed to go back to them in chapters 7, 9 and 14. A premise that has to be minuted is not yet in the reader's hands.
2. *His middle never asks for the same thing twice.* Every Crouch chapter leaves the hero somewhere he was not. Here the gate is refused in chapter 6, 7, 10 and 13, and Jeannot refuses to stay home in chapter 6, 9, 13 (twice) and 15.
3. *His central decision is always on the page.* Maylis's is not (fault 2).

**Level with Crouch** on fear for the marriage, and above him on what the marriage is made of: a shopping pad, two fingers on a wrist, a pillow. Nothing in Recursion is as exact as "Because you'd have told me."

**Below The Good Place on rate, level on kind.** Its people are each wrong in their own way and say so every thirty seconds. Here Larrère, Jeannot, Coralie and Madame Etcheto are that, and whenever two of them are in a scene the book is funny. The two leads are not funny in themselves; they are funny only when sharing a body.

**Level with The Good Place on the reversal.** "Maylis was about to do what Lesca had done. She would do it for the same woman, and to the same man" re-reads the whole book, and so does the kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice.

### The lens questions

**What I did not understand.**

- Why Larrère ordered the gate raised on the Monday with the river low. It cracks the rib and starts the book, and no reason is given.
- Why the rain came twelve years late, and what the steel room did. The book's answer is "I don't know" / "I don't know either."
- How Lesca can be in the van and in the steel room at once in chapter 14. Until then, the one who rides has left the chair.
- Whether Madame Etcheto sat down in the place laid for Lesca — whether a death was owed and she paid it — or whether "Twelve. One, two." is only news. I could read it either way and I do not think I was meant to.
- Whether the gate would have opened at all. The winch stops at nineteen for Jeannot, and Maylis's side has just given. Lesca says "two minutes would have covered them" as fact; the winch says otherwise. The blame in chapter 18 is reckoned on something the book has made unknowable.
- Why a Port that heard a prophecy read back to the word on Tuesday the eighth is asleep "with their shutters closed" on Friday.
- Small: "It takes a small m." "Monsieur le maire stands here." "The bench that thanked the State" (first and only mention, chapter 17). Whether the meadows are grass or maize in December. Teal.

**Where I was bored, lost or skimming.** Bored: the middle of chapter 7 (two forms in a row), the vote in chapter 10, the Hontaube phone call in chapter 13. Lost: chapter 8 (who has which hand, and what Maylis is getting out of the day), chapter 14 from "Seven." to "She let go." Skimmed: every river-gauge reading after the second; the Darrigrand doorstep in chapter 4.

**Where I could not stop.** Chapter 4 from the wardrobe to "Who is that?". Chapter 6 entire. The landing in chapter 9. Chapter 11 to the end of chapter 12. Chapter 13 from the platform to the end of chapter 16, with one stumble at 14.

**What I will remember in a month.** A thing made of leaves and rivets that sits on you with its back turned. A woman who has only her right hand left and uses it to tip the soup out of the spoon. "Either." A mayor who speaks through a gap in a car window, and the window going all the way down. A boy counting his fingers in a flood and getting five. "Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him", said again by a loudspeaker a moment late. I will not remember Hontaube, the association, the undated letter, the complaint, the lake or the ponies.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The middle asks for the same thing four times and is refused four times. (Chapters 7–10.)**
"'Four to three,' Larrère said. 'It stays shut.'"
On Monday the thirtieth (chapter 6) the reader knows the date, the breach, the man and the obstacle. On Tuesday the eighth (chapter 10) all four are exactly where they were. In between: Larrère refuses (6), Larrère refuses with a letter (7), the prefect is written to (9), the café votes (10). The rides alternate — hers, mine, hers — and each ends with Inguma fetching and a reset. This is where a Crouch reader puts the book down; I nearly did at the end of 8. *Do:* keep two refusals, the first (6) and the public one (10). Cut the Sarthou complaint and the nets blackmail from 7 outright — Jeannot votes "Shut" for his blind without them — and let 7 be the resignation letter and the decision to ride. One of chapters 7–9 must end with something that cannot be undone before Friday; at present only Coralie's move to the spare room qualifies, and it is told in four lines.

**2. Maylis's one real decision is taken offstage. (Between 13 and 14.)**
"Maylis wanted the gate. She meant to raise it now, with the man still in his cellar at the end of the track, because Coralie was under that bank."
For thirteen chapters she says Either. Then, between the last line of 13 and the first page of 14, she decides to drown Jeannot Castagnet, and I learn it from Lesca's point of view in a subordinate clause. Chapter 13 ends with her naming ponies to stay awake. The reversal in 14 — each woman now wants what the other did — is the best idea in the book and it arrives without its first half. *Do:* put the decision in the van between 22:41 and 02:49, in her own head, with Coralie's back door in view. One paragraph. Then 14 can show Lesca recognising it instead of announcing it.

**3. The climax is the hardest scene in the book to follow. (Chapter 14.)**
"In the steel room Lesca was still sitting on Inguma. It had finished counting her mother's ponies, and it was pushing up under her, trying to fetch her home. She felt it lift, and she held it down."
Two bodies, two rooms, one steering wheel, a count of rings, and a rule (you can be in the chair and in the van at once) that has not been used before. I read the page three times. The choice that matters — Lesca lets go — is clear only after I have solved the staging. *Do:* never cut back to the steel room. Let her feel the lift under her in the driver's seat; "she stood up off Inguma's back" can be one sentence at the moment she leaves. And have chapter 12's "I'm going to sit on it" say what sitting on it buys her: both places at once, for as long as she can hold.

**4. At five on Friday, with the man on the bank and her hand on the handle, she stops for a sheet of paper. (Chapter 13.)**
"'Either,' Maylis said." Against: "That's prison, for eels."
This is the moment the whole book has been built to — daylight, falling tide, Jeannot above ground, the handle fitted — and what stops her is a threat to a boy who is not there, about a crime the book itself calls small. I did not feel torn. I felt she was being held back so that everyone could be in position by three. Madame Darrigrand says as much in 18, which tells me the book knows. *Do:* move Kévin's arrival for his nets to before the platform scene, so that he is standing on the bank when she fits the handle and the cost has a face and a voice. The dialogue of both scenes survives the swap.

**5. Forty, forty-one, forty-two — and five surnames beginning with L. (Throughout.)**
"'Forty-one,' Coralie said." Against: "Forty-one times I've paid for a registered letter."
Forty houses, forty-two residents, forty-one letters becoming forty-two, forty euros a month, forty-one survivors. The last line of chapter 15 should hit clean; I spent it checking it was not the letters. The names do the same: Lesca, Larrère, Labat, Lafitte, Lassalle; Darrigrand, Daguerre, Dufau, Ducasse; Castagnet, Castéra; Maylis, Maïté, Marie-Jo. In a short book with some twenty-five named people I mixed up Lafitte and Labat until chapter 10. *Do:* move the letters to another decade (twenty-six and twenty-seven) and rename Lafitte, Ducasse and Castéra off their initials. It costs an afternoon and nothing else.

**6. Maylis's day in the mayor's chair is given a third of the room of Lesca's day in hers. (Chapter 8.)**
"At two Maylis stood Lesca at the window until the church clock had finished striking. Nothing came for her. She had worked the job out wrong."
Chapter 6 is the funniest in the book because we are inside the woman who has lost her mouth. Chapter 8 is the mirror, told from the other side, in summary, and Maylis — who has wanted the sash since chapter 3 — is seen wanting only once: "'I want that,' Maylis said." She later asks to be paid in "a table of people who won't sit down until I do", and I never saw what that was like for her. Also, this Coralie offers her pillow to "a woman at this table who sat down when she was told", which chapter 12 makes untrue: the landing is about a phone. *Do:* cut the Kévin paragraph and the two o'clock paragraph, drop the pillow offer, and play the council standing until she sits as a full scene.

**7. Too many documents. (7, 10, 13, 16, 17.)**
"'Is this a request, or a notification?' the woman said. 'I've got a tray for each, and a book.'"
By my count: the shopping pad, the card, letter 41, the undated resignation, the blank sheet, the complaint, the café pad, the motion, the letter to the prefect, the petition, the handover note, the speech, the list on its cord, the Hontaube form, the summons, the visitors' book, the police form. Four of them are the book — pad, card, list, third sheet. The rest blur into one gag about a blank that needs filling, and by "The form needs a third box" in 17 I had heard it. The Hontaube call is a page of it, with a new voice, on the afternoon of the flood. *Do:* Hontaube in four lines. Cut the complaint (fault 1). Cut "You've left out the place" and the visitors' book entries in 16.

**8. Lesca prices her own life at nothing before I have seen it. (Chapter 10.)**
"Staying costs me my life in my own village. I've worked out what that's worth, and I've written it down." … "'Nil.'"
At this point I have had two chapters in her village, and in them she has a wife who leaves the light on, a mother who feeds her, and a council that obeys her. The line is asserted. Chapter 12 would justify it and comes two chapters late. *Do:* give her one concrete thing in chapter 4 or 8 that she cannot stand another year of — the kiss at the stone is the obvious one, and at present it is only reported — or move "Nil" to after chapter 12.

**9. Chapter 18 goes back in time to do the accounts. (Chapter 18, first section.)**
"That was two minutes. You were two hundred metres short of the bank when it broke, and two minutes would have covered them. Those two minutes are mine."
Chapter 17 ends on a Tuesday with the pillow on the bed. Chapter 18 opens the previous Sunday at three in the morning, and the two women divide the blame by the minute. It reads as the book making sure I have the sums right, and the sums do not hold (the winch). The same scene raises the rain again only to say nobody knows; chapter 5 already said that better with "Nobody was to blame for a dry one." *Do:* keep "Don't sign my sum for me. I've hidden behind it for twelve years", the exchange of advice about the two Coralies, "Drowned", and Inguma going out by the door. Cut the arithmetic and the rain. Place what is left where it happens, between the landing and the lieutenant in 17, so that 18 is Maylis's Sunday and nothing else.

**10. The steel room is a promise the book does not keep. (Chapter 1; never again.)**
"'One of these two lamps will come on,' Dr Kessler had told her at the door."
A university, a named scientist, a sealed card. A reader who has come from Dark Matter waits for Kessler, or the university, or one sentence about what the room was for. They are never mentioned after chapter 4. The room survives as furniture and as a café joke. I can live without the explanation; I cannot stop expecting it while a name is attached. *Do:* take the name off. "The woman from the university" promises less, and then nothing is owed.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The right hand.** All she keeps, and what she does with it: tips the spoon ("It won't keep still"), clamps her own mouth, pulls out from under two fingers, crosses the t, low. It is the premise made visible and it is funny and frightening at once.
2. **Chapter 12, the landing.** "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." and "Because you'd have told me." and the wardrobe with nothing on it.
3. **The speech in chapter 16**, exactly as it runs: the six-word sentence not read, "and I drowned him", the loudspeaker on the bank road a moment late, the sentence travelling back through the square at walking pace, the man who claps.
4. **Kévin's fingers.** Planted in a tractor bucket in 3; "Five" in the yard in 15; "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much" in 18.
5. **Madame Etcheto, whole.** "Staff to wait. Staff not to join in." The rest of the prayer said across a bingo hall over a duck. Nineteen sandbags counted aloud. Room twelve, the buttoned coat on the chair, the pencil line through the last name.
