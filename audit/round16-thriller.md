# Round 16 — Thriller

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes — 7.** The water is worth an 8: "Behind it were forty houses, and the care home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." The human lever set against it is worth a 5: "That's prison, for eels."
- **Tension — 7.5.** "At nine minutes to three something heavy landed on the roof of the van. The metal bent in over her head and sprang back." Chapters 13 to 15 are at the bar. Chapters 7 to 9 are not.
- **Progression — 6.** "At two Maylis stood Lesca at the window until the church clock had finished striking. Nothing came for her. She had worked the job out wrong." The only rule that can kill before the flood goes live for five hours and gets three sentences.
- **Comedy — 7.5.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station."
- **Characters — 8.** "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them."
- **The switch between dark and comic — 7.5.** "'It's Saturday.' 'Then they'll want the bins out,' he said." In the flood it is clean. On flood morning the Hontaube form call is not.
- **Prose — 7.5.** "Then the street lamps of the Port went out. They went out one after another, starting at the bank end."
- **The ending — 8.** "He went and got it. Then he stepped over the string in his waders and began to dig."
- **Clarity — 6.** "Lesca had the legs, the left hand and the mouth. Maylis had her right hand." By chapter 9 the reader is tracking who sits, who stands, who wakes first, whose alarm, what was given to count and for how long.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. From the end of 10 I could not have stopped. The risk was 7 and 8.

**Press it on a friend?** Yes, for 13 to 15, and I would tell them the middle is three short chapters.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch, as a thriller, taken whole. Level with him from chapter 13 to chapter 15. Above him on what the ending costs.**

- **Speed of premise: below.** Dark Matter has its man taken by the end of chapter one and in the wrong world a tenth of the way in. Here the two villages and the date are in play by the last paragraph of 2, which is fast, but nobody crosses until the end of 4 and the two women do not meet until 5, a quarter of the way through. Chapter 4 spends its first half re-proving to Lesca what chapter 3 proved to the reader.
- **Escalation: below.** Recursion doubles the size of the problem at every turn. Here the possession is the same size in 6, 8 and 9: one hand against the rest of a body, a jaw fought for, a day. The takeover threat peaks on the stairs in 6 and is never larger again; in 14 it lasts eight rings of a phone.
- **Fear for the marriage: level, by a different route.** Crouch lets the impostor into the bed and makes the reader watch. This book stops her on the first stair and then has the wife do the detecting: "That's her. In this hand." Coralie works out in a week what takes Daniela most of a novel. The fear is shorter and the wife is better.
- **The reversal that re-reads: below The Good Place, with one exception.** Chapter 11 confirms what the opening showed. The exception is Coralie in 12, "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one", which re-reads the landing, the pillow and every "Madame le maire" before it. That is the book's real turn and it is as good as the comparison.
- **Cost: above.** A woman is dead who was funny two hundred lines earlier, the wife leaves, the job goes, and the man she saved says he was in no danger. Crouch does not charge that much.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. Chapter 13 — the decision that loses the Port is buried in the middle of the longest chapter and has nothing heavy enough on the other side of the scale.**
"Her weight was on the handle. She could have turned it." Then a paragraph of recap about Kévin's cap, then "'Either,' Maylis said."
This is the one moment the book can be won: daylight, falling tide, Jeannot above ground an arm's length away. Chapter 18 says so in plain words: "At five on Friday it was light... You had the key on your belt then." Yet it is the third of seven sections, the chapter ends on a different beat, and the reason she lets go is six months for eels against forty houses she knows to the minute will flood. The same folder has already been held up in 7 and 10 with the same two sheets in it; by the third showing it is furniture. A Crouch reader does not believe the refusal, so the death that follows from it reads as arranged.
Do: split 13 at "She was scared." and end the new chapter there, with the reflector going under the ground. Rebuild the platform scene so the refusal is physical. She turns eighteen: "At eighteen clicks the bottom of the gate was a hand's width above its sill" is already established in 6. The nineteenth needs the shoulder, and that is the turn she does not take, with Jeannot asking and the car a hundred metres off. Then she lets it down a click at a time. A gate open a hand and shut again is "Either" made of iron, and it rhymes with Jeannot's "the gate is up by the width of a hand and no more" in 15. Cut the Kévin recap to one image and show the folder twice in the book, not three times.

**2. Chapter 15 — the central choice is stated as a clean binary, taken in five words, and then called something else.**
"On foot she could reach the winch or the man. She could not reach both. If she went back, she would turn the handle on him, as Lesca had. If she went on, the river would take the Port." Then: "She got out and went on."
Five minutes earlier she woke "meaning to raise the gate... because Coralie was under that bank." Now she walks away from the gate with Coralie under it and the book gives no reason. Four minutes later she tells Jeannot "Come up. I'm opening the gate", so she has not chosen the man at all; she is still trying for both. And 18 confirms it: "You said both... That was Either, with the river in it." The narration has told the reader she knows she cannot have both and the plot needs her to believe she can. The reader cannot tell whether they watched a choice or a refusal to choose, at the one place the book exists for.
Do: decide, and make the sentence at the stuck van say it. If it is "both", the arithmetic at 02:59 must be hers and wrong (four hundred there, a thousand back, the bank has held before) and the narrator must not correct her. If it is the man, give her the reason in one line and rewrite Coralie's charge in 18.

**3. Chapters 7, 8 and 9 — the middle is told at outline speed and throws away its one mortal beat.**
"For two nights Maylis pushed up at Inguma with all the breath she had, and stayed out of the chair. On the Friday night the cracked rib had no push left in it."
Three changes of driver in three chapters, two of them half the length of every other chapter. In 8 the rule "if it cannot fetch her, she is found dead in her own bed" comes due at two o'clock, and the five overdue hours are passed over. Maylis's one day in the other village yields four facts in summary. This is where a Crouch reader puts the book down: the river is rising a line a day and the chapters read like minutes of it.
Do: write 8 from two o'clock to seven as scene, on the clock, with Lesca knowing the rule and wanting it both ways (if the other one is never fetched, what does Lesca keep?). Give 9's two nights of pushing one night on the page.

**4. Prologue, 2, 8, 10, 11 — the mystery is answered on the first page and then asked four times.**
Prologue: "Then she took out her phone. The number was already in it." Chapter 2: "'Did you know he was out?'" Chapter 8: "When you stood on that platform, did you—" and the timer rings. Chapter 10: "That's not a no."
By the end of 2 the reader has the turned van, the phone and the one man on the meadows. Nine chapters later the reveal delivers what they have been holding. Meanwhile the reader sits inside Lesca's head in 2, 4 and 8 and she never once thinks of it, which is felt as a cheat. The timer cuts off the key word twice (Maïté's verb in 2, Larrère's question in 8); the second time the device shows. What 11 gives that is new is the reflector, the four minutes, and 02:51, and those land.
Do: stop asking whether she knew. Ask the question the book cannot answer and already treats as the real one in 12 and 17: who it was for. Put Coralie's car in the yard in the prologue, unexplained, beside "One light was on in its stairwell." The reader then carries the car for eleven chapters. Keep one timer interruption.

**5. Chapter 14 — the usurper the book has promised since chapter 4 gives up in a paragraph of accountancy.**
"She went through the list, the way the cost of a motion is read out before a vote. None of those things would drive a van." Then: "She let go."
Since "and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it" the reader has been told this woman will die to stay ("Nil"; "She didn't mean to come back"). The payment is one gear lever and one horn. And the ledger in 18 does not add up: "You were two hundred metres short of the bank when it broke, and two minutes would have covered them." Two minutes puts Maylis at the winch as the bank goes, with nineteen turns still to make.
Do: give the fight in the van one more exchange that Lesca wins, so letting go is giving up something she holds. Fix the sum so the two minutes are truly the margin, or have Maylis refuse the figure.

**6. Chapter 17 — Lesca's ending costs her what she had already written off and pays her what she wanted.**
"That night Lesca was in the bedroom when Coralie carried her pillow back across the landing and laid it on the wall side of the bed."
She priced her life in that village at nil and meant to leave it dead. She loses the sash and the medal and, three days after confessing to a drowning, gets the pillow. The book is far harder on Maylis, who did less. The lieutenant and the council vote are also two procedural scenes in summary between the reader and Maylis's morning, which is what they want after "Forty-one."
Do: end 17 on "She left her door open and the lamp on." Leave the pillow unshown and the police file open. Cut the council scene to its vote.

**7. Chapter 5 — the mechanism arrives as a three-point manual and then keeps growing.**
"Two. One of us always wakes first, and she wakes at home, in her own bed. If the other one is still sitting in this chair at that moment, she is carried along. She wakes up in the first one's bed, inside the first one's body."
The joke of the meeting does not hide that it is a rule sheet at the quarter mark, and it is amended in 7 (no alarm), 9 (say the words again), 12 and 14 (sit on it). Crouch's box has one rule and one cost.
Do: the crossing in 4 has already shown point two. Keep Maylis's line "So the one who stays sitting rides the one who wakes" and cut Lesca's version of it. State each later amendment at the moment it is used, in one sentence, by the woman using it.

**8. Chapter 15 — she loses the race by forgetting what she was told at dusk.**
"Jeannot's van had gone down it at dusk, and if his van could do it, hers could."
Nine hours earlier: "The track's soft at the dip... You'd sit there till Easter." She has waded it twice. The heroine must be beaten by the arithmetic, not by a lapse.
Do: make it a gamble she takes with her eyes open. Eight minutes by van, twenty-five on foot, ten in the bank; she knows the dip and tries the edge of it. Two sentences.

**9. Chapter 4 — the first half replays chapter 3.**
"*left* The t was crossed low." The reader saw the two cards at the end of 3. Lesca spends nine hundred words catching up.
Do: open 4 on the gauge sheet, "*26 November 2014. Under the line. Rain.*", and reach the crossing a page sooner.

**10. Chapters 5 and 18 — the clock that drives the book is a coincidence, and the book says so twice.**
"'Why did it rain? There, that year, and not here. And now here.' 'I don't know.'"
Chapter 5 lays out a cause and walks away from it: "I cracked the rib on Monday. It sat on me that night. The rain started on Tuesday."
Do: take the cause the dates already give, or cut the question from 18. Asking it at the end to shrug is the worst of the three.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. The last page of 13, from "02:49. She was awake." to "In her hand, the phone began to ring."
2. Kévin's hand in the headlights: "'Five,' he said. He said it like a man who turns over a card and finds it isn't the one he hoped for." And its answer in 18: "I wanted six, Maylis."
3. Room twelve to the hall: the coat "buttoned, and wet up to the collar", the pencil through the last name, the hatbox on the wardrobe, "Forty-one."
4. Coralie reading the call log in 12, and "Because you'd have told me."
5. Nine rings against one: Jeannot's numbered answer at five in the morning, and the same man stepping over the string with her father's shovel.
