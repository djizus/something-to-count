# Critic report: Characters

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "One woman could turn the winch, or she could reach this hatch. She couldn't do both." The man and the houses are both people by then. Maylis's own stake, her body, is stated once (ch. 10) and never felt.
- **Tension: 7.** "On foot she could reach the winch or the man. She could not reach both." Chapters 11 and 13 to 15 hold. Chapters 5 to 9 repeat one situation three times.
- **Progression: 6.** "Lesca had the legs, the left hand and the mouth. Maylis had her right hand." That is chapter 14, and it is the fourth time the book lists who has which limb (ch. 5, 6, 8, 14).
- **Comedy: 7.5.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" The jokes come out of what each person is.
- **Characters: 7.** "'It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you.'" The villagers are at 8 or above. The two women and the first Coralie are held at 7 by the faults below.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Then they'll want the bins out,' he said." Said from a wheelchair in a flooded yard, and it makes the yard worse.
- **Prose: 6.** "She was frightened, and she wanted the form gone." The narrator names the feeling after the scene has shown it, and uses the same sentence for both women.
- **The ending: 7.** "'That was Either, with the river in it.'" The cost is real: a woman dead, a job gone, a pillow in a car. The verdicts on Maylis and on Coralie are aimed at the wrong moments (faults 1 and 2).
- **Clarity: 6.5.** "For Lesca it was the night after the speech. Her Monday in Bayonne and her Tuesday in front of the council had not happened yet, and the pillow was still in the spare room." A chapter that has to open by correcting its own order is in the wrong order.

**Finished unpaid:** yes. From chapter 11 I could not have stopped; through 5 to 9 I kept going for Jeannot and Coralie.
**Press it on a friend:** no, not as it stands. I would tell a friend about the forty-two letters. I would not hand over the book until Maylis is tried for the right hour.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Above Crouch on people, below Crouch on fear for the marriage. Level with The Good Place among the villagers, below it for the two leads. Overall for this lens: 7, one point under the bar.**

- **Above Crouch: the wife is a person and the cast is a village.** Daniela in *Dark Matter* is what Jason wants. Coralie has a job she does on the page, a form she posted "for me", and she catches the impostor by the wrist in the first twenty minutes ("'Who is that?'"). Crouch has no one like Jeannot, who goes down to his blind "because you've said it".
- **Below Crouch: the marriage is not at ease long enough to be feared for.** *Dark Matter* gives a whole evening of the family before it takes the husband, then lets the wrong man into the bed for chapters. Here the couple are happy twice (the oven door, the corridor outside room twelve), both by chapter 3, and the usurper never gets past the fourth stair. Coralie's quickness is good character and it removes the dread.
- **Level with The Good Place: everyone is wrong in a way of their own, and the reversal re-reads what came before.** Lesca counts ("'Your counting comes out right for you every time'"). Larrère never signs. Jeannot does what he is told not to. The second village is wrong as a body: for twelve years nobody asked. Coralie on why she never asked, "'Because you'd have told me'", re-reads the landing: she left the bed to avoid an answer.
- **Below The Good Place: the leads are not told their own fault to their faces.** Eleanor and Chidi are told plainly, by each other, and change in the telling. Maylis is Chidi: her failing is that she will not choose. The book's one room where nobody can lie tries her for a different thing, and the first Coralie is never tried at all.

**The lens questions, answered.**
- *Distinct?* Yes in speech. Lesca talks in agenda items; Maylis measures. In narration they are given the same interior sentence (fault 10).
- *Wanted-for?* Lesca yes, from "*and Coralie Mounet in it*". Maylis less: the reader wants her to ask, and is not shown what she has to lose besides Coralie (faults 4, 5).
- *Softened or excused?* Both, at the end. Maylis by Lesca in chapter 18. The first Coralie by the book.
- *Described from outside as a woman?* No. Neither has a body beyond a rib and a scar, and it does not matter.
- *Who would be missed?* Jeannot, Madame Etcheto, Kévin, the widow Maïté, Lafitte and his pad, Monsieur Daguerre. Not missed: Tastet, Madame Ducasse, the young man from Pau, the woman at Hontaube, Bixente, Nadine, Père Honoré.
- *Which relationship carries it, and does it pay?* The book says Maylis and Coralie. It pays at a discount. The one that pays in full is Maylis and Jeannot, from "Put it in forty-two" to a man stepping over a string.
- *Does the reader care who lives?* Jeannot, from chapter 1. Madame Etcheto, once it is too late, which is right. Lesca, no: she prices her own life at "Nil" and the reader takes her figure, so her chair at the table of the dead is watched with interest and without fear.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. Maylis is tried for the night and acquitted. Her fault is five o'clock, and the room where nobody can lie never mentions it. (Ch. 13, 18. Structural.)**
At five on Friday the man is on the bank beside her, it is daylight, the handle is on the winch. "She had said one word, and two men had decided for her. She knew it, and she locked the box anyway." That is the book's central choice, and it is the choice of a woman who says Either. At ten she reads "*Etcheto back in 12*" twice and thinks about being home at seven. Chapter 18 opens: "Maylis had come to be told that it was her fault. Lesca was the only one who knew enough to say so." What Maylis then confesses is "'The van in the dip was mine. So was saying both.'" What Lesca answers is "'You rang nine times. You walked his track at dusk, and you ran it in the night. A woman is dead because of that, and it still isn't what I did.'" Five o'clock is left to a woman with a pram: "'You had the key on your belt then.'" So the reader's verdict and the book's differ, and the book's is the softer.
*Do:* in the last steel room Maylis says five o'clock first, in her own words, and says why: a boy's name on a sheet. She does the sum she has refused all book, six months of Kévin against a woman of ninety-four, and cannot stand it. She says the text message. Lesca keeps her line about the one ring, but it comes after Maylis has charged herself with the right hour, and it does not clear her.

**2. The first Coralie put Madame Etcheto back in room twelve, and the book lets her prosecute. (Ch. 13, 15, 18. Structural.)**
She had the warning from 30 November ("'Get them out of their beds by two. Dressed, in the corridor'"). At ten she walked one woman back to the room under the bank. At twenty past three "'Twelve wouldn't open. The water was pushing on the door from that side, and I couldn't move it, and I had the corridor full.'" She came back to it at ten past four. In the kitchen she states this as fact and passes sentence in the same breath: "'I'm not telling you this so that you'll carry less of it. You had the key.'" Then she leaves on the charge that "both" was "Either, with the river in it". The second Coralie has a wrong and owns it. This one has a larger wrong and is given none.
*Do:* she says "I walked her back" as hers. The leaving then comes from her own share as much as from Maylis's word: she cannot stand at that door at lights-out again, Bayonne is flight as well as want, and she knows it. She still goes. "Both" is also the first time Maylis refuses two boxes; let one of them say so, so that Coralie's reading is a reading and not the book's ruling.

**3. Maylis's one day in the life she did not get is told from inside the other woman. (Ch. 8. Structural.)**
"The village's girl was enjoying herself, and Lesca could tell." Lesca's crossing (ch. 4) is the best chapter in the first half because the reader is in her when she wakes beside a Coralie who sleeps in the bed. Maylis's crossing gives the reader Lesca's one hand. Maylis wears the sash, sits while a table stands, eats soup made by a Coralie who sleeps across a landing, looks at a Kévin who has been to prison, and the reader gets "'I want that'" about a timer. Chapter 1 planted the stone as an omen: "Above her name was a man's name, and she could not read it." She crosses that square in chapter 8 and the book does not walk her round to the front of it.
*Do:* tell chapter 8 from Maylis. Stand her in front of the stone. Let her want the chair badly enough that the reader fears she would trade for it, and let her see in that kitchen what it cost. Then the hire in chapter 10 ("one day in your mayor's chair") is a temptation and not a clause.

**4. The one time Maylis asked is not in the book, and the couple are never at ease after chapter 3. (Ch. 1, 13, 18.)**
"Maylis had asked for Coralie, once, and got her." "'Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you, and that was me.'" The relationship the book rests on is founded on an event reported twice and never given: where, in what words, what Coralie said. From chapter 5 on the two meet only in dispute, and their love is carried by three objects (the pad, the wrist, the pillow). When the car door shuts "on a pillow" the reader is mourning a scene seventeen chapters back.
*Do:* give the first ask, in Coralie's mouth, at the latest in chapter 9 when she sets her terms ("'But you'll have to ask'"). Then the back door in chapter 13 is the second time in sixteen years and the reader knows what the first sounded like. Add one scene of ease between chapters 9 and 13. Pay for the room by halving the phone call to Hontaube.

**5. Maylis's village holds nobody she would lose but Coralie. Her mother arrives in the last chapter. (Ch. 18.)**
"Arlette was on the road to the Port, leading the old mare back down to the meadows on a halter." That is the first appearance of Maylis's mother. Lesca's mother has four chapters, a pasture lost to a lake, and the best exit in chapter 16. Maylis kept all of that, mother, ponies, grass, and is never seen having it; she learns her own grandmother's words from a stranger in a care home. So "'It's a whole life with one hand'" has one person in it.
*Do:* one kitchen scene with this Arlette before chapter 10. Best with Lesca driving: a mother who has never voted against her, at a table Lesca has not sat at in seven years. It gives Lesca a second thing to stay for and Maylis a second thing to lose.

**6. In the ridden chapters the two women become a vehicle and a hand. (Ch. 6, 9; rule restated ch. 5, 6, 8, 14.)**
"Lesca waited for the arm. Then Lesca got the left shoulder under the handle and pushed with the legs." "'A metre and a half under the line,' Lesca said out loud, to the right hand." It is funny for the length of chapter 6. By the third ride (ch. 9) the grammar has made Lesca a driver and Maylis a limb, and from chapter 5 to chapter 9 Maylis's speech comes to three lines. The moment that works is the one with a mind in it: Coralie laughs at a joke made with Maylis's mouth and "In sixteen years Maylis had never got that laugh out of her."
*Do:* state the rule once, in chapter 5. In 6 and 9 cut limb reports and give more of what Maylis thinks while her mouth is used.

**7. Kévin is the lever of the book's choice and his fate is dropped; in the other village the woman who jailed him never speaks to him. (Ch. 8, 16, 18.)**
"He didn't know that his name was on a sheet in Larrère's folder, or that she had kept a gate shut on Friday at five because of it. She didn't tell him." The letter is dated. Does the second sheet go to Bayonne? The book does not say, so the reader does not know whether the shut gate bought anything. Over there, "Lesca had called those six months the law. She had never had to look at them for this long", and then Marie-Jo mentions "the coat you gave him when he came out of prison" and that is all.
*Do:* Larrère, in the silt, says what becomes of the sheet. One line. Lesca, among the chairs, has one exchange with this Kévin, or tries and he takes his cap off.

**8. The two women are the only two people who had that father, and he never comes up between them. (Ch. 10, 14.)**
Every man in the village uses him on Maylis: "'Your father turned it with one hand'", "'like your father never—'", "'You've got his key and none of his sense.'" Both daughters find him at the table of the dead ("with his watch beside his card") and walk past. "He did not look up from his numbers. She did not stop." They meet five times and trade a gate, an arm, a day in a chair and advice. He is where Either comes from ("Her father had kept that key for thirty years by never telling those men what he wanted") and where a third way comes from ("'he stopped four turns short, to leave a man his water'").
*Do:* two lines between them in chapter 10, at the edge of the hall. It makes them sisters for a moment, which the book otherwise never does: "They were the same height, and neither of them liked it" is as near as it gets.

**9. "Either" is spent eight times before the gate, once with no motive. (Ch. 1, 3, 5, 6, 7, 13.)**
Chapter 1 gives the word a reason: "if Maylis ticked that box it would be Maylis's doing." It spares someone else a cost. In chapter 3, alone in bed, choosing between stars and grass: "'Either,' Maylis said." Nobody is being spared. There it is a catchphrase, and it teaches the reader to hear a catchphrase at the gate.
*Do:* cut the chapter 3 bedroom one. Keep the jaw in chapter 6, which is the best use: she fights for her own mouth and that is the only word in it.

**10. The narrator states the feeling after the scene has shown it, in one sentence shape for both women. (Throughout; worst at ch. 7, 18.)**
Maylis: "She wanted that. It surprised her how much." Lesca: "She wanted that to be true, and it frightened her how much." Jeannot leaves a glass half full for the first time in thirty years, and then: "Maylis was ashamed." The last answer of the book is glossed: "It was hers to want. Nobody had to give anything up for it." That gloss tells the reader that a want which costs nobody is the arrival. It is the logic of the shopping pad in chapter 1, unchanged.
*Do:* cut the label wherever a hand, a glass or a pillow has already said it. Cut the gloss on the grave and leave the verdict to Coralie: "'Then you'll dig it. With that side. I'll strap it first.'"

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Jeannot Castagnet, entire.** The numbered grievances, "'And six. Bugger off'", "Put it in forty-two" becoming letter No. 42, "I was in no danger, and I'll say so to anyone", and the second shovel.
2. **Lesca stating her wants on her own forms.** "*That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*" "All she found was a handle, turning." "'The form needs a third box.'" She never learns why she did it. Do not let her learn.
3. **Each Coralie preferring the other woman, and the refusal on the landing.** "'I'd have this one again.'" "'I'd keep that one in the house.'" "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'"
4. **Kévin's fingers.** Five in the yard at four o'clock with a wheelchair half up on the van, and "'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much.'"
5. **Room twelve.** The two fingers on a dead wrist for the full half minute, "There was a hatbox on it, and nothing else", "'Forty-one'", and the same woman alive in the other square asking "'Where was it?'"
