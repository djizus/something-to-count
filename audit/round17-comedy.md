# Critic report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "The hatch was twenty-five minutes away on foot. The bank had ten." The sum is clear and it is personal by chapter 10, when Coralie goes on nights. It is not personal before then.
- **Tension: 7.** "In her hand, the phone began to ring." Chapters 11, 13 and 15 hold. Chapters 7 to 10 run the same ride-and-fetch cycle four times and the grip loosens each time.
- **Progression: 6.** "Anybody could send Inguma away to count." A new rule arrives in chapter 9, another in chapter 10, another in chapter 12, each one chapter before it is needed.
- **Comedy: 7.** "'Opened what? There's a name over it. It reads that she opened him.'" Funny in one register, and that register is used by half the cast.
- **Characters: 7.** "'I'd fly. Wouldn't you? Straight up over the church. Then I'd do my mum's gutter.'" The minor people are funny in themselves. Of the two leads, one is a straight woman by the book's own account: "Coralie was the funny one, and Maylis was the one who laughed."
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" This is what the book does best.
- **Prose: 6.** "The pawl, the steel catch that held each turn" is the fourth time the pawl is defined. "Flashlight" and "dish towel" sit beside "wheelie bin", "lorry" and "mum".
- **The ending: 7.** "'It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you.'" It costs enough. It is told as two queues.
- **Clarity: 7.** "Her Monday in Bayonne and her Tuesday in front of the council had not happened yet." I was never lost, and I paid for that in repetition.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. I would have put it down once in chapter 4 and once in chapter 7 and picked it up both times.

**Press it on a friend?** I would lend it to a friend who likes village comedy. I would not press it on one who came for The Good Place.

## 2. Against the comparisons

Crouch writes no jokes, so the comic bar is The Good Place alone. Against it: **below on comedy, level on the switch.**

**Below, on range.** The Good Place has five comic voices in a room and none of them could say another's line. This book has one voice, the official who treats life as a form, and gives it to Larrère, Lesca, Sarthou, the woman at Hontaube, the young man from Pau, Madame Ducasse, Tastet and the lieutenant. "I've got a tray for each, and a book" (13), "It's a line on the sheet" (12), "It's in the inventory" (8), "That's for the file" (15) are one joke. Kévin, Madame Etcheto and Josiane are the only people who are funny some other way, and the book is at its funniest when one of them is talking.

**Below, on the double act.** The Good Place puts its people in a room and keeps them there until they have been wrong at each other. This book's high concept is one woman meeting herself in a room where neither can lie. They stand in that room for three scenes of any length (5, 10, 18), and only the first is played for comedy. The trolley-problem episode makes its dilemma funny by making Chidi live through it again and again with blood on him. Here the dilemma itself is never funny; the paperwork around it is.

**Level, on running gags that turn.** The Good Place rarely lets a joke die on screen. This book kills four on purpose and each death lands: the oven impression that Coralie cannot finish (18), "I'll be brief" followed by nothing (17), "still in the box" replaced by "had the key and gone for a walk" (18), Either becoming "Both" on the phone with the water at the door (15). That is as good as the comparison.

**Level, on the reversal.** The dry cellar is the book's one comic reversal that re-reads what came before: she chose the man, and the man was never in danger. "I was in no danger, and I'll say so to anyone." The other reversals (the van in the headlights, Coralie and the phone, the kitchen clock) are good and arrive without a laugh.

One more difference. In The Good Place nobody is exempt from being ridiculous. Coralie, in both villages, is the funny one and the one who is right, and nobody ever laughs at her.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Chapter 8. The reversal day is a sketch.** Chapter 6 puts the mayor in the verge-cutter's body and gets four stations out of it: kitchen, winch, car window, café, then soup and the stairs. Chapter 8 puts the woman who says Either in the mayor's chair, which is the half of the swap the reader has been waiting for, and gives it half the length and one line:

> 'I want that,' Maylis said.
> 'The timer?' said Madame Ducasse, the town hall secretary. 'You have it, Madame le maire. It's in the inventory.'

Then she does not use it. The eleven unbelled minutes are the best thing in the chapter and they are narrated in a paragraph. The day from six to half past six in the evening is three short scenes, two of them dark. Chapter 10 then promises the day again as a wage ("I want the timer, and a table of people who won't sit down until I do") and chapter 13 crosses it out. The promise is made twice and paid once, thinly.

What I would do: build chapter 8 to the shape of chapter 6. Give Maylis a vote she has to call, with Lesca's one hand doing to her what her own right hand did to the soup spoon. Let Lesca's village meet a mayor who sits down when told and like her better, as Coralie does, so that Lesca has to watch the whole table prefer the other one. Play Larrère's eleven minutes in dialogue.

**2. Chapters 5, 7, 10, 18. The two women are rationed, and the room where they cannot lie is never used for a laugh.** Chapter 5 is the funniest meeting in the book:

> 'Then it's carried, or it's a tie, and in a tie the chair decides.'
> 'There's nobody in the chair.'

After that the form is dropped. Chapter 7's meeting is twelve lines. The no-lying rule is used three times, each time solemnly: "'I'd have gone up.' Neither of them could lie, standing in that room." Two versions of one person who cannot lie to each other is a comic machine, and the book runs it once. The last meeting, in 18, is two speeches of accounting with no chair, no item and no vote.

What I would do: give the chapter 7 meeting its own business (Maylis moves that Monday be struck from the record; Lesca rules it out of order). In 18 keep the accounting and close the meeting in the form it was opened in. One procedural line at the end is enough.

**3. Chapters 16 and 18. Two receiving lines.** After the speech the villagers come up one at a time, deliver a verdict and leave: the young man, the lieutenant, Marie-Jo, Tastet, Arlette, Darrigrand, the young man again, Madame Etcheto. Chapter 18 does it again: Larrère, Sarthou, Kévin, Arlette, Madame Darrigrand, Maïté, the café, Marie-Jo, Coralie, the priest, Jeannot. Each is one beat and no scene builds on the one before. They also speak alike:

> 'I wanted to say that to your face, with nobody writing it down.' (16)
> 'I'm saying it to your face.' (18)
> 'I'm not asking you why. Not once.' (16)
> 'I'm not asking where you were. I've been told.' (18)

Chapter 18 also starts two days before chapter 17 ends and needs a sentence to say so.

What I would do: cut Darrigrand from 16 and Arlette from 18. Put Tastet's summons and Marie-Jo's chairs in one exchange so that they collide. Move the last meeting of the two women to the end of 16 or the head of 17, so that 18 does not rewind and belongs to Maylis alone.

**4. Chapters 3 and 4, and the head of 6, 8, 9 and 14. Things told twice.** A farce needs speed. Each ride opens by restating the rules:

> They shared the body the way they had on the other days. Lesca had the legs, the left hand and the mouth. Maylis had her right hand. (14)

The same inventory is at the top of 6 and 8. Chapter 4 replays chapter 3's four days from the other bed, including the envelope, the card and "Come back across", and has one laugh in it (the motion). The 2014 night is told in 00, summarised in 2, told in full in 11 and echoed line for line in 13 and 15.

What I would do: cut the inventory from 8 and 14 outright; by then a reader has it. In 4, cut the opening of the envelope to three lines and get to the chair. Keep the echo in 15 and cut the one in 13.

**5. Chapter 6. The adultery farce is set up and dropped.** Josiane starts the best misunderstanding in the book:

> 'A woman takes flowers to the wife because she's after the husband or because she's after the wife. It's adultery either way, and that one lives with a nurse.'

A farce would send that rumour round the village until it reached the one person it should not. Here it reaches nobody. The nurse never hears it. Maïté never hears it. Larrère uses it once as a threat in 13. Meanwhile the real affair is going up the back lane, and the village suspecting the wrong one is never sprung as a joke.

What I would do: have Coralie bring it home at the soup in 6. She already brings the three minutes ("By ten the care home knew"). She is the funny one; give her the line about which Castagnet.

**6. Throughout. The narrator names the feeling after the punch.** The chapter 7 café scene ends on its best line and then steps on it:

> In thirty years at that counter Jeannot Castagnet had never left a glass unfinished.
>
> Maylis was ashamed.

The same in 6: "Lesca had got it in ten minutes, with Maylis's mouth. / It hurt to hear." In 7: "It was the kitchen that scared him." In 8: "The village's girl was enjoying herself, and Lesca could tell." "She was scared" stands as a paragraph by itself in 00, 13 and 15.

What I would do: cut every one-sentence paragraph that names an emotion the scene has just shown. Keep "She was scared" in 00 only.

**7. Throughout. Too many clerks, and three people sharing one retort.** See section 2 for the clerks. The retort:

> 'Don't give me the minutes. Go.' (15, Coralie)
> 'Don't give me him for that.' (17, Lesca)
> 'Don't give me the measurement.' (18, Coralie)
> 'Don't give me your sum to hide behind.' (18, Lesca)

What I would do: let Larrère and Sarthou own the officialese. Make the woman at Hontaube wrong in a different way (she is the funniest of the clerks; she could simply be unhelpful and warm). Give "Don't give me" to Coralie alone, twice.

**8. Chapters 5 and 7. Either is overdrawn before it is needed.** It is said or written in 1, 3 (twice), 5 (twice), 6, 7 and 13. The one in 13 is the hinge of the book and must hit a reader who is not tired of the word. This one does not parse as an answer:

> 'That's what I heard. I said it over to myself on the stairs, so I wouldn't lose it. Was that you?'
> 'Either.'

What I would do: cut that one and the one to Sarthou in 7, and keep Sarthou's reply by having her simply not name him ("I cannot summon Nobody to the station"). That leaves five before the gate.

**9. Chapter 18. Kévin explains "Five."** In 15 he counts his fingers in the floodwater and gets five, and the narrator already glosses it. In 18 he says it out:

> 'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much.'

The beat in 15 is the best dark joke in the book and it needs no help.

What I would do: cut both of those sentences from 18 and the two sentences after "'Five,' he said" in 15 ("Five meant he was awake. He said it like a man who turns over a card..."). Keep "'It was five,' she said."

**10. Chapter 15. Jeannot's list stalls the flood.** The numbered speech is funny in 6, funnier in 10 and funniest in 11. The fourth one is delivered in a doorway with the water rising and seven people waiting:

> 'It's stuck,' he said. 'Your winch. I've got three things to say about it. One. I counted the clicks, the way you do...'

It runs to a hundred and thirty words. Here the joke weakens the dark. His voice also carries the narrator's gloss in 18: "the hatch of my duck-hunting blind". He would say "my blind".

What I would do: in 15, keep "It's stuck. A hand. I put it in number six" and cut the numbering. Take "duck-hunting" out of his mouth everywhere.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie and the oven.** "'In my tray,' she said, and shut the door on him" in 1, and the same routine failing after one word in 18.
2. **Madame Etcheto's care plan and her duck.** "Staff to wait. Staff not to join in." And the rest of the prayer said across the hall "over my duck".
3. **The verb for the stone.** "It reads that she opened him" in 2, the timer ringing on Maïté's mouth, *left* in 17, "Drowned" in 18.
4. **Larrère's window.** The gap, the glass going "all the way down, into the door", the eleven minutes without a bell, "I'll be brief" and then nothing, and the last walk with "no car around him".
5. **The end of chapter 11.** Nine rings, "Who's dead?", six numbered points and "Bugger off." The cleanest switch from dark to comic in the book.

## 5. Chapter by chapter

- **00.** No comedy and none wanted; "It rang once." **8**

- **6.** The best comic chapter: the shed with a lawnmower, the small m, the spoon. The stairs turn it dark without a seam. **9**
- **7.** Two blackmails in summary and one laugh. The dark smothers here. **7**
- **8.** The day I was waiting for, at half length. **6**
- **9.** Bingo and the hand over the mouth. The landing is dark and right. **7**
- **10.** The association chaired through a café door is very good. The bingo hall of the dead arrives late and is explained in a speech. **8**
- **11.** The night told straight, then the phone call. **9**
- **12.** Slow start, "Monsieur le maire", then Coralie and the call log. Not a comic chapter and does not need to be. **7**
- **13.** Hontaube on the phone is funny. Either at the gate is the word turned into the wound. Long. **9**
- **14.** Sitting on Inguma is a sight gag that is also the climax. "Apologies received" is the right joke; it also lets her out of the afterlife on a quip. **8**
- **15.** "Here." "Then they'll want the bins out." "Five." Small jokes that do not weaken room twelve. **8**
- **16.** The speech holds; the man who claps and Tastet turning the sheet over are exact. Then a queue. **6**
- **17.** *Left*, the silent three minutes, the form with two boxes. Three good scenes in a row with no rise between them. **6**
- **18.** The oven, the retired joke, letter forty-two. A second queue, and it starts before 17 ends. Closes well on the second shovel. **7** for the close.
