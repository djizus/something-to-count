# Critic report, round 17: Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else opened. Counts below are mine, taken from the chapters.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.5.** "Then his death is mine as well, and I've stood beside his widow for eleven Decembers." (12) The stake is a marriage as much as a village, and the book knows it.
- **Tension: 7.** "02:59. The phone on the seat began to ring." (15) holds. "So her plan was simple." (13) lets it out. The narrator keeps handing the reader the plan before the scene runs.
- **Progression: 6.** "It was the same as on Saturday." (6, second paragraph) The narrator admits it. Chapters 6 to 10 repeat one manoeuvre, who rides whom, five times.
- **Comedy: 7.5.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (7)
- **Characters: 7.5.** "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'" (9) Coralie is the best thing in the book.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—' She stayed bent over for a moment, with her hand on the door." (18) The joke from chapter 1 fails in her mouth and the scene turns on it.
- **Prose: 6.5.** "The pawl, the steel catch that held each turn, dropped into its first tooth with a click." (13) That is the fourth time the pawl is defined, and it is in the climax.
- **The ending: 7.5.** "Then he stepped over the string in his waders and began to dig." (18) It costs a death and a marriage. The narrator explains the last choice two lines after Maylis makes it.
- **Clarity: 7.5.** "At ten she pulled the blanket off the bed and shook it out over the floor." (1) The she is Coralie and the paragraph before it is about Maylis. Otherwise clear, at a price charged to the prose.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. From chapter 11 I could not have stopped.
**Press it on a friend?** Not yet. I would lend it and tell them to get to chapter 11.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below the bar on prose, by about a point. Level in chapters 13 to 16. Above both in dialogue.**

1. **A Crouch narrator never explains while the character is running.** The exposition arrives as argument, under threat, once. Here the narrator restates the dilemma in full six times between chapter 9 and three o'clock in chapter 15, and the body-sharing rule thirteen times (the phrase "stayed sitting" or "still sitting" alone). A Crouch reader holds the rule after one telling and is insulted by the fourth.
2. **The Good Place states its rules once, as a joke, and then breaks them.** Chapter 5 does exactly that ("All those in favour." "There are two of us.") and is the best exposition in the book. Then chapter 6 opens with four paragraphs of the same rules in a manual's voice. The show also never explains a punchline. This narrator does: "It was true, word for word, and it was meant to pass for Maylis." (9)
3. **Where it beats them: the spoken line.** Nobody in Crouch talks as well as Coralie, Jeannot or Madame Etcheto. The council register (minutes, timers, "through the chair", a form with two boxes) does what The Good Place does with its points system, and it does it with fewer words. The objects are exact: the reflector "screwed on crooked", the tan suitcase with no dust on it, eleven stairs.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

Faults 1, 2, 4, 6 and 7 are one habit: the narrator does not trust a reader who has already been told. The fix for all five is cutting. I count some forty passages. Removing them takes out roughly 2,000 words (my estimate) and adds none.

**1. The climax restates its dilemma until it has no pressure left. (13, 15, with 9 and 14)**
"One woman could turn the winch, or she could reach this hatch. She couldn't do both." (9) is enough. It returns in 13 ("If she turned the handle now, she would be sending the boy to prison herself... If she waited until the bank moved, she would know for certain."), again at the end of 13 ("So her plan was simple."), in 14, and twice in 15: "Lesca's choice was in front of her now, all of it." and "On foot she could reach the winch or the man. She could not reach both." The best use of the book's key word is wrapped in commentary on both sides: "She could not do the first thing. She did not want to look at the second. She had a word for that." before it, and "She had said one word, and two men had decided for her. She knew it, and she locked the box anyway." after.
*Do:* in 13, go from the handle and the name on the sheet straight to "'Either,' Maylis said." and from "Then it's shut" to the box being locked. Delete "So her plan was simple" to "It didn't help." In 15, cut the two restatements and keep the clock, the stuck van and "She got out and went on."

**2. The rules are recited after they have been dramatised. (6, 9, 10, 12, 14)**
"It was the same as on Saturday. Lesca had stayed sitting in the chair, so she had come home inside Maylis's body, and she controlled nearly all of it." (6) Four paragraphs of briefing open a chapter whose first scene shows every item in them. The fetch-or-die rule is told by Madame Etcheto (3), by Lesca as point three (5), by Lesca again at the bingo table (10), by the narrator (12) and by the narrator again (14: "the one Inguma cannot fetch belongs at that table"). "This was her plan." (14) repeats what Lesca told her mother in 12.
*Do:* chapter 5 is the only statement. Open 6 at "Coralie came down in her tunic at ten past six. Lesca spoke first." Cut every later "Lesca had stayed sitting, so Lesca came home in her."

**3. Chapter 2 announces the premise in a voice that knows more than Lesca can. (2)**
"So there were two Peyrebarthes." Then: "She was wrong. It was 2026 there as well, and that village had not had its flood yet." A woman who woke with a strange memory cannot know the year in another world. The narrator is certain on her behalf, and that certainty spends chapter 4 in advance. The card that says *left* in her own hand with "the t crossed low" is the book's proof, and the gauge file ("The river over there was following this file, one line a day.") is the proof of the date. Both arrive as confirmation of what the reader was told two chapters earlier.
*Do:* keep the forty letters and the rain heard under a dry roof as facts she cannot account for. End 2 on Maïté's "Today was the twenty-fourth of November too" and Lesca's fear, as a suspicion. Let the card and the file make the deductions where they already do.

**4. The narrator glosses the payoff lines. (18, 15, 9, 13)**
"'I want to dig the grave myself.' It was hers to want. Nobody had to give anything up for it." Then, in the cemetery: "Nobody had ordered it... She was digging because she wanted to." The line the whole book has been walking towards is explained twice. Also: "Five meant he was awake. He said it like a man who turns over a card and finds it isn't the one he hoped for." (15), which Kévin then says himself in 18. "It was weather, and nobody had sent it." (18) after two characters have said "I don't know." And a slip into Coralie's head to explain her own line: "She was thinking of the landing, the Saturday before." (18) The chapter is Maylis's.
*Do:* delete each gloss. In 15 keep "'Five,' he said." and the hand going under the woman's knees.

**5. Chapter 18 opens with the narrator repairing the calendar. (17, 18)**
"For Lesca it was the night after the speech. Her Monday in Bayonne and her Tuesday in front of the council had not happened yet, and the pillow was still in the spare room." Chapter 17 has run to Tuesday and paid off the pillow. Chapter 18 then steps back to Sunday and has to say so. "On Monday night she would lie awake at that minute... and she would find nothing on the wardrobe." is the only flash-forward in the book, and it is there for the same repair.
*Do:* move the last steel-room scene into 17, after "She left her door open and the lamp on." Bayonne, the vote and the pillow follow it. Chapter 18 then opens at "At first light on Sunday Maylis walked down into the Port" and neither patch is needed.

**6. Feelings are named in sentences of their own. (throughout)**
"Maylis was ashamed." (7) "It hurt to hear." (6) "She was afraid." (16) "She was frightened, and she wanted the form gone." (7) Seventeen such labels, and forty-six sentences in which she, Maylis or Lesca "wanted" something. In chapter 1 the stated want is the device: the reader hears what Maylis cannot say, then hears "Either". By chapter 7 the scene already shows it. Jeannot leaving half a glass is the shame.
*Do:* keep "She was scared." in 0, 13 and 15, where it is a refrain. Keep the wants in chapter 1. Elsewhere, cut the label when the next action carries it.

**7. Terms are defined again each time they appear. (1, 3, 6, 10, 11, 13, 15)**
The pawl is glossed four times (3, 6, 11, 13). "Duck-hunting blind" appears twelve times, usually with its cellar and hatch re-described: "His duck-hunting blind was out on the flood meadows, a cellar under a hatch, and an open gate would fill it with river." (10) The red line is explained in 2, 3 and 10. "The Adour was their river." (13) comes eight chapters after Lesca named it. "Madame Ducasse, the town hall secretary" is introduced in 8 and again in 12.
*Do:* define each term once, at first use. After that it is "the blind", "the pawl", "the line".

**8. Stock constructions. (throughout)**
- "In sixteen years Maylis had never..." Ten uses of "In N years", four of them in chapter 6 alone, and twenty-eight of "For N years". Each is a small drum roll before a fact.
- "the way": fifty uses. "the way a dog does", "the way you hold a door shut", "the way warmth goes out of a chair", "the way a man votes".
- The hand as a unit: fifteen uses ("a hand of water", "a hand's width", "the width of a hand", "a roof a hand above her head"). The first three are a motif. After that it is a reflex.
- Log-entry openings. Fifty-seven of 106 chapter and scene openings start on a time or a date: "At eight", "At nine", "At noon", "At seven" (6). The clock earns its place at chapter heads and in the countdown of 15. In 6 to 10 it is a timetable.
- Cadence. Chapter 1 averages 9.3 words a sentence and 71% of its sentences are twelve words or fewer. Chapter 15 is the same. Chapter 16, the best-written chapter, averages 11.3 and lets a sentence run: "She watched her sentence travel back through the square and down the road at walking pace, row by row." The short sentence works under pressure. As the default for forty thousand words it reads as a primer.
*Do:* halve each tic. Join definition chains such as "The mayor was Guy Larrère. Guy Larrère was also president of the landowners' association, and the association owned the sluice gate" (1).

**9. Pronouns fail where there are two women, and names pile up where there are two in one body. (1, 6, 9, 14)**
"That morning she had written *Either* to save Coralie trouble, and it had made more. She broke eggs into the pan." (1) The second she is Coralie. The opposite cure is worse to read: "Lesca" forty-seven times in chapter 6. "Lesca didn't answer her. Lesca pulled." "Lesca waited for the arm. Then Lesca got the left shoulder under the handle."
*Do:* name Coralie in the two chapter 1 sentences. In the shared chapters Maylis only ever has the right hand and is named when she uses it, so within a paragraph the driver can be "she" after the first naming.

**10. Usage and continuity.**
- American words in a British text: "flashlight" (25 times) and "dish towel" (7), beside lorry, windscreen, pavement, tarmac, pyjamas, wheelie bin and "bugger off". "Trucks" once (4) against "lorry" three times.
- One object, two names: the gate's "pillar" (3, 4, 16) and its "pier" (6, 10).
- "After that the file was typed, by the police." (4) Everywhere else they are gendarmes.
- "were in the Port by nine" (16). They are driving home, and Coralie "had come up from the Port" to the square in 12.
- Maylis gives Inguma eight hours of verges (7). It takes thirteen (8, 9). Nobody remarks on why.
- "It did, and I said then that I was coming to stay." (9) Lesca said that to Maylis in the steel room, not to Coralie on the stairs.
- "Four winters you've had that key" (7). She has held it since at least 2011 (3). Jeannot means four winters of nets.
- "He had said it in other winters, and he noticed nothing strange in it." (10) Larrère's head, in a Maylis chapter.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The mirrored openings of 1 and 2, and rain against frost as the sign of which village we are in.** "The roof over her was dry and quiet." does the work of a page of explanation.
2. **The confession and its aftermath in 16.** "Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him." The loudspeaker saying "drowned him" late, the man who claps because he thinks she has finished, Tastet turning the sheet over to see if there is more on the back.
3. **The bingo of the dead in 14.** "'The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies,' she said, in the voice she used for the prefecture."
4. **Coralie's lines, all of them.** The two fingers on the wrist. The three stages of one word: "That's Either again, with more words." "That's Either again, in pencil." "That was Either, with the river in it."
5. **Chapter 15 from the hatch to "Forty-one".** The bare timestamps, Daguerre's "Here", "Then they'll want the bins out", room twelve told as an inventory with "a hatbox on it, and nothing else."
