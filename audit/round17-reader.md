## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below the bar, by a little.** Level with it from chapter 11 to the last page. Below it from 7 to 10, and below it on clarity throughout.

- **Below Crouch on ease.** His reader never stops to work out which rule applies. Here there are seven states to keep apart (push, lie still, sit, stand up, stay sitting, sleep on your side, sit on it) and the outcome of every night depends on which. I could always say what had happened. I could rarely say what might.
- **Below Crouch on room.** This is half the length of one of his books, and the shortage is all in the middle. Chapter 6 gets 2,668 words for the mayor's day in the keyholder's body. Chapter 8 gets 1,513 for the reverse.
- **Level with The Good Place on people who are each wrong in their own way.** Larrère through four centimetres of window, Jeannot numbering his grievances, Marie-Jo speaking to the wall, Sarthou's file. I never confused two of them.
- **Above Crouch on the cost of the ending.** He gives the marriage back. This book gives one woman her pillow for confessing and takes the other's away for doing better.

### What I did not understand

1. **Why it rains.** The same nineteen days, twelve years late, starting the night after the rib. Chapter 5 offers a cause and withdraws it. Chapter 18 says "It was weather, and nobody had sent it. They left it there." I could not leave it there: it is the one coincidence the whole book stands on.
2. **What the lamp experiment did.** One woman sat in a box and a whole village split. Dr Kessler never comes back. The card is a keepsake.
3. **Why the counting takes as long as it does.** Maylis works out "a working day. Eight hours." It takes thirteen. Lesca's lake takes fifteen. Nobody remarks on it.
4. **What killed Madame Etcheto.** Chapter 16 says a chair was pulled in when Lesca refused hers. Chapter 18 says two minutes. Her eyes are open, as the lore says of the one who cannot be fetched. I do not know which I am meant to believe.
5. **Why Lesca lands in the hall of the dead after letting go.** She released the thing, so it should have fetched her. "Inguma had not fetched her yet" is all I get.
6. **Why a falling tide in daylight saves the bank that night.** I took it on trust every time it was said.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Chapter 8 (and the back half of 7): the return crossing is a synopsis.**
"The village's girl was enjoying herself, and Lesca could tell."
I am told she enjoys it. I do not get it. This is the reversal the book has promised since chapter 6: the woman who says Either, loose for a day in the life where she is obeyed. She never looks at the stone with her own name on it. She never sees the lake where her mother's ponies were. The eleven-minute speech is one sentence of report: "He had eleven years of it saved up, and it lasted eleven minutes." Two o'clock, the hour on her pad, goes by in three lines. Because the day is thin, the deal in chapter 10 ("one day in your mayor's chair") and its crossing-out in chapter 13 weigh nothing.
*Do:* write it at the length of chapter 6. Keep the passenger's point of view; let Lesca watch Maylis meet each thing Lesca stopped seeing years ago. Play Larrère's speech until he starts repeating himself. Make two o'clock a scare.

**2. The rules grow a clause in the chapter that needs it, and the sums do not add up.**
"I'm going to sit on it," Lesca said. "It can't come and fetch me home while I'm sitting on its back."
Nothing before chapter 12 says the thing can be sat on. The plan the climax runs on is asserted the afternoon before. The counting has the same trouble: eight hours worked out, thirteen taken, so "*Back at two*" fails for no reason I can see and Coralie's move to the spare room rests on an accident of bookkeeping. Add the two engines (a box of lamps that splits the world, a folk demon that crosses it) and the unexplained rain, and I never felt I held the rules, only that the book did.
*Do:* plant the sitting-on in chapter 3 or 4, in one line of the old woman's or Arlette's. Give the overrun a cause I can repeat. Either let the lamp do one thing after chapter 4 or cut Dr Kessler to a sentence. Raise the question of the rain once, not twice.

**3. Three chapter endings are answered off the page.**
Chapter 6 ends "Are you coming up?" Chapter 7 answers in the past tense: "It had taken her an hour to lock a house with one door." Chapter 9 ends on five dangerous mornings; chapter 10 opens with them survived. Chapter 10 ends "So who holds my hand down then?" and nobody ever answers; the plan changes between chapters.
*Do:* stage the hour of locking up, half a page. Cut the last paragraph of 9 or make Monday morning a scene. In 12, let Lesca answer her own question aloud.

**4. Chapter 18 opens by going backwards and explaining the verdict.**
"For Lesca it was the night after the speech. Her Monday in Bayonne and her Tuesday in front of the council had not happened yet, and the pillow was still in the spare room."
The book knows it is out of order and apologises. Chapter 17 has just closed Lesca's story on the pillow. Now she recounts chapter 16 to Maylis, then delivers the meaning in one block: "No. You won't sign it, and I'll take my time telling you why." I had worked it out in room twelve.
*Do:* move the last meeting to where it happens, inside chapter 17 after the landing. Cut Lesca's speech to its three true sentences: no embankment made her ring only once; Maylis rang nine times; a woman is dead and it still is not what Lesca did. Keep "What was the verb?" "Drowned." Chapter 18 then opens in the silt.

**5. Chapter 13: at the central moment Maylis doubts what the book has already proved.**
"The only reason to think it would break at three in the morning was the word of a woman in a steel room."
Eight days earlier the café read the proof aloud: "*At the line, on the day she said.*" Four pages later in the same chapter: "She knew to the minute what was going to happen." She cannot do both. The doubt is an excuse handed to her by the narration. The true reasons are on the page and are worse: the sheet with Kévin's name, and two men looking at her.
*Do:* cut the doubt. Let her believe it completely and say Either anyway.

**6. Chapter 11 tells me the night a third time.**
"It ran down the clay in a sheet as wide as a door and crossed the road in her headlights."
That sentence is in the opening. So is the hand on the clay, the ten minutes, the platform, the turned van, the phone. Chapter 2 gave it again as a speech. I have known since the first page that she rang, and since chapter 2 whom. What is new here is four things: the reflector, Coralie's car in the yard, the kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice, and 02:51.
*Do:* cut the replay to those four and the walk to them. The fourth telling, Maylis's own hand on the clay in chapter 15, is the one that pays, and it pays more if the third is shorter.

**7. Chapter 3 carries three new systems in one chapter.**
"Then it's in the minutes. And it isn't the council's winch, it's the association's, and nobody has written to the association."
Waking inside dreams, the old woman's words, then council against association against the merger with Hontaube. Five scenes over four days. This is where a reader who came for the flood stops.
*Do:* keep the inspection, because "Either" before six men comes back in chapter 10. Move the Hontaube conversation to chapter 7, where the key is taken from her.

**8. The Port sleeps through a night it has been told about.**
"Behind her, under the bank, were Sarthou and the Darrigrands' baby and old Lafitte, and the rest of the forty houses with their shutters closed."
Lafitte kept the pad. Nine people signed. Coralie was told on the thirtieth: "Get them out of their beds by two. Dressed, in the corridor." On the night, forty-two are in bed and forty houses are shuttered. I asked why, and the only answer offered is "It's orange."
*Do:* one exchange in chapter 13. Somebody in the Port says why they are staying, in their own wrong way.

**9. The book tells me what it has shown me, and then tells me again.**
"The pawl, the steel catch that held each turn."
That is the fourth time the pawl is explained, in chapter 13. "Duck-hunting blind" is spelled out twelve times. The rules are restated at the head of chapters 6, 7, 8 and 14. Feelings are labelled after the action has shown them: "She was scared." "It hurt to hear." "Maylis was ashamed." "Wanted" appears 68 times; it is the book's subject, and by the middle I stopped hearing it.
*Do:* explain each thing once. Keep the rule recap in 6 and drop the others to a sentence. Where a picture precedes a label, cut the label.

**10. The names blur.**
Larrère, Lafitte, Labat, Lesca, Lassalle. Darrigrand, Daguerre, Dufau, Ducasse. Castagnet, Castéra.
I mixed Lafitte and Labat twice, and in chapter 15 I could not place Castéra against Castagnet while one was carrying the other.
*Do:* change the first letter of Lafitte and of Castéra.
