# Round 17 — Thriller lens

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

Overall as a thriller: **7**. The bar is 8. It is one point short, and the point is lost in two places: chapters 7 to 9, and the four hours before the phone rings in chapter 13.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** "The hatch was twenty-five minutes away on foot. The bank had ten." Forty houses, forty-two beds, one man, one woman on nights. Every number is fixed early and never moves.
- **Tension: 7.** "Lesca took the body up one stair, and Maylis's arm pulled it back." At its best (the stairs, the landing, 02:53 to 03:11) it is as tight as the comparison. Chapters 7 to 9 run at half that.
- **Progression: 6.5.** "For Lesca it was the night after the speech. Her Monday in Bayonne and her Tuesday in front of the council had not happened yet, and the pillow was still in the spare room." A book that has to say that sentence in its last chapter has stepped backwards once too often.
- **Comedy: 7.5.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station."
- **Characters: 8.** "I'd have this one again."
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" The joke from chapter 1 fails in chapter 18 and nothing is said about why.
- **Prose: 7.** "The pawl, the steel catch that held each turn, dropped into its first tooth with a click." Clean and quick, and it is the fourth time the pawl has been explained, at the moment the book can least afford it.
- **The ending: 8.** "Forty-one."
- **Clarity: 8.** "So the one who stays sitting rides the one who wakes." The rules are never in doubt. The price is that they are restated at the head of almost every chapter.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. I would have read 7 and 8 fast and without much attention, and I would not have stopped.

**Pressed on a friend?** Yes, on a friend who loves The Good Place. To a friend who reads Crouch I would say: it takes until the bingo hall in chapter 10 to become the book it promised in chapter 4.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch as a thriller, by about a point. Level with him on the marriage. Above him on what the ending costs.**

- **Premise speed: level.** Dark Matter has its man abducted in chapter one and its premise named by chapter three. Here the thing is on her chest by line 9, the stone has her name on it by the end of chapter 1, and the clock is set by the last line of chapter 2: "the flood would come there on the night of the eleventh of December. Jeannot Castagnet would be out at his blind." That is Crouch's pace.
- **Fear for the marriage: level, and in chapter 6 above.** Crouch makes you afraid for Daniela by putting another Jason in her kitchen. This book puts the other woman in the same body at the same table, has her tick the box, get the laugh, and has the wife say "I'd have this one again" while the real one tips soup out of a spoon. Crouch never got the wife to prefer the impostor out loud in front of the husband.
- **The middle: below.** A Crouch middle escalates in the body: new place, worse threat, less time. Here, from chapter 7 to the top of chapter 10, the threat moves onto paper: an undated letter, a gendarme's form, a register entry, a petition. Each is funny. None of them makes me read faster. The one crossing in that stretch (chapter 8) has no danger in it.
- **The climax: split.** 02:53 to 03:11 with the clock on the phone is level with Crouch. The evacuation that follows has no one in danger on the page; the one death happened before she arrived and is found afterwards. The finding is better than anything in the comparison. The hundred lines before it are not.
- **The device: below.** Crouch pays off his box. This book builds a steel room with two lamps and a Dr Kessler, then hands the whole mechanism to a folk demon and answers its own best question with "It was weather, and nobody had sent it. They left it there."
- **The reversal that re-reads: level in kind with The Good Place, smaller in size.** "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." re-reads eleven years of a landing. The kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice re-reads him. Neither turns the premise over.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. Chapter 13 — the heroine's plan for the night is to wait, and the second pair of hands is sent away without a thought.**

> "So her plan was simple. She would stay awake, so that she was alone in her own body when the phone rang at nine minutes to three."

> "Take it down to the care home."

The book has told me twice that this night needs two people ("One woman could turn the winch, or she could reach this hatch. She couldn't do both"; "She needed more hands"). She rings Hontaube for two men and is refused. Then a strong young man climbs onto her platform at dusk and she sends him to Coralie without noticing what she has just spent. From 22:41 to 02:49 she names ponies. She knows the bank has ten minutes and the track takes twenty-five, so waiting for the phone *is* choosing the gate over the man — and the text gives that two sentences ("The man in the cellar had been warned twice. She told herself that more than once. It didn't help"). At 05:00 that morning she "meant to let it ring until he answered." By 02:51 she "meant to raise it now, with the man still in his cellar." That turn, from the woman who rang nine times to the woman about to become Lesca, is the hinge of the whole book and it happens off the page.

Do: when Kévin offers his van, let her see that he is the hands she asked Hontaube for, and choose Coralie over Jeannot knowingly — one line of her seeing it. Then show the drift. At some hour she takes the handle out of its box and lays it on the seat, as the woman in the opening did. When Lesca says "Go to the blind first" there is then a visible intention to overturn, and chapter 14's "Maylis was about to do what Lesca had done" is something I watched happen, not something I am told.

**2. Chapter 15 — the evacuation has no one in danger on the page.**

> "Coralie stood in the doorway with the list on its cord and a flashlight in her fist."

> "Twelve wouldn't open. The water was pushing on the door from that side, and I couldn't move it, and I had the corridor full."

For three chapters the stated fear is Coralie under the bank. Maylis reaches the gatepost and Coralie is standing in the light reading names. The fear is discharged in one sentence. From 03:19 to 04:10 the scene is logistics: chairs, mattresses, a minibus. The water rises from shin to knee and threatens nobody. Meanwhile the reader holds a text message — "Etcheto back in 12" — that Maylis read twice, and Maylis does not think of room twelve once in fifty minutes. Coralie reports the stuck door afterwards, as history.

Do: have twelve named at 03:19. Maylis asks; Coralie says the door would not move and she has a corridor full of people who can be reached, and goes back to the list. Now every chair Maylis pushes across that yard is a minute taken from the room at the dark end, both women know it, and the reader counts with them. The body in the bed, the hatbox and "Forty-one" lose nothing. Dread beats surprise here, because the message already told me.

**3. Chapters 7 to 9, and the first paragraph of 10 — the threat goes onto paper, the one crossing is safe, and a hook is defused between chapters.**

> "Eighteen years of cutting verges had come down to six lines and a gap."

> "It was a poor chance."

> "It did not sit on her, so she did not go to the chair, and nobody came home in her body when the alarm rang at six."

Chapter 7 is three signatures. Chapter 8 sends Maylis into the other village for a day to learn one word, "prefect", which Lesca herself calls a poor chance and which returns in chapter 13 only as a stick to beat her with. Nothing can go wrong for her there. The one thing that does — she is due back at two and Inguma does not come until seven — is skipped: "Nothing came for her," then a jump to six o'clock. Chapter 9 ends on a threat ("on any of those mornings, Lesca would come home in her") and chapter 10 opens by telling me she solved it two nights ago by sleeping on her side.

Do: in chapter 8, play two o'clock to seven from inside. The rule says the one who cannot be fetched is found dead in her bed, and her body is lying open-eyed beside Coralie with a pad that says two. Five hours of not knowing whether she is dead at home is the chapter's missing danger. And either put the side-sleeping discovery on the page as the last beat of 9, or let one of the five mornings go wrong before she finds it.

**4. Chapter 14 — the takeover the book has built since chapter 4 is settled in eight rings, by a paragraph of costing.**

> "This was her plan. While she kept Inguma down, it could not get up to fetch her home."

> "She went through the list, the way the cost of a motion is read out before a vote. None of those things would drive a van."

"That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it" is the best threat in the book. Its payoff is one stall, one horn, and five paragraphs of Lesca reasoning with herself while the phone counts. The two women do not exchange a word in the van. The plan is explained at the moment of execution, though chapter 12 already gave it to me.

Do: cut the plan paragraph. Let the hands fight longer — wheel against gear lever is the right image and it gets two sentences. Give Maylis the jaw for one word. Let Lesca's letting-go come from what she sees through the windscreen (the reflector, the second time in her life) and not from a list.

**5. Chapters 8 and 16 — a confrontation is promised at a chapter end and never comes.**

> "She's voted herself onto the platform. A metre from that stone, with a camera on her. I'll have my answer there."

That is the last line of chapter 8. In chapter 16 he is "at the back, under the last tree. He kept his hat on," and he never opens his mouth. Her confession is better for being unforced, but I spent eight chapters waiting for an ambush that the book forgot it had set.

Do: put him where he said he would be — at the foot of the steps, a metre from the stone, visibly waiting to ask. Then her sentence takes the question out of his mouth in front of the square, and "He took his hat off" lands on a man I have been watching.

**6. Chapters 4, 13 and 18 — the book keeps stepping back in time.**

> "It was the same as on Saturday."

> "For Lesca it was the night after the speech."

The hand on Coralie's fingers is shown at the end of 3, shown again from inside in 4, and recited back at the top of 5. Chapter 12 spends the morning of flood day laying tape for a seating plan, between Maylis hearing a living man swear at her (end of 11) and Maylis acting on it (13, which starts ten minutes after 11 ended). Chapter 17 carries Lesca through to Tuesday and the pillow; chapter 18 then rewinds to Sunday 02:51 for the last meeting of the two women, and has to apologise for it in its second paragraph.

Do: cut the hand scene to two tellings (drop the recitation in 5; start on the pad with the two boxes). Open 12 on Arlette or on Coralie with the speech; the tape and the eight names can come in a quarter of the space. End 17 on the landing with the lamp on, open 18 in the steel room with no patch, and place Bayonne, the vote and the pillow after it.

**7. Chapter 18 — the moral ledger rests on sums that do not add, and is then read out.**

> "I held on to it for eight rings, because I wanted the van, and after the van I wanted your bed. That was two minutes."

> "A woman is dead because of that, and it still isn't what I did. Don't give me your sum to hide behind."

Eight rings of a phone is under a minute, not two. "Two minutes would have covered them" asks me to believe that a woman with a broken side, two hundred metres short at 03:11, would have turned a winch past the nineteenth click that defeated a fit man with two arms. A book whose people count everything invites the reader to count, and a thriller reader will. Then Lesca tells Maylis, and me, exactly how to weigh the two nights. Chapter 15 had already made the case without a word.

Do: fix the clock (let it ring out and ring again, or drop the equivalence and count from 02:51 to 02:53). Let Lesca own her minutes without claiming they would have saved anyone; "I don't know what they'd have bought" is truer and worse. Cut the verdict from "If you had turned that winch first" to "what I did"; keep "Don't give me your sum to hide behind."

**8. Chapters 00 and 11 — the reader is ahead of the reveal.**

> "So Lesca had known. She had seen his van before she opened the gate."

The opening shows a woman turn her van to light the track, look down it, and ring a number once. Chapter 2 tells me a man was out there. From then on "Did you know he was out?" is a question I can answer, asked in 2, in 8 and in 10. Chapter 11 is then built as a revelation and replays the opening almost sentence for sentence before reaching what is new.

Do: keep the opening as it is; "It rang once" is worth the cost. Treat chapter 11 as confirmation: cut the approach (the drive through the Port, the hand on the clay) to three lines and spend the space on what the reader has not seen — the crooked reflector, 02:51 on the screen, the four minutes, the wader.

**9. Chapters 1, 5 and 18 — the device is set up as an experiment and abandoned; threads are left hanging.**

> "It was weather, and nobody had sent it. They left it there."

> "He didn't know that his name was on a sheet in Larrère's folder, or that she had kept a gate shut on Friday at five because of it. She didn't tell him."

Dr Kessler, the university and the sealed card promise a mechanism. The card does one job in chapters 3 and 4 and is then carried in a pocket to the last page. Maylis herself proves in chapter 5 that Lesca did not bring the rain, the question is put again in 18, and both women shrug. And the lever that made her say "Either" at dusk — Kévin's name on the sheet — is never resolved: did it go to Bayonne or not?

Do: one line of answer is enough for each. Either the lamp explains the weather (in the village where the left lamp came on, the wet winter waited) or the question is not asked a second time. And say what became of the sheet; if he sent it, the ending costs more, and if he did not, she kept a gate shut for nothing, which costs more still.

**10. Chapters 13 to 15 — terms and rules are re-explained at full speed.**

> "They shared the body the way they had on the other days. Lesca had the legs, the left hand and the mouth. Maylis had her right hand."

> "Larrère was in Jeannot's kitchen, on Jeannot's phone, and Jeannot was a kilometre away on the flood meadows, in his duck-hunting blind, under a hatch."

By chapter 13 I know what a pawl is, where the blind is, what the red line marks and who has which hand. Each is glossed again in the three chapters where every sentence should be moving.

Do: gloss once, the first time. From chapter 13 on, cut every appositive that defines something already defined.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Room twelve through "Forty-one"** (15). The hands outside the blanket, two fingers for the full half minute, the pencil line, the hatbox on the wardrobe, the empty chair in the hall.
2. **Kévin's five fingers at four o'clock** (15) and "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much." (18).
3. **The soup and the stairs** (6). The tick with a time on it, the tipped spoon, "I'd have this one again," the cut palm locked on the post, "Are you coming up?"
4. **The red reflector, both times** (11, 13). "Then the water rose up the door and covered it, and the red light went out." And at dusk: "She looked at it, looked away at the river gauge and looked at it again. It had not changed."
5. **"Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him"** and the sentence travelling "back through the square and down the road at walking pace, row by row" (16), with the man beyond the last loudspeaker who claps.
