# Critic report: Characters (round 18)

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** "I've worked out what that would cost me. It's a whole life with one hand." (10) The flood threatens the village and the other woman threatens the self, and both are in play by chapter 6.
- **Tension: 7.5.** "One woman could turn the winch, or she could reach this hatch. She couldn't do both." (9) Chapters 11 to 15 are held without a slack page; chapters 7 to 10 are held by procedure.
- **Progression: 6.5.** "For two nights she pushed up at it with all the breath she had, and stayed out of the chair." (9) The fight for the body, which is the book's middle, is summarised in a sentence here and again at the start of 10 and 11.
- **Comedy: 7.5.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station." (7)
- **Characters: 7.5.** "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." (9) Coralie is at 8.5, Lesca at 8, the village at 8, Maylis at 6.5.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'" (15) A running gag carried into the water and not dropped.
- **Prose: 7.** "It hurt to hear." (6) The narrator files the feeling after the scene has already delivered it.
- **The ending: 8.** "He went and got it. Then he stepped over the string in his waders and began to dig." (18)
- **Clarity: 6.5.** "She had kept Inguma from fetching her for eight rings of a phone, and that had been long enough." (14) Long enough for what was never a rule the reader was given; the death rule bends at the moment it matters.

**Finished unpaid:** yes. **Press it on a friend:** on a friend who loved The Good Place, yes. On a friend who reads only Crouch, no: that friend stops at the three numbered rules in chapter 5.

## 2. Against the comparisons, for Characters

**Above Crouch. Below The Good Place.**

- **Above Crouch on the partner.** Crouch makes a reader afraid for a marriage with one ordinary evening in a kitchen and then keeps the wife as the thing to be got back. This book does the kitchen as well (the eggs, the oven door, "In my tray") and then gives the partner her own knowledge and her own want. Chapter 12, where Coralie reads the call log off a chart, "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one", is a reversal that re-reads eleven chapters, and it belongs to her, not to either heroine. Daniela never gets that.
- **Above Crouch on the minor cast.** His walk-ons carry plot. Here Kévin, Jeannot, Maïté, Marie-Jo, Madame Etcheto and Sarthou each have a manner, a grievance and a turn, and five of the six pay off.
- **Below The Good Place on the central pair.** Its four are each wrong in their own way and they change each other with heat: every scene between Eleanor and Chidi has appetite in it. The two Maylises are wrong in their own ways (one decides and hides behind the sum, one refuses to decide) and that part is level with the comparison. But they meet five times and every meeting is an agenda. Neither is ever curious about the other. They trade advice in the last scene and part. The show would have made them need each other out loud; this book lets them invoice each other.
- **Below it on its lead.** Eleanor is funny in herself from her first line. Maylis is the one who laughs at somebody else's jokes, and for thirteen chapters the narrator has to tell us what she wants because she won't.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Lesca vanishes as a point of view for seven chapters, and her worst acts happen while she is gone.** Chapters 5 to 11 are all Maylis. Chapter 6: "Lesca didn't answer her. Lesca pulled." Chapter 7: "'I'd have gone up.'" Walking another woman's body up the stairs to that woman's partner is the worst thing anyone does in this book, worse than one ring of a phone, and the reader is never inside the woman doing it. She is seen from outside while she does wrong and from inside while she confesses (12, 14, 16, 17), which excuses her by arrangement. The book's own rule is also inconsistent: when Lesca rides in chapter 4 and Maylis rides in chapter 8 the point of view is the rider's, and when Lesca rides in 6 and 9 it is the host's. Fix: give Lesca chapter 6 from the soup to the newel post. Let the reader hear that laugh as the thing she crossed for, feel the arm that will not come, and choose the stairs with her. Maylis gets the hearing of the laugh back in the first page of 7, where she already washes two clean bowls. It costs the stairs seen from the trapped side; it buys a co-lead who is not let off.

**2. The hinge of Maylis's character is hung on a motive that does not survive the next six hours.** Chapter 13, five o'clock, Jeannot beside her in daylight: "If the gate moved, the sheet went to the prosecutor at Bayonne." Then: "'Either,' Maylis said." At eleven the same night: "She unlocked the box, lifted out the handle and fitted it onto the winch." She primes the winch to do at three the exact thing she would not do at five, and the sheet goes to Bayonne either way. So the boy's name is not why she failed, yet chapter 18 confirms it as the reason: "I kept that gate shut for a boy's name on a sheet of paper." The village then judges her on it. It also keeps Kévin a ward: he is on the platform an hour later with two good arms and she decides his prison for him without a word, and never tells him. Fix: make five o'clock what it is. She cannot say Open to a man's face, in daylight, with no order and nobody forcing her hand; the folder is in the car but it is not the reason and she knows it. Then let her tell Kévin about the sheet on the platform and let him answer, in three questions. A woman who dies for Maylis's own flaw costs more than one who dies for a blackmailer's device.

**3. The five words go unclaimed in the one room where neither can lie.** Chapter 14: "'Go to the blind first.'" Before them Maylis's plan was the gate: "I'll raise my gate by myself." After them she drives down the track. In the last meeting Lesca owns this: "Those two minutes are mine. I don't know what they would have bought." Two minutes. Not the sentence that sent the other woman a kilometre from her winch. Maylis lists the van, "Both", five o'clock and asking once, and does not say "you told me to". Lesca spends her last speech absolving: "You rang nine times. You walked his track at dusk." It reads as a woman still hiding behind a count, and nobody in the room notices. Fix: Maylis says the five words back to her. Lesca claims them as hers, as the answer she wanted run for her by somebody else's hands. Then "I'm not having you behind it as well" is earned.

**4. Lesca is paid too soon, and nobody on her own side prices the stairs.** Chapter 17: "That night Lesca was in the bedroom when Coralie carried her pillow back across the landing and laid it on the wall side of the bed." Three days after the microphone. When Lesca tells her own Coralie what she did over there, the answer is a joke about distance: "It's four steps from your door to mine, Madame le maire. They've taken you eleven years." The other Coralie saw it exactly: a body with somebody in it who could not speak. This one hears only that she was passed over. And the plot has just run the experiment for Lesca and shown the blind-first choice kills, so her sum is vindicated the same week. Confession, vindication and the pillow inside four pages is a reward. Fix: this Coralie asks the nurse's question, whose mouth it was and whether that one had a say, and the pillow does not move until Lesca has answered it. Or end 17 on "Arlette didn't ask" with the door open and the lamp on.

**5. The book never says what Coralie sees in Maylis.** Chapter 1: "Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you, and that was me." Chapter 18: "I chose you, Maylis, over somebody who asked." Chose her for what? We are shown what Coralie does for Maylis (cooks, straps, counts her pulse, makes her laugh) and what Maylis fails to give back. In sixteen years neither is given one thing she likes in the other that is not a service. There is no appetite anywhere: "bed" means a pillow and touch means "two fingers", which appears fourteen times; "pillow" twenty-seven. The only sentence with desire in it is Lesca's motion, "and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it". Coralie makes the complaint for me in chapter 10: "And then there's me, as a distance." Fix: one reason, in Coralie's voice, once, on the landing in 9 or at the table in 18. And one scene between 7 and 10 where the two are easy together and nothing is being tested.

**6. Maylis is narrated, not played.** Chapter 1: "Maylis did worry about it. She wanted that winch repaired before it broke more than a rib, and she had written to say so. But the man behind the glass paid her wages, and she didn't knock on it." The pattern is want, obstacle, silence, and it is stamped on scene after scene: thirty-odd sentences open "She wanted", "Maylis wanted" or "didn't want", five in chapter 1 alone. A character who cannot say what she wants needs the reader to work it out from what she does (writing *Either* and going out to the van does it; so does washing two clean bowls). The gloss does the reader's work and makes her a case being explained. Fix: keep the want-sentence only where it contradicts the act or surprises ("She found that she wanted the pillow more"; "She liked it. She slowed down to get more of it"). Cut the rest, about two in three.

**7. "Either" is a switch, not a psychology.** Twenty-five times, six in chapter 1 and six in chapter 5. By chapter 7 the reader hears the click before she speaks. It stands in for a person: the two sources given for it (she asked for Coralie once and would not push her luck; her father kept the key by never saying what he wanted) are one narrator sentence each and are never dramatised. Fix: ration it to the places where it turns something: the pad, the estimate on the bank, the jaw at breakfast in 6, Sarthou's form, the winch at five. Drop it to Kessler's panel, to Inguma, to Lesca's agenda and to Coralie's two boxes in 5, where ticking both already says it. Then give the father's silence one remembered moment that Maylis herself was present for.

**8. The two women never look at each other.** Chapter 5: "They were the same height, and neither of them liked it." That is the whole of what it is to meet yourself. Every meeting after is chaired: three points, one item, any other business. It is funny once and it is Lesca's voice, but it means the relationship the book is named for has one register. Maylis envies Lesca in narration ("She wanted to be able to do that. She didn't want it done for her"); Lesca never once envies, pities or recognises Maylis to her face. Fix: one exchange in chapter 10 that is not on the agenda. Each asks the other the thing only she would know to ask, about the life not lived: what the pasture looks like with ponies on it; what it is to have a table wait for you to sit. Ten lines.

**9. Maylis has a living mother a field away and never sees her.** Chapter 13: "Her mother's ponies had gone up onto the higher ground." That is the mother's whole presence in Maylis's village. Lesca's Arlette gets three scenes and some of the best lines in the book ("I've listened to the lake for you"). The un-estranged Arlette, the one who still has her pasture and a daughter who never signed it away, is the plainest measure of what the other life cost, and she is offstage for eighteen days of rain while her daughter loses a job, a key and a partner. It also leaves Maylis with nobody but Coralie, which makes her smaller than she is. Fix: one page on the Friday afternoon or the Sunday morning. Let that Arlette say what the father actually did with the key; at present it comes only from men using him as a stick.

**10. The cast list defeats a cold reader.** Larrère, Lafitte, Labat, Lassalle, Lesca. Darrigrand, Daguerre, Dufau, Ducasse. Castagnet, Castéra. Maylis, Maïté, Marie-Jo. In chapter 10 Lafitte, Larrère and Darrigrand act within eight lines. A reader cannot miss a villager she cannot tell from his neighbour. Fix: rename Lafitte, one of the D's and Castéra. An afternoon's work.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 12, the call log.** "Because you'd have told me." Coralie knowing for twelve years, and choosing a landing over an answer. It is the best scene in the book.
2. **Chapter 9, the landing.** Coralie stopping two steps short with two fingers on the wrist: "That's her. In this hand." The triangle decided by a nurse's ethics, not by plot.
3. **Kévin's fingers.** "Five", in the headlights with a wheelchair half up on the van, and "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much."
4. **Room twelve.** The pulse held "for the full half minute", the line through the last name, the hatbox on the wardrobe, and the chapter ending on "Forty-one".
5. **The kitchen and the graveyard of chapter 18.** The oven routine that breaks off at "The—", the car door shutting on a pillow, letter No. 42 delivered by hand, Jeannot calling her "Lesca", and the second shovel.
