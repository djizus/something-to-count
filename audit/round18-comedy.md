# Critic report, round 18: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "'The gate, or Castagnet? Say which, Maylis. For once.'" The public stake and the private one arrive in the same question.
- **Tension: 7.** "She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it broke." Chapters 13 to 15 hold. Chapters 7, 9 and 10 do not.
- **Progression: 6.** "For two nights she pushed up at it with all the breath she had, and stayed out of the chair." The middle keeps putting the premise away and taking it out again.
- **Comedy: 6.5.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" The gags are well built and pay off. There is one kind of joke, the lead does not make any, and the best comic situation is used once.
- **Characters: 7.** "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'" Coralie is the funniest person in the book and the only one whose jokes cost her something.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 7.5.** "Beyond the last loudspeaker a man began to clap, because he thought she had finished." This is the closest the book gets to the bar.
- **Prose: 6.** "It hurt to hear." The narrator tells the reader what the joke was, then what it felt like.
- **The ending: 7.** "'It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you.'" The last comic payoff is also the only kindness, and it is given grudgingly.
- **Clarity: 7.** "She had kept Inguma from fetching her for eight rings of a phone, and that had been long enough." Long enough for what is never said, and the death that follows depends on it.

Would I have finished it unpaid: yes. Would I press it on a friend: no. I would tell them about chapter 6 and chapter 16.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below The Good Place on comedy. Level with it on the switch. Crouch does not try to be funny, so he is no test here.**

1. **The Good Place has six people who are each wrong in their own way. This book has two, and a village that is dry and right.** Maylis is wrong by Either and Lesca is wrong by the sum, and when the two collide the book is at the comparison's level: "'All those in favour.' 'There are two of us.' 'Then it's carried, or it's a tie, and in a tie the chair decides.' 'There's nobody in the chair.'" Around them, Darrigrand, Maïté, Arlette, Marie-Jo and Coralie all make the same joke in the same rhythm.
2. **The Good Place makes its premise worse every episode. This book runs its best comic engine for one day in each direction.** A mayor inside a road-mender, in public, with one hand in revolt, is the farce the premise promises. It gets chapter 6 and half of chapter 8. The next full day of it is skipped in a sentence.
3. **Level: a joke planted early comes back as the wound.** "I'll be brief" ends as a man who says nothing at a garden gate, and as Coralie unable to finish the impression. The shopping-pad box ends as a police form with two boxes. "Here" ends in a flooded corridor. Lesca forbids Jeannot the eleventh and he goes "because you've said it". Her evacuation list empties the wrong end of the building first. That is how The Good Place uses Chidi's indecision, and it is done as well here.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The possession farce is played once each way and then put away (chapters 6, 8, 9).** Chapter 9: "At six an alarm rang across the landing, and it was Lesca who got Maylis out of bed." The next sentence is three in the afternoon. A whole second day of the mayor loose in the village, with the café already keeping a page headed *Madame le maire*, is not on the page. Chapter 10 shows what was missed: "'Ask her what you're going to say tomorrow,' said the man dealing the cards." Nobody does. Write that Saturday in full as the escalation of chapter 6. The village now knows, and it brings her its business: a boundary, a fuse, what Larrère will say next. Maylis has one hand to wreck each consultation. Pay for the pages by cutting chapter 7's eight-line steel-room meeting and the two pushed-against nights that open chapter 9.

**2. Maylis is the straight man of her own comedy (throughout).** The book says so: "At that table Coralie was the funny one, and Maylis was the one who laughed." Her one device is a refusal, and it is one word. She is funny twice. Once is when she has another woman's mouth: "'I go in at the front, Guy. In daylight, and she holds the ladder. I've never had to leave my car in the back lane with its lights off.'" The other is three pages from the end, when she is accused in the street and her mind goes to the survey: "It was closer to a kilometre and two hundred metres. She didn't correct her." That second one is a comic habit of mind, and it is hers. She prices and measures everything. Coralie has already named it ("'And then there's me, as a distance.'" "'Don't give me the measurement.'"). Plant it in chapter 1 and run it beside Either, so that she has one active tic and one passive one. The right hand is her other instrument: tipping the spoon, held up bandaged to apologise for the mouth. Give it more to do.

**3. Bingo night is thrown away, and the afterlife's joke has no original (chapter 9).** The whole cast is in one hall, the barmaid is possessed, an old woman wins a duck and finishes a prayer across the room. It is given about thirty-five lines and no number is ever called. The dead play bingo, so this is the scene that should teach the reader the caller's voice. Instead the formula is first heard among the dead: "It called her the way it called the numbers, first whole and then in parts." And the best gag of the night is reported four chapters later: "'He's been one number short of the grass trimmer ever since bingo night.'" Play the night at length. Let the living caller say a number whole and then in parts. Let Daguerre be one short on the page. Then "'Twelve. One, two.'" in chapter 16 re-reads a scene the reader laughed at.

**4. One wit in fifteen mouths, and every scene exits on it (chapters 4, 5, 12, 16).** "'Then the village can sweep it.'" "'I'd never hear the end of it. I wouldn't want to hear the beginning.'" "'I said I'd never set foot in here. Now I've set it.'" "'She isn't leaving the area.'" Four different people, one retort in two beats with the turn in the second. By chapter 10 the reader hears the button coming before the section break. The people who are funny in themselves have a form of their own: Jeannot numbers, Larrère announces brevity, Sarthou bills, Kévin asks, Daguerre says "Here". Keep the dry retort for Coralie and Marie-Jo. Take it away from Arlette, who should be blunt and long, and from Maïté, whose own form already exists and is better: she answers the unbearable question with a household repair.

**5. The narrator explains the joke.** Chapter 6: "In eighteen years beside that car Maylis had never seen it do that." Chapter 7: "In thirty years at that counter Jeannot Castagnet had never left a glass unfinished." Chapter 1: "It was true. Maylis had asked for Coralie, once, and got her." Chapter 5: "Maylis knew how a person lets a question go by. She did it herself, with Either." Chapter 6: "Lesca asked because she didn't know who he meant. Maylis knew." Chapter 8: "She was enjoying herself." Chapter 2: "The chair was Lesca, and her name was on the stone." Each follows a line that had already landed. Cut every one of them. "In N years she had never" appears at least six times and each time it is the caption under the picture. In chapter 8, keep "At home she got this man a few centimetres at a time", which is a joke, and cut the sentence before it, which is a label.

**6. The villain loses his comic form indoors, straight after the peak (chapter 7).** "'I don't want it back,' Guy Larrère said. 'I want it written down. Sit.'" Larrère is funny because he governs through three centimetres of window. Chapter 7 puts him behind a desk and he becomes a blackmailer in an office. The chapter after the book's funniest has one comic line that is his, "'That's prison, for eels.'" Dictate the resignation through the gap in the car window. She writes it on the bonnet in the rain and the ink of the tall L runs, as it did on the receipt card in chapter 1. Then the folder held against the glass in chapter 10 has a precedent, and he stays a comic figure while he does his worst.

**7. The first laugh comes too late.** The opening night is solemn. Chapter 1 opens on a paralysis, then a physics experiment, then a cracked rib. The first joke is "'The one with the flag,'" some sixty lines in. A reader who was promised comedy has by then been told three times that this is a grave book. The Good Place makes its first joke before it explains anything. Open chapter 1 in the square, with the mayor in his car denying he is the mayor to a postman. Bring the thing down off the wardrobe that night, where the chapter already brings it down a second time.

**8. Rule recaps stand in front of the comedy (chapters 6, 8, 11).** Chapter 6 opens: "It was the same as on Saturday. Lesca had stayed sitting in the chair, so she had come home inside Maylis's body, and she controlled nearly all of it." Chapter 5 set this out as a three-point meeting two pages earlier and ended on legs that moved untold. The recap delays the funniest scene in the book by two paragraphs. Open chapter 6 on the mayor's sentence coming out of the wrong mouth: "'What time does the director of the care home get in?'" Chapter 11's "By the first rule of the chair" can go the same way.

**9. The café goes offstage for the last third, and its last joke is only reported (chapter 18).** "'You'll hear it at the café, so you can have it from me.'" "'I'd rather you heard it from me than from the café.'" Two people tell her what the café says. The café itself is never entered again. It was the book's public: Josiane, the dealer, Lafitte and his pad, "'Which do I put?'" The line that replaces "still in the box" is the cruellest joke in the book and it is delivered second-hand. Lafitte's page headed *Madame le maire* has no last entry. Have Maylis walk in for bread on Sunday and hear it said to a man who is late. Have Lafitte write one line and turn the pad over.

**10. Words.** Chapter 5, breakfast: "'Was that you?' 'Either.'" It does not answer a yes-or-no question, and it spends the word in a chapter that uses it better eleven lines later, in the steel room ("'Don't. Not in here.'"). Cut the breakfast one. Chapter 5: "'So the one who stays sitting rides the one who wakes.'" In a book where the café's first response to the possession is a sex joke, "rides" gets a snigger that the rule-setting does not want. Everywhere else the word is "drives". Chapter 8 says of Kévin, "At home this boy asked three questions in every sentence," but his long speech in chapter 3 is a lecture with no question in it. Write the speech as questions, so that the silent Kévin with his cap off in chapter 8 has something to be the opposite of.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie and the oven, both times.** "'In my tray,' she said, and shut the door on him," in chapter 1. In chapter 18, "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'", and she cannot finish it.
2. **The square after the sentence (chapter 16).** The man who claps. Tastet turning the sheet over to see whether there is more on the back. "'I've got nothing on the sheet for this.'" "'Finish your word. You're writing on my chair.'"
3. **Madame Etcheto's care plan and her number.** "'Staff to wait. Staff not to join in.'" Then "'Twelve. One, two,'" and a chair pulled in. The funniest villager is the one the choice kills, and the reader learns it from a bingo call.
4. **Jeannot's count.** "'And six. Bugger off,'" coming straight after the drowning. "'So I'll go down on Friday the eleventh, because you've said it.'" Letter forty-two, delivered by hand because he will not pay to register it.
5. **Kévin's fingers.** The hourly count on the tractor, the count in the flooded yard with a wheelchair half up on the van, and "'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much.'" Add "'Here.' 'I know you are.'" to the same list. Neither should be explained.
