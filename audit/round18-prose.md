# Round 18, lens: Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

Counts below are mine, taken over the nineteen files. Sentence figures are for narration with the dialogue removed.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** 'Then which do you want?' [...] 'The gate, or Castagnet? Say which, Maylis. For once.' / 'Both.' (15)
- **Tension: 6.5.** Chapters 13 to 15 are an 8.5. The middle spends its tension on summary: "For two nights she pushed up at it with all the breath she had, and stayed out of the chair." (9)
- **Progression: 6.** Chapters 5 to 10 go round the same three rooms. The text admits it: Inguma "climbed up into the chair behind her, as it had done three times before." (14)
- **Comedy: 7.5.** 'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.' (7) All of it is in the dialogue. The narrator is never funny.
- **Characters: 8.** 'I've held hands for a living since I was nineteen. I know when a hand is holding on, and I know when it wants to be let go.' (5)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** 'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—' / "She stayed bent over for a moment, with her hand on the door." (18)
- **Prose: 6.5.** The two halves of that mark, side by side: "She watched her sentence travel back through the square and down the road at walking pace, row by row." (16) and "It was the same as on Saturday. Lesca had stayed sitting in the chair, so she had come home inside Maylis's body, and she controlled nearly all of it." (6)
- **The ending: 7.5.** "Then he stepped over the string in his waders and began to dig." (18) Maylis's ending costs her the job, the key, Coralie and a woman's life. Lesca's is softer and is closed by a labelled feeling: "She found that she wanted the pillow more." (17)
- **Clarity: 7.** I never lost the mechanism. The book pays for that four times over: "By the first rule of the chair, whatever one of them had in her head came to the chair, even when she was not sitting in it." (11)

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. From chapter 11 I could not have stopped. Between 6 and 10 I read on for Coralie's lines and skimmed the narration.

**Would I press it on a friend?** No. I would tell a friend about the oven door and room twelve, and I would keep the book back until the narrator stops explaining them.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Prose: below Crouch on the page, above him in the mouth.**

1. **Crouch changes gear and this narrator has one.** A Crouch chapter drops to one-word lines when the body is in danger and runs long when the mind is. Here every chapter averages between 9.1 and 11.7 words a sentence. Sentences of 25 words or more are 0 to 4 per cent of every chapter, and 35 to 51 per cent are eight words or fewer. The leaf rake in chapter 1 and the flood in chapter 15 have the same pulse. Chapter 15 gets its speed from the clock stamps (02:55, 02:57, 03:03), which work, and from nothing in the sentences.
2. **Crouch never explains his own scene.** He ends on the image and cuts. This narrator shows the screen reading 02:51 and then says "Now she knew why Inguma always came at the same time." The Good Place never glosses a joke after telling it. This narrator does: "It was what she always said to him." (6), "The chair was Lesca, and her name was on the stone." (2), "He meant the medal." (16).
3. **The dialogue and the callbacks are level with The Good Place and better than anything Crouch writes for a speaking person.** *Either*, *I'll be brief*, *Here*, *put it in forty-two*, the two fingers on the wrist, and the word on the card (*left*) coming back as Maïté's verb for the stone. Each one re-reads what came before it. None of them is explained when it lands, and that is why they land.

The book has two prose writers in it. The one who writes what people say is at the bar. The one who writes what Maylis thinks is at 5.5.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. The narrator explains the book's highest points, and chapter 11 spends chapter 12 (structural)

Chapter 11, after the flashback: "Now she knew why Inguma always came at the same time. Nine minutes to three was the time on Lesca's phone when she rang him." Then: "So Lesca had known. She had seen his van before she opened the gate." Then: "nothing in it told her which of the two Lesca had turned the handle for."

The scene had already shown all three. "The screen said 02:51." "Only one van in the village had a reflector like that." "Coralie's car was in the yard."

One chapter later Coralie says the same three things, and hers is the best speech in the book: 'Nine minutes to three,' she said. 'I've known that for twelve years.' / 'So for twelve years I've had two answers.' The reader arrives at it already told. The same question is then put a third time by Lesca on the bed and a fourth by Maïté in chapter 17.

It happens at the other peaks as well. Chapter 15 opens by explaining the end of 14: "She had wanted this body for good, and she had let go of it herself, to say five words." Chapter 16 states the motive one paragraph before the confession: "She could not ask that of her and then read this sentence." Chapter 6 closes the soup scene with "It hurt to hear."

**Do:** cut lines 61 to 69 of chapter 11 (235 words) down to the two sentences nobody else can say: "The time it came at was not hers. She had never had a time of her own." Let Coralie own 02:51 and the two answers. Cut the two sentences in 15 and the two in 16. Let Lesca look up from the sheet with no reason given.

### 2. Recaps and rule restatements at the head of chapters (structural)

A chapter break is where the reader is moving fastest. Seven chapters open by telling the reader what the last one ended on or how the chair works.

- 6: "It was the same as on Saturday. Lesca had stayed sitting in the chair, so she had come home inside Maylis's body [...]" The second to fourth paragraphs (168 words) restate chapter 5's three points and the end of chapter 5.
- 7: "She was waiting for the woman who had ticked a box and made her laugh with her head back. That woman had just left the house in a cold draught."
- 8: "She had given Inguma eighteen years of verges to count, and she had worked that out at eight hours. It was eight hours at the mayor's desk, to find out who could order a gate raised over Guy Larrère's head." This is the last page of chapter 7 again.
- 10: "Madame Etcheto had told her that Inguma didn't come for her. It came to that bed, and since the rib she had been lying where it sat."
- 11: "By the first rule of the chair [...]"
- 12: "On Wednesday night she had asked Maylis who would hold her hand down on the night of the flood. Maylis had not answered [...]"

Lesca's three numbered points in chapter 5 are funny and clear, because she chairs them as a meeting. They are enough. Every later restatement is the narrator checking that the reader was listening.

**Do:** open each of these chapters in the new action. Where a reminder is unavoidable, give it one clause inside a sentence that is doing something else.

### 3. The middle repeats its set pieces in the same words (structural)

Entry to the chair: "lay still under it and was in the chair" or a near copy nine times (3, 4, 5, 7 twice, 10, 11, 18). Exit: "Inguma came in over the sill [...] crossed the steel floor between them and climbed into the empty chair" in 5, 7 and 14. The fight for the mouth: "Maylis threw herself at the jaw, and got it." word for word in 6 and 14, with two variants in 9.

The repetition is meant as ritual. By chapter 10 I was reading it as a stage direction and skipping to the first line of speech. The steel-room meeting in chapter 7 is ten lines long, changes nothing, and exists to say "I'm coming to stay." The flood night is told in near-identical sentences in 00 and again in 11 ("in a sheet as wide as a door", "The clay moved under her palm"). The third echo in 15 pays because Maylis's hand is on the clay this time. The second, in 11, is a rerun of the prologue with a reflector added.

**Do:** fold the chapter 7 meeting into the end of chapter 6 as two lines on the stairs, or cut it. After chapter 5, get into the chair in a clause and out of it in a clause. In 11, skip what 00 told and start the flashback at the headlights on the track. Keep the verbatim jaw line for 14 only, where it is the one that counts.

### 4. One sentence shape for everything

"The mayor was Guy Larrère. Guy Larrère was also president of the landowners' association, and the association owned the sluice gate on the flood meadows below the village." (1)

"The picture was of a stone. The stone stood in front of the town hall, in the space where the mayor parked." (1)

"The Port was forty houses and the care home, on the low ground behind the embankment. The embankment was a bank of clay built between them and the river." (1)

Each sentence ends on a noun and the next one picks that noun up as its subject. It is easy to follow and it reads like a reading primer. Subordinate clauses appear in 3 to 9 per cent of narration sentences. Between a third and two thirds of all narration sentences start with a name or a pronoun (62 per cent in chapters 9 and 12).

The voice can do more. Chapter 16 has the longest sentences in the book and the best narration: "Beyond the last loudspeaker a man began to clap, because he thought she had finished." Chapter 11 is the second longest and the second best.

**Do:** join the chain-link pairs into one sentence each. In chapter 15, let the run across the meadow be one long sentence that stops when her breath does. Keep the short declaratives for the moments that deserve a full stop: "The water was level with the bed."

### 5. Wants and fears named outright, in one template

I count 79 narration sentences that state a want or a fear. "Wanted" appears 67 times. The template is: she wanted X, but Y, and she did not.

- "She wanted Coralie to stay awake [...] But Coralie had to be up before six, and Maylis didn't ask." (1)
- "Maylis wanted the duck. But Coralie would be the one cooking it [...]" (1)
- "She wanted that winch repaired [...] But the man behind the glass paid her wages, and she didn't knock on it." (1)
- "Maylis wanted to ask before the pillow was down. But she couldn't promise [...]" (9)
- "She wanted to say: don't go. But Coralie wanted that floor [...]" (9)

Three in the first chapter. The damage is to the two women. They are distinct the moment they speak and identical the moment the narrator speaks for them: "She was afraid of that folder. She wanted the gate more." (Maylis, 10) and "She found that she wanted the pillow more." (Lesca, 17). That is one sentence used for both.

"She was scared." stands alone as a paragraph in 00, 13 and 15. The echo from 00 to 15 is the point. The one in 13 uses it up early.

**Do:** keep a stated want only where the action hides it. "Maylis wanted the duck" earns its place because she then writes *Either*. Cut it wherever the next action shows it ("She wanted to say yes." / 'No,' Maylis said.). Cut "She was scared." from 13. Give each woman her own way of thinking on the page. The book already has them: Lesca itemises ("She had the sum."), Maylis prices a job ("the way she worked out the price of a job").

### 6. Glossary sentences

"The blind was his duck-hunting blind, a cellar under a hatch out on the flood meadows. Ducks only come down at night onto standing water. A hand of water is enough, the depth of a palm laid flat. On Monday the river had been low. When the river is low, a raised gate lets the water run off the meadows. Raising the gate had drained his blind." (1)

Sixty-seven words of lecture between Jeannot's 'Last night I sat over mud.' and Maylis's 'I was told to raise it.' It stops the first comic exchange in the book dead.

The pawl is defined three times: "The pawl was the steel catch that held the winch after every turn." (3), "the pawl, the steel catch that held each turn" (6), "the pawl slipped, the steel catch that held each turn" (11). "Duck-hunting blind" is written out in full nine times, as late as chapter 17, where it is put in Maïté's mouth: 'who was supposed to go round the duck-hunting blinds and warn them.' She has lived beside those meadows all her life and would say "the blinds".

**Do:** define by use, once. The maize farmer scene in chapter 3 already shows the pawl clicking and holding, so the definition can go from all three places. Let Jeannot explain his own water in his own numbered way; he would enjoy it. After the first mention, "the blind".

### 7. Chapters that end on the explanation and leave the image one paragraph up

- 2 ends: "She couldn't know that the rain over there would keep to her dates. But she knew the dates. If it kept to them [...]" The line to end on is two paragraphs earlier: "That village had not been spared its flood. It hadn't had it yet."
- 8 ends: "She didn't know what Coralie had done about it." The image is one paragraph up: "the council of Peyrebarthe watched its mayor not lift one."
- 9 ends: "Maylis had five nights to get through in that bed, on her back, with a rib that couldn't push." The image is: "Coralie got up and took her bowl to the sink."

Compare the endings that cut on the image or the line: "It rang once." (00), 'Are you coming up?' (6), "In her hand, the phone began to ring." (13), 'Forty-one,' Coralie said. (15). Those four are as good as a Crouch chapter ending.

**Do:** cut the last paragraph of 2, 8 and 9.

### 8. Who "she" is

Chapter 1: "Maylis watched her do it. Ticking a box didn't look hard. She wanted to be able to tick one and leave the other empty. / At ten she pulled the blanket off the bed and shook it out over the floor." The second "she" is Coralie. I read it as Maylis until the plane-tree line. Same chapter: "That morning she had written *Either* to save Coralie trouble, and it had made more. / She broke eggs into the pan." Coralie again.

The possession chapters over-correct. "Lesca" appears 47 times in chapter 6: "Lesca waited for the arm. Then Lesca got the left shoulder under the handle and pushed with the legs." [...] "Lesca kept the eyes on it" [...] "Lesca let the gate down" [...] "Lesca tied Maylis's handkerchief". The device of "the left hand", "the mouth", "the legs" already tells the reader who is driving, and it is a good device.

**Do:** name the woman when the subject changes and use the pronoun until it changes again. Fix the two in chapter 1 by name. In 6 and 9, halve the "Lesca"s.

### 9. Counted tics

- **"In N years, X had never."** Five in chapters 6 to 8: "In sixteen years she had never ticked one of those boxes", "In eighteen years beside that car Maylis had never seen it do that", "In sixteen years Maylis had never got that laugh out of her", "In thirty years at that counter Jeannot Castagnet had never left a glass unfinished." Then "in eighteen years she had never disobeyed that man" (13). Keep two. The window going all the way down needs no gloss; the glass has just gone into the door.
- **"At home"** fourteen times in chapter 8: "At home this boy", "At home that lake", "At home her name", "At home the rib", "At home nothing had ever". The chapter becomes a two-column table.
- **"the way" as a simile hinge**, 47 times, 17 of them in chapters 13 to 15: "the way Kévin did", "the way you carry a child", "the way he would have carried a sack of maize".
- **"Then" opening a sentence**, 129 times; 16 in chapter 13, 13 in chapter 15.
- **"It was"**, 29 times in chapter 15 alone, 18 of them opening a sentence: "It was Jeannot." "It was Monsieur Daguerre." "It was four o'clock." "It was Kévin Labat's van."
- **Absence reported by "Nobody" or "Nothing"**, 75 "nobody"s: "Nobody had asked her whether she minded.", "Nobody looked up.", "Nobody spoke.", "Nobody came up the steps. Nobody shouted."

**Do:** one pass per tic with a ceiling. None of these is wrong once.

### 10. Spelling, usage and continuity

- **Dialect.** The text is British in spelling and idiom (metre, colour, windscreen, tarmac, pavement, wheelie bin, handbrake, gear lever, mum, the tip, "Bugger off") and American in three nouns: "flashlight" 25 times (14 in chapter 15), "dish towel" 8 times, "truck" 8 times against "lorry" once (6).
- **The typed speech changes wording.** Lesca says of it 'It's the same every year. I change the date.' In 2 and 12 it reads "Jean Castagnet was at his blind that night". In 16, at the microphone, it reads "at his duck-hunting blind that night".
- **"Wednesday night" means two nights.** 11: "On Wednesday night Maylis had pushed against Inguma and kept out of the chair." 12: "On Wednesday night she had asked Maylis who would hold her hand down". The second happened in the small hours of Wednesday, a night earlier.
- **Pillar and pier.** The gauge is on "the concrete pillar of the gate" (3, 4, 10, 16) and "the pier" (6).
- **In and at.** "the prosecutor in Bayonne" (7), "the prosecutor at Bayonne" (13, twice). "The prefect's office in Pau" (6), "the prefecture at Pau" (10, 12, 13).
- **Police and gendarmes.** "typed, by the police" and "a police officer" (4), "police tape" (18); gendarmes everywhere else.
- **Small room and spare room.** "the small room" (4), "the spare room" thereafter.
- **Contractions in the narration** change by chapter. Chapter 8 has 22 contracted forms and one full form. Chapter 11 has none contracted and eight full. Chapter 13 mixes them a few lines apart: "She didn't." and "She did not go down." (Counted with dialogue included.) The formal register suits the flashback in 11. Elsewhere the narrator's voice is moving without a reason.

**Do:** one style sheet, one pass.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Room twelve**, chapter 15, from "Twelve was shut." to "There was a hatbox on it, and nothing else.", and the count that ends the chapter: 'Forty-one,' Coralie said. No feeling is named in it. That is why it works.
2. **The oven door in chapter 18.** 'I'll be brief,' [...] 'The—' and the joke not finishing. It is the best single beat in the book and it is two lines long.
3. **Coralie reading the call log**, chapter 12: 'Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one.' With the whole speech that follows, down to 'You didn't have to.'
4. **The square after the confession**, chapter 16: the sentence going back row by row, the loudspeaker saying 'drowned him' late, the man who claps, Tastet turning the sheet over to see whether there is more on the back, and Marie-Jo's 'Somebody has to stack them.'
5. **Kévin's fingers.** 'Five.' in the yard (15) and 'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much.' (18). The one place in the book where a stated want is exactly right, because a character says it.
