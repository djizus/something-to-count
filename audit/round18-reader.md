# Round 18: Reader

Lens: an ordinary reader with no special knowledge of rivers, villages, Basque, or French local government. Read `00.md` to `18.md` in order, in full, and nothing else.

Verdict against the bar: **below it, at 7.3.** The last third (13 to 16) is as good as the comparisons. The first six chapters are good. Chapters 7 to 10 and the amount I was told twice are what keep it off 8.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.5.** The river and the beds frighten; the thing that stops the gate does not. "That's prison, for eels." (7) is the counterweight to forty-two people, and the book has already shown me that it costs six months and ends with a job and a coat.
- **Tension: 7.** 13 to 15 are a 9: "One ring. The right hand moved before anything else did." (14). 7 to 10 are a 5: "She signed, and Sarthou took the form away with him." (7), the second signature in one chapter.
- **Progression: 6.** I was given most things twice. "It was the same as on Saturday. Lesca had stayed sitting in the chair, so she had come home inside Maylis's body, and she controlled nearly all of it." (6), two pages after I watched it happen and one chapter after Lesca itemised it in three points.
- **Comedy: 8.** "'Opened what? There's a name over it. It reads that she opened him.'" (2).
- **Characters: 8.** "'I know what you picked. I was the other one.'" (18).
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.5.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—' She stayed bent over for a moment, with her hand on the door." (18). The book's best joke fails on purpose and I felt it.
- **Prose: 7.5.** I never tripped on a sentence. I was often told what I had just seen: "It hurt to hear." (6). The word "wanted" appears 67 times in 47,000 words.
- **The ending: 8.** "Then he stepped over the string in his waders and began to dig." (18).
- **Clarity: 6.5.** "She had kept Inguma from fetching her for eight rings of a phone, and that had been long enough." (14). Long enough for what, and at whose cost, I could not say when I shut the book.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. I would have put it down for a day after chapter 7 and again in chapter 10. From 13 to the end I read in one sitting.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On one friend, with the sentence "it sags between the soup and the flood; keep going." Not on a friend who reads Crouch for the pull. A book I have to apologise for in advance is not at the bar.

## 2. Against the comparisons, as a reader

**Below Crouch on ease.** A Crouch reader never stops to work out what just happened, and is never told it twice. I stopped four times: the last line of 10, the hall in 14, the landing in 17, Lesca's sum in 18. And I learned to skim the first paragraph of a chapter because it restated the last paragraph of the one before.

**Below Crouch on premise.** He puts the box in play and keeps escalating the box. Here the box is in play by chapter 2 (faster than Dark Matter, and that is to the book's credit), then Dr Kessler and the university are never heard of again and a different rulebook takes over. I waited eighteen chapters for somebody to ask for the envelope.

**Level with The Good Place on people.** Everybody is wrong in a way that is theirs: Larrère through a gap in a window, Jeannot in numbered points, Marie-Jo through the chair, Sarthou billing the village for his own sandbags, Lesca in motions, Maylis in one word. The show did the trolley problem as a gag; this does it with a list on a cord and "Then they'll want the bins out," and keeps it both funny and expensive.

**Above Crouch on the marriage.** His wives are in danger; Coralie has a job application behind the fruit bowl, a call log she has kept quiet about for twelve years, and legs to walk out on. I was more afraid for this bed than for any in Crouch, because the threat to it was a person deciding.

**Below The Good Place on reversal.** The show's turn re-reads everything before it. This book's secret (she saw the van, she rang once) is on page one. The one true re-read is the kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice (11, 15, 17), and it is a good small one.

**What I did not understand.** Why the rain keeps to a twelve-year-old file. What the nineteen sandbags did. Whether the old woman took the chair Lesca refused. Where the Port sits relative to the meadows.

**Where I was bored or skimming.** All of 7. The first section of 9. The association vote in 10. The opening paragraphs of 6, 7, 8, 12. The first half of the flashback in 11, which I had already read as the prologue.

**Where I could not stop.** The end of 2. All of 6. The council in 8. From the reflector in 13 to "Forty-one."

**What I will remember in a month.** A boy counting his fingers in his own headlights and wanting six. An old man in a wheelchair saying "Here." A car window going all the way down. A red reflector going under. A man at the back of a crowd clapping because he thought she had finished. "I don't cook Either."

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The book tells me everything twice, and the flood night seven times.** (0, 2, 5, 11, 12, 16, 17; openings of 6, 7, 8, 12)
"Water was coming through the bank. It ran down the clay in a sheet as wide as a door and crossed the road in her headlights." That sentence is in the prologue and again, near word for word, in 11. By the time chapter 11 gives Maylis "the whole of that night," I have had the night from the prologue, the council speech, Lesca's telling in the steel room, and the phrase "it rang once." The chapter contains two new facts: the reflector and the kitchen clock. The same habit runs the chapter openings: 7 opens by re-describing the stairs I just left, 8 re-explains the verges and the eight hours from the last paragraph of 7, 12 re-explains the question from the end of 10. The double structure already shows me every event from two sides; the narrator then recaps on top of that.
*Do:* cut the recap paragraph at the head of 6, 7, 8 and 12 outright. In 11, start the flashback where she turns the van around; the phone call from Larrère can stay as two sentences, the bank and the palm on the clay go. Cut Lesca's ordered retelling in 5 to the date, the cellar and the nets. Keep the prologue.

**2. Chapters 7 to 10 run on signatures and votes, and the threat does not move.** (7, 9, 10)
"'I'm coming to stay.'" (7). "'This time I've come to stay.'" (9). "'I won't go.'" (10). Three chapters, one pitch. Around it, every move is a document: a resignation with a gap, a blank sheet, a gendarme's complaint, the complaint withdrawn, a letter to the prefect, a petition through a car window, a handover note. Each is funny alone. Together they are four chapters in which the river rises in one line per section and people sign things. After the delight of 6, chapter 7 is two scenes of Maylis being made to sign, and that is where I put the book down.
*Do:* delete Sarthou's complaint and its withdrawal (the café table in 7, the line in 9). Let Maylis spend her threat about the nets on the one thing I care about, keeping Jeannot off the meadows on the eleventh. He still says "He did that to you, and you've come in here and done it to me," he still votes Shut for the same reason, and one form, one signing and one loose end are gone. Then let Lesca say "to stay" once, in 7, and act on it afterwards without announcing it.

**3. At the climax I cannot tell what the rule did.** (13, 14, 16)
"'Nineteen,' Madame Etcheto said. 'That's not many. It counts quickly.'" Then: "Somebody had sat down to play with the dead, and it wasn't Lesca." A card with Lesca's name lies at an empty place; Lesca declines it with a joke; a number is called and a chair is pulled in; the number is the old woman's room. Did Lesca's refusal seat her? Did the nineteen bags bring the thing early to room twelve? Or did she drown because a woman ran four hundred metres the other way, and the hall is only an echo? Chapter 18 says the last ("Those two minutes are mine"), chapter 14 stages the first, chapter 13 plants the second. These are three different books, and the moral weight of the ending depends on which one I am in.
*Do:* pick the flood. Cut "It counts quickly" from 13 so the sandbags are an old woman's comfort and no more. In 16, keep the number called and the chair pulled in, and cut "and it wasn't Lesca," which is the sentence that turns an echo into a swap.

**4. Five o'clock on the bank hangs on a paper I never feared.** (13)
"In it was a folder, and in the folder was a sheet that had been blank on Tuesday, when she said open." This is the real choice of the book: daylight, the man above ground at her window, the key on her belt, her weight already on the handle. What stops her is explained to me in a paragraph about a folder, and I was shouting at the page. I believe she would say Either to Jeannot's face with Larrère watching; she has done it to six men on a bank already. I do not believe the sum the paragraph gives me, because she is certain of the river and the folder's worst is something the other village's Kévin has visibly survived.
*Do:* cut the folder paragraph at the handle. Go from "The river went by under the planks" and her belief in the dates straight to "'Either,' Maylis said." Jeannot's question, asked out loud with the car a hundred metres off, is enough; the folder is already in my head from the morning. The failure then reads as hers, which is what chapter 18 charges her with.

**5. I cannot draw the map the dilemma is measured on.** (0, 11, 15)
"On her other side the flood meadows lay far below." and "Forty houses stood below it." Both the Port and the meadows are below the bank on the land side, two hundred metres apart. The book gives me metres and minutes everywhere (six hundred behind, four hundred in front, twenty-five on foot, ten for the bank) and never the one fact that makes the sum mean something: why a river let onto the meadows does not arrive in the Port, and why a river in the Port does not run out onto the meadows. "Terrace" for the high ground also read to me as a patio the first two times.
*Do:* one sentence in chapter 1, at the first description of the Port, saying what stands between the houses and the meadows. Replace "terrace" with a plain word at first use.

**6. The box is abandoned and the rain is never accounted for.** (1, 5)
"'I want somebody to blame.' 'We had a dry winter here in 2014,' Maylis said. 'I was cutting verges in December. Nobody was to blame for a dry one.'" That is the only time anyone asks why a flood turns up twelve years late and keeps to a file day for day, and the scene moves to the next item. Dr Kessler told her to keep the envelope "until someone asked." Nobody from the university asks. A reader brought here by Crouch reads the whole book expecting the steel room to come back as more than furniture.
*Do:* not more science. Close it: let Maylis's line stand as the answer by having Lesca accept it in so many words, and give Kessler one closing line somewhere late (a letter never sent, a number that no longer answers) so I stop waiting.

**7. The two places where the book says what it means are the two hardest to follow.** (17, 18)
"'A woman is dead because of that, and it still isn't what I did.'" I read Lesca's paragraph in 18 three times before I had the claim: opening the gate was right, ringing once was the wrong, and Maylis did the opposite and it cost somebody else. And on the landing: "'I've had one thing from you at a microphone and three on the stairs, and I'm taking an hour over the last one.'" I had to count back to find the three. These are the verdict and the reconciliation. They should be the clearest lines in the book.
*Do:* in 18, cut Lesca's paragraph to the file, the van, "You rang nine times," and the refusal to let Maylis behind the sum. In 17, replace the count with what Coralie is doing: making her wait.

**8. I am glossed to as if I might forget.** (throughout)
"The pawl was the steel catch that held the winch after every turn." (3). "the pawl, the steel catch that held each turn" (6). "the pawl slipped, the steel catch that held each turn" (11). The blind is "his duck-hunting blind" nine times, to the last chapter but one. "The chair was Lesca, and her name was on the stone." explains a joke I had got. Once is a kindness; three times is a hand on my shoulder.
*Do:* gloss each thing once, at first use. After chapter 1 it is "the blind." Cut the sentence that explains "the chair."

**9. Five surnames begin with L and four with D.** (2, 6, 10, 15, 16)
"'I saw Larrère's window go down. Right down. What did you tell him?'" says old Lafitte, to Lesca, in a café where the Labats are discussed. Add the Lassalle sisters. Then Darrigrand, Daguerre, Dufau, Ducasse. In the café scene in 10 I twice gave a line to the wrong man.
*Do:* rename Lafitte and the Lassalles; rename one of Daguerre or Dufau.

**10. The cliffhanger of chapter 10 needs decoding, and it is the hinge into the last act.** (10)
"'So who holds my hand down then?'" I took it as a real question. It is a threat: on Friday there will be nobody in the house to stop her. I only understood it when 12 opened with "'Nobody,' Lesca said," and a paragraph of explanation, which is fault 1 paying for fault 10.
*Do:* let Lesca answer herself in the same breath, in 10. Then 12 can open on Arlette at the office door with no recap.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The evacuation in 15, as it stands.** "Here." "It's Saturday." "Then they'll want the bins out." Kévin setting the wheelchair down level to count five fingers at four o'clock, and "I wanted six, Maylis" in 18.
2. **All of chapter 6.** One tick with a time on it, the window going down into the door, "It takes a small m," the soup tipped back into the bowl, "Are you coming up?"
3. **Coralie and the oven door,** in 1 and in 18, and "I chose you, Maylis, over somebody who asked" between them.
4. **The square after the sentence in 16.** The sentence travelling back at walking pace, the man who claps, Tastet turning the sheet over to see if there is more, Marie-Jo and the two hundred chairs.
5. **Letter No. 42 and the second shovel.** The last page.
