# Critic report: Thriller lens

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `18.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

Verdict against the bar: below it as a thriller. A Crouch reader finishes this book and does not say "as good". The design is as good as his. The propulsion is not: the heroine is rescued by a timer too often, the night of the flood has a hole a reader can walk through, and the one fear the book spends eight chapters building, Coralie under the bank, is never made to hurt on the page.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** Countable and personal, and the villain's goal is written down in her own hand: "*That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*" (4). Held under 8 by the lever that decides the night: "I kept that gate shut for a boy's name on a sheet of paper." (18). Six months for eels is too light against forty houses.
- **Tension: 6.** It peaks on the stairs (6), in the mayor's office after two o'clock (8) and from 02:49 to 03:11 (13 to 15). Between those it is discharged from outside: "It came off the last stair and onto Maylis's chest, where she stood. The pull stopped." (6).
- **Progression: 6.** Every chapter changes something, but the first third tells its events twice and the middle goes into summary: "For two nights she pushed up at it with all the breath she had, and stayed out of the chair." (9).
- **Comedy: 7.5.** The comedy is made of obstruction, so it adds to the pressure: "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (7).
- **Characters: 7.** Coralie and Jeannot are at the level of the comparison: "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'" (9). The lead is defined by not choosing, and a thriller pays for that for thirteen chapters.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 7.** Clean at its best: "'Five,' he said." in the flooded yard (15). It fails once, where a joke waives the book's death penalty: "'The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies,'" (14).
- **Prose: 6.5.** Fluid and plain. It names the feeling a scene has already produced ("It hurt to hear.", 6), repeats its glosses (the pawl is explained in 3, 6 and 11), and mixes usage: "one lorry load at a time" (6) against "truck" (8, 12, 16); "flashlight" beside "windscreen" and "colour".
- **The ending: 7.** It costs Maylis everything she had: "'I know what you picked. I was the other one.'" (18). The other half is settled too fast and too neatly (17).
- **Clarity: 6.5.** The two women never blur. The rules do: "She had kept Inguma from fetching her for eight rings of a phone, and that had been long enough." (14). Long enough for what, and why she is in the hall of the dead and not dead, is not on the page.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. From the last line of 3 I was not going to stop before "Forty-one." I skimmed in 9, in the first half of 10 and in 17.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who reads for people and jokes, yes. On a friend who reads Crouch for the ride, not yet.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch on tension and propulsion. Level on design and reversal. Above on what the choice costs.**

1. **Below: who ends the crisis.** In Dark Matter the other Jason gets the house, the wife and a month in the bed, and the reader has to sit through it. Nothing saves the hero but what he does. Here the other woman never gets past the fourth stair, and three of the first four possession crises end because the creature turns up: "Then the thing was on Lesca, and it took her home." (4); the stairs (6); the council table (8). Maylis's grip on the post is the best physical image in the book and it wins nothing. The clock wins.
2. **Below: one clock is legible, one is not.** The river gauge is a Crouch clock, simple and falling: "a metre and a half under the line", "a metre twenty", "fifty-five", "forty", "a hand's width below". The counting clock is not. A lake takes fifteen hours, verges take sixteen, signatures take a day and a half, maize takes "till morning", ponies take eight rings. The reader can never see the deadline coming, except once, in 8, when "two" was written on a pad, and that is the tensest afternoon in the book.
3. **Level: the reversals re-read the book.** 02:51 turns out to be the minute of a phone call, and it re-reads the third sentence of chapter 1. The kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice (11) becomes "Under the time it said Castagnet, house." (15). The reflector "screwed on crooked" appears twice, twelve years apart. That is Good Place-grade planting.
4. **Above: the ending does not escape.** Crouch's heroes get out. This one saves a man who "was in no danger", loses a woman of ninety-four, loses her job and her village's opinion, and loses Coralie to a floor in Bayonne. "She had given the bank ten minutes. It had held for sixteen." keeps the question open for good. Crouch does not leave a reader with that.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The crises are ended by the timer, and the timer cannot be read. (4, 6, 8, 9; structural)**
"'Six until nine,' Lesca said at the table, to the right hand. 'Fifteen hours for a lake, and it's still counting.'" Then, on the stairs: "Cold came down the stairs."
The reader learns how long the lake took only when the time is up, so the stairs are not a race. They are a rescue that arrives. The same goes for the twenty minutes in 4 and the maize in 9. The one place it works is 8: "Nothing came at five past two. Nothing came at half past." There the reader had the hour in advance and watched it go by.
Do in 6 what 8 does. Give the creature one rate the reader can use and state the hour at six in the morning: the weighbridge book has so many loads, it will be done at nine. Put the kitchen clock at five to nine when Coralie goes to the stairs. Then Maylis's hand on the post is a five-minute hold the reader counts, and she wins it herself. Do the same for the signatures in 9. Nothing is added to the book but a number said early.

**2. Five o'clock is the fault the ending prosecutes, and it is the lightest scene of the night. (13)**
"'Either,' Maylis said. / He waited for her to say more. She didn't. / 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said."
The man is on the bank, in daylight, the handle is on, and she believes every date. Chapter 18 says three times that this was the moment (Maylis, Madame Darrigrand, Coralie). On the page it lasts half a page and is decided by a paragraph recalling a folder in a car a hundred metres away. The weight on the other side of the scale is a sheet of paper nobody present can see.
Put the weight on the planks. Kévin arrives an hour later to pull his nets: move him to five. He is on the platform with a green net in his hands, in Larrère's headlights, when she fits the handle. To turn it she has to convict him in front of the man who holds the sheet. Make Jeannot's body an obstacle as well as his question. Then let the scene run to twice its length. No new material is needed, only a reordering of two scenes in the same chapter.

**3. The night has three idle hours and a declined rescue, and the reader sees both. (13)**
"She knew to the minute what was going to happen" and then "Then the screen said 01:12, and she had not seen it change."
She knows the bank goes just after three. She pulls Jeannot out by the collar in seconds when it comes to it. Between 23:34 and 02:49 she sits in a van. Earlier, Kévin offers the whole solution in one line, "'I'll sit on his hatch all night, if you want. Then when you say, I put him in the seat.'", and is sent away in four: "She wanted to say yes." / "'He's mine.'" A thriller reader stops trusting the night here. The disaster feels steered.
Close both on the page. Give the dead stretch one event: at midnight she goes down a third time, gets the hatch open, gets a hand on him, and he goes back down, drags the weight across and does not answer again. Now three o'clock is harder than it was, and the idle hours are gone. And give the refusal of Kévin its price in the scene: she looks at the care home door, counts twelve seats against forty-two names, and says no out loud to the thing she wants. One more exchange is enough.

**4. Coralie is never in danger on the page. (15)**
"At the other end the front yard was lit up like a stage."
From chapter 10 the book says this is what frightens Maylis most. Sarthou gives one line of dread: "'The water went in at that end first. I saw it go.'" Twenty lines later Coralie is standing in a doorway reading names, and the reader was never made to think otherwise. The fear Crouch would have run for five pages is paid off before it is felt.
Have her arrive at a dark building. The minibus and the van are both up the hill on their first trip, the street lamps are out, and there is one flashlight on a windowsill and nobody in the doorway. She has to go in up to the knees and call. Coralie answers from the fire door, where she has just failed to open twelve. That costs thirty lines and uses only what is already in the chapter.

**5. The hall of the dead sits in the middle of the climax, and its penalty is waived with a line. (14, 16)**
"'The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies,' she said, in the voice she used for the prefecture."
"Go to the blind first." is the highest point of want in the book. The reader then gets a page and a half of bingo before Maylis is allowed to move. Worse, the rule the book has threatened since chapter 3, that the one who cannot be fetched is found dead, is enforced on neither woman. Lesca reaches the chair with her name on it, declines, and walks.
End 14 on "Then there was no steering wheel, and no rain." Open 16 with the hall. The reader then runs the whole of 15 not knowing whether Lesca is alive ("No cold had come into the van. Nothing had fetched Lesca."), and "'Twelve. One, two.'" is called while she is half down on the seat, straight after the reader has stood in room twelve. The voice stops reading her name because it has its player. She does not talk her way out. Somebody else has paid.

**6. The book's secret is half spent on page one and then handed over free. (00, 11)**
"She looked down that track while the wipers crossed the windscreen twice. Then she took out her phone." (00). And: "By the first rule of the chair, whatever one of them had in her head came to the chair, even when she was not sitting in it. So Maylis was given the whole of that night." (11).
By chapter 2 the reader of the prologue knows she looked and rang. Maïté's "Did you know he was out?" is a question only the village is still asking. Then the full night arrives as a transfer, justified by the narrator citing a rule. Maylis does nothing to get it.
Cut two paragraphs from the prologue: the van turned on the bank and the look down the track. Go from the handle held for three breaths to the phone. "It rang once." then reads as a call for orders, and the headlights on the reflector in 11 become a discovery. In 11, cut the narrator's rule. Have Maylis sit down in the cold chair on purpose to take what is in Lesca's head, knowing the price if she is still sitting when Lesca's house wakes.

**7. After "Forty-one." the book leaves Maylis for two chapters, and settles Lesca too easily. (16, 17)**
"That night Lesca was in the bedroom when Coralie carried her pillow back across the landing and laid it on the wall side of the bed."
The reader wants one thing after 15: what happens to Maylis and Coralie. Sixteen delivers a second climax and earns its place. Seventeen is seven scenes of settling accounts in a world where nothing can go wrong any more: the answer to Madame Etcheto, the kitchen, the gate, the landing, Bayonne, the vote, the pillow. Larrère's reckoning is "He said nothing." The woman who drowned a man gets her wife back in three days, in a one-sentence paragraph.
Cut the opening exchange of 17 (it tells Madame Etcheto what the reader watched) and end 16 on the church clock striking two. Fold the Bayonne statement into two lines. Keep Maïté's kitchen and the ladder whole. Make the pillow cost a line more than the vote: as written, losing the office buys it. Seventeen should run at two thirds of its length so that 18 arrives while "Forty-one." is still in the ear.

**8. Chapters 3 to 5 tell the same three days twice. (3, 4, 5)**
"'Come back across,' her mouth said." (3). "'Come back across. I've left you on that landing eleven years.'" (4). "'Come back across. I've left you on that landing eleven years.' She was standing in her work tunic" (5).
The envelope is opened twice, the warm chair is entered twice, the card is read twice in each chapter, and the hand over Coralie's fingers is given three times in twenty pages. This is where a Crouch reader first puts the book down. He would have crossed by now and stayed crossed.
Keep the last line of 3. In 4, cut the envelope scene to the word on the card and cut the first chair visit to the warmth and the gauge. Its new matter is the motion and Arlette. Open 5 on the pad and the two boxes, with the sentence already written on it.

**9. The middle goes flat and cancels its own hook. (9, 10)**
"Maylis had five nights to get through in that bed, on her back, with a rib that couldn't push." (end of 9). Then, one page on: "At six an alarm rang across the landing. Maylis got out of bed on her own legs." (10).
The threat that closes 9 is removed by a bandage before it is used. Lesca's second and longest possession, a day and a half, gets fewer pages than her first and repeats its beats. The letter to the prefect, the one move over the antagonist's head, returns three chapters later as a sheet "for my comments".
End 9 on "'It's twenty minutes to Bayonne,'" and Coralie taking her bowl to the sink. Drop the five nights. In 10, let lying on her side cost something on the first night so that the safe position is not free. Let Pau's reply reach the square in 10, on the day of the vote, where it can change what Larrère holds up to the glass.

**10. The narrator names the fear the scene has already made. (throughout)**
"It hurt to hear." (6). "She was afraid of that folder. She wanted the gate more." (10). "That frightened Maylis more than anything Lesca had told her." (10). "It scared her to do it." (3).
In each case the action beside it does the work: the spoon tipped back into the bowl, "Open" said a second time at the folder, the empty spare room. Named fear lets the reader off. Cut the label wherever a hand, a wrist or a number is already carrying it. Keep "She was scared." in 00, 13 and 15, where it is the echo between the two nights. Cut the second and third glosses of the pawl and of the duck-hunting blind: the reader has them by chapter 3.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The two planted clocks.** 02:51 as the minute of the call, and the kitchen clock ticking behind Larrère's voice in 11, paid in 15 with "Under the time it said Castagnet, house." and seen on the wall in 17.
2. **The right hand.** The ridden woman keeps one hand: the spoon tipped back, the wrist that pulls away under Coralie's fingers, the t crossed low, the handbrake. It makes a possession story physical and it is the only rule the reader never has to be reminded of.
3. **Coralie and the call log.** "'Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one.'" through "'Because you'd have told me.'" (12). It is the scene that makes a reader afraid for a marriage.
4. **The yard and room twelve.** From "Madame Hirigoyen. Frame." to "'Forty-one,' Coralie said.", with Kévin's phone buzzing at four and "'Five,'" in the middle of it, and the two fingers on a dead wrist "for the full half minute".
5. **The confession and how it travels.** "'Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him.'", the loudspeaker on the bank road a moment late, the man who claps because he thinks she has finished, Tastet turning the sheet over to look for more.

## 5. Chapter by chapter

- **00.** A woman, a bank, a phone. Frightened in a page, though it shows one thing too many. **8**
- **01.** The thing on the wardrobe is real dread. The postman and the registered letter run long before any threat returns. The last two lines save it. **7**
- **02.** The premise is in play and the date is set by the end, but she reasons her way to two villages lying in bed. I was told it, not shown it. **7**
- **03.** Four days in five scenes, and I drifted until the card said *right*. The last line is the first true hook. **8**
- **04.** The crossing and the motion give the book its villain. Half the chapter I had already read. **8**
- **05.** The rules as a meeting are funny and a lot to carry. The chrysanthemums are the best slow dread in the first half. **8**
- **06.** The best chapter before the flood. Every warning makes things worse, and the stairs frightened me, until the cold came down and did Maylis's work for her. **9**
- **07.** The undated letter is a good lever and Maylis learning to threaten is a real turn. It ends with her leaving her own body beside Coralie. **8**
- **08.** The afternoon after two o'clock is the tensest daytime stretch in the book. She enjoys the chair too much, which is right. **8**
- **09.** Summary nights, a second possession that repeats the first, and a hook the next page cancels. Coralie on the landing is the exception. **6**
- **10.** The vote and Jeannot's hand are sharp. The hire offer and "I won't go" restart the engine. The last question is the best chapter ending since 6. **8**
- **11.** The 2014 night is gripping and I already knew its outline. Nine rings and "Bugger off" are a release, not a pull. **7**
- **12.** Coralie has known for twelve years, and it changes the villain. As a thriller it is a pause with the clock running elsewhere. **7**
- **13.** Long and almost all of it earns its place. Five o'clock goes by too fast, the three hours in the van go by empty, and the roof bending in at 02:51 is the scare of the book. **9**
- **14.** Eight rings and a handbrake: the fight I had been waiting for. Then a bingo hall stood between me and the blind. **8**
- **15.** I could not stop. The run, the dip, the lamps going out from the bank end. I was never afraid for Coralie, and I should have been. **9**
- **16.** The sentence into the microphone is a second climax and works. It ends on a question I could answer. **6**
- **17.** The kitchen and the ladder are good. The rest is accounts being closed, and I wanted Maylis. **4**
- **18.** It pays. "I was the other one." and a second shovel. It leaves the right question open. **closes; 8 as an ending for Maylis**
