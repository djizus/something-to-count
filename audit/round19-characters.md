# Round 19 — Characters

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else opened but the brief.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes — 7.** "Coralie had a list, a minibus, Kévin's van and ten minutes' warning. Jeannot had a bolted hatch and nobody coming but Maylis." The stakes are people with names, but they are weighted at the dip so that her choice is defensible, and the bill is one woman of ninety-four who walked back to her own bed.
- **Tension — 6.5.** "It came off the last stair and onto Maylis's chest, where she stood. The pull stopped." The worst threat to the marriage ends because the monster has finished counting. That happens three times (stairs, kitchen table, council room).
- **Progression — 6.** "She meant never to lie still under Inguma again. The cracked rib gave her two nights of pushing. On the Friday night it had no push left." The heroine's will is beaten in a summary so that chapter 8 can have its bingo night.
- **Comedy — 8.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'"
- **Characters — 7.5.** "Lesca wanted to be looked at by the one who didn't know." Sentences like that one are at the bar. The pair of selves is not.
- **The switch between dark and comic — 8.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" The joke from chapter 1 fails in her mouth, and nothing is said about it.
- **Prose — 7.** "She found that she wanted the pillow more." The narrator says in a sentence what twelve years of landing had earned a scene for.
- **The ending — 7.5.** "Then he stepped over the string in his waders and began to dig." Maylis's ending is paid in full. Lesca's is a subordinate clause.
- **Clarity — 6.5.** "She had kept Inguma from fetching her for eight rings of a phone, and that had been long enough." Long enough for what? The rule in chapter 5 says the one it cannot fetch is found dead. Here she is called to the table and then fetched anyway.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. Chapters 11 to 13 at one sitting. I would have put it down for the night in chapter 7 and again in chapter 8.

**Press it on a friend?** On the friend who loves The Good Place, yes. I would not hand it to the Crouch reader as a book like Dark Matter.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Above Crouch on people. Level with The Good Place in the village. Below The Good Place at the centre, and below Crouch in fear for the marriage.**

- **Above Dark Matter: the second self is a person with a case.** Jason2 is a thief the reader never has to weigh. Lesca has paid for her life in Kévin's six months, four houses and her mother's ponies, and when she adds up what she would lose by staying she says "Nil." Daniela is what two men want. Coralie tells two women apart by a pulse ("That's her. In this hand."), refuses both of them a bed ("I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them"), and takes a hospital floor.
- **Level with The Good Place in the ensemble.** Each villager is wrong in a way that belongs only to them and is funny in it: Larrère governing through "a few centimetres of open window", Jeannot numbering his grievances, Marie-Jo speaking "to the wall above Lesca's head", Sarthou billing the village for his own sandbags, Madame Etcheto reporting Bixente because Inguma "had been told twice and given two lots of stars". The reversal in chapter 10, that Coralie has known the minute for twelve years, re-reads the landing the way that show's reversals re-read a season.
- **Below both at the centre.** The Good Place makes its humans better by sticking them together, and they come to like each other. Maylis and Lesca hold four minuted meetings and part on "Any other business?" Crouch keeps the wrong man in the bed for a hundred pages. Here Coralie has the two sorted by the wrist inside a week, so the dread for the marriage is over by chapter 8, and what replaces it (Bayonne) is an envelope until chapter 16.

**The lens questions, answered:**

- **Distinct?** Yes, down to syntax. One speaks in agenda items, the other in measurements and one word.
- **Wanted-for?** Both.
- **Softened or excused?** Both, by the same man (fault 1). Maylis more than Lesca.
- **Described from outside as a woman?** No. Neither has a face, a figure or a described age. Their bodies are a rib, a scar and a wrist. There is nothing to fix here.
- **Who would be missed?** Jeannot, Madame Etcheto and Kévin, then Arlette and Marie-Jo. Tastet, Madame Ducasse and Josiane would not be.
- **Which relationship carries the book?** Maylis and Coralie, not Maylis and Lesca. It pays in chapter 16, by loss: "I know what you picked. I was the other one."
- **Does the reader care who lives?** Yes, for Jeannot and Coralie. The book kills the one I had stopped watching, and that works.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. Both women are handed Guy Larrère as an excuse at the moment they should be alone with what they did. (11, 15, 16)**

Maylis, at five o'clock with the handle on the winch: "In it was a folder, and in the folder was a sheet that had been blank on Tuesday, when she said open. Now it had Kévin Labat's name on it." Then "'Either,' Maylis said."

Lesca, in the widow's kitchen: "He rang you with his order from that phone. He sent nobody to the blind, and he went home by the lane. For twelve years he's sat across a table from you and let you carry it."

Sixteen years of Either come from fear of owning a choice ("it would be her pawl"). The one Either that kills is bought with a hostage. The reader acquits her on the bank, so Madame Darrigrand's "You had the key" lands on a woman already forgiven. Lesca gets the mirror gift: the widow's twelve-year question turns out to be aimed at Larrère, and Lesca gets to be noble about it ("Don't give me him for that. He has his own to carry"). In both villages the worst person present is neither of them.

What I would do: keep the sheet and keep the kitchen phone, and take the absolution out of each.

- **Chapter 11.** Put the sheet second. The paragraph now spends all its words on Kévin's cap. Give the first place to what has always stopped her mouth: the man who pays her, a hundred metres off with his engine running, and Jeannot asking "Shut, or open?"
- **Chapter 16.** Use the room's own rule ("neither of us can lie"). Let her start "I kept that gate shut for a boy's name on a sheet of paper" and be unable to finish it standing. What she can say there is that the name was what she would have told people.
- **Chapter 15.** Cut "and let you carry it" and cut Lesca's two sentences of refusal. The phone on the wall then stands as Maïté's own guilt ("She kept her eyes off the phone"), which is what that scene is about.

**2. The last meeting of the two selves leaves the five words unclaimed. (16)**

"Those two minutes are mine. I don't know what they would have bought."

Lesca claims the minutes and not the instruction. "Go to the blind first" closes chapter 12, opens chapter 13 and is the reason she cannot read her sentence in chapter 14. In chapter 15 she tells her own Coralie "I don't know yet what that cost." Then, in the one room where she cannot lie, she says of Maylis's run "A woman is dead because of that, and it still isn't what I did." Maylis lists four faults of her own and never says "you told me to." Lesca's best line ("I'm not having you behind it as well") is cheap while she is still behind this.

What I would do: one exchange. Maylis says the five words back to her. Lesca has to say who they were for: herself. She sent another woman's legs down the track to do 2014 over. That is the last true thing about Lesca, and the book stops one sentence short of it.

**3. The two women never once meet as the same person. (5, 8, 12, 16)**

"'Any other business?' Lesca said." / "'No.'"

They share a father, a childhood, and a first and only asking ("I've had one straight answer out of you, and that was me"). None of it is ever exchanged. Each sees their father among the dead: "Her father was sitting at the end of the second row, with his watch beside his card" (8), and Lesca passes him: "She did not stop" (12). Neither mentions him to the other. Maylis spends a day being said good morning to and "slowed down to get more of it." Lesca gets the laugh Maylis never got. Neither tells the other. The meeting register is funny and it is Lesca's own, but it is the only register they have, so the relationship the premise sells is rivalry, a deal, a refusal, and no feeling. "They were the same height, and neither of them liked it" is the last time the book looks at the two of them as two of one.

What I would do: two lines in chapter 16 before "Any other business?" The father is the unused card. "He's at the end of the second row. He's got his watch out." / "I walked past him." Nothing warmer than that is needed.

**4. Maylis's Coralie is never wrong and never answered. (5, 8, 16)**

"On her way to the door she reached behind the fruit bowl and took out an envelope. It had been standing there since March."

She has drawn boxes for sixteen years to get a straight answer, and she has kept her own want behind a fruit bowl for nine months. She tells it first to a stranger using Maylis's mouth ("I've wanted it since March. I posted the form today, and I posted it for me"). The book puts even that on Maylis: "She had never asked Coralie what she wanted either." Coralie takes the last line of nearly every scene she is in, and nobody lands one on her. A character who is always the judge and never the defendant is not being written as a person, and the departure in chapter 16 reads as a verdict passed on Maylis, not a parting of two people who both failed to ask.

What I would do: give Maylis one true shot in the last kitchen, "Since March. Behind the fruit bowl", and let Coralie have no line back, once.

**5. Lesca's reward is narrated. (15)**

"That night Lesca was in the bedroom when Coralie carried her pillow back across the landing and laid it on the wall side of the bed."

The landing has been built since chapter 4 ("For eleven years Lesca had heard that woman get up on the far side of a landing"). The leaving in chapter 16 gets a bag, a strap, eleven stairs and "the car door shutting on a pillow." The return gets one sentence at the tail of a council vote, in a chapter with six scenes, and Coralie says nothing. The sentence before it is the narrator doing the feeling: "She found that she wanted the pillow more."

What I would do: cut "She found that she wanted the pillow more." Give the pillow a few lines of its own after the vote, with one thing only this Coralie does: she gives an order, and Lesca obeys it. Chapter 16's "Sit down when she tells you" then reads as where that came from.

**6. Lesca is worked from outside during the hour she crossed for. (6)**

"Lesca walked the legs."

Naming her without pronouns keeps the bodies clear, and it makes her an operator for the whole of the comic centrepiece. Her first meal in eleven years with the Coralie she lost is seen only as a spoon being tipped. Her side of it arrives two chapters later as a report: "I've had one bowl of soup at your table. I won't go." Chapter 4 shows it can be done from inside: "The fingers were warm, and rough at the tips from the soap at the care home." At the soup the only reading is Maylis's, and it is told: "It hurt to hear."

What I would do: keep the point of view. Maylis is in the same body, so let her feel what the laugh does to the part she is not driving, the way "Lesca kept the eyes on it for longer than a cut needed" does at the winch. Put one such beat at the table in place of "It hurt to hear."

**7. Maylis's will is beaten offstage, and her first public "Open" is buried. (8)**

"The cracked rib gave her two nights of pushing. On the Friday night it had no push left."

Chapter 8 runs seven sections and crosses three ways. In the middle of it she says what she wants in front of six men for the first time ("'Open,' Maylis said"), says it again with the folder held to the glass, and stops at the third ("She could give up her own job. She couldn't give up Kévin"). That is the hinge of her arc, and it comes between a possession she lost in a summary and a handover note.

What I would do: cut "She meant never to lie still under Inguma again", so the bingo night is not a broken vow. Put a chapter break before "On Tuesday the eighth", so "Open" opens something.

**8. Coralie learns the impossible offstage, and Maylis never has to say it. (5 to 8)**

"'It's still you,' she said. 'On Monday it fetched you off my stairs. I felt the cold go past me.'"

In chapter 5 Maylis cannot tell her: "Everything true she had would sound mad, or worse." By Sunday breakfast in chapter 8 Coralie has the whole cosmology (a mayor, a thing that fetches, a wrist to pin) and "It didn't take her long." Reading it off the wrist is right for her. But a care assistant who never once says this cannot be so is a convenience, and it spares the woman who cannot ask the hardest sentence she could be made to say.

What I would do: one beat on the landing in chapter 8 before the pillow goes. Coralie says it flat, "There are two of you", and makes Maylis answer yes or no.

**9. Maylis has no mother. (11)**

"Her mother's ponies had gone up onto the higher ground."

The book's method is the same face seen twice: Kévin with his cap in his hands, Maïté in black and in a housecoat, Coralie at a door frame and on the wall side. Arlette is the sharpest of those on Lesca's side ("You hear everybody. Then you do the sum by yourself"). She is the only one never shown on Maylis's side, where she still has her pasture and her ponies and presumably a daughter she speaks to. So Maylis has nobody but Coralie, and the reader never sees what Either kept.

What I would do: add no scene. Arlette moves her own ponies up at dusk on the Friday, and has one line across the bank.

**10. The narrator states the feeling after the scene has shown it.**

"Lesca wasn't afraid of a signature. She had Maylis's face and Maylis's hands, and she said what she wanted as if it cost nothing. Maylis wanted to be able to do that. She didn't want it done for her." (5)

The plain "She wanted" is the book's voice and mostly earns its place ("She wanted the duck"). These do not, because the scene has just done the work:

- "It hurt to hear." (6)
- "She was ashamed of that now." (9)
- "It was the first time she had asked anybody for help on that bank." (11)
- "She found that she wanted the pillow more." (15)
- "Maylis could put up with a thing sitting on her. She didn't want it near Coralie." (3)
- the last two sentences of the chapter 5 passage quoted above.

What I would do: cut them. Nothing replaces them.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The oven, twice.** Chapter 1: "'In my tray,' she said, and shut the door on him", then the strap and "Now you can." Chapter 16: "'The—'". The marriage is made and unmade with one prop.
2. **The right hand.** It tips the spoon back into the bowl, it crosses the t "low" in a letter it did not dare write, and it pulls away under two fingers until Coralie says "That's her. In this hand."
3. **Coralie and the call log.** "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." And "'Because you'd have told me.'" The whole of chapter 10's landing scene.
4. **Castagnet, living and dead.** "'And six. Bugger off.'" and "He put a kernel on a card." The living one will not stop numbering and the dead one will not look up. Lesca's "sends her apologies" belongs with it.
5. **Kévin's hand.** "'Five,' he said", in the yard with a wheelchair half up, and "'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much.'"
