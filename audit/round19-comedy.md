# Critic report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Then his death is mine as well, and I've stood beside his widow for eleven Decembers." (10) The stakes are exact and personal. The one that should frighten most, dying in your bed if the thing cannot fetch you, is only ever delivered as a joke (fault 6).
- **Tension: 7.** "03:11. She had given the bank ten minutes. It had held for sixteen." (13) Chapters 11 to 13 are a countdown as tight as Crouch's. Chapters 7 and 8 are procedure.
- **Progression: 6.** "The cracked rib gave her two nights of pushing. On the Friday night it had no push left." (8) Two days and a change of mind in two sentences, in a chapter that also holds eight scenes.
- **Comedy: 7.** "'I don't cook Either,' she said. 'There's no recipe for it. Soup or duck.'" (1) Funny, and the gags are load-bearing. Not as funny as The Good Place, for the reasons in section 2.
- **Characters: 8.** "I've held hands for a living since I was nineteen. I know when a hand is holding on, and I know when it wants to be let go." (5) Coralie is the best thing in the book.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" (16) The joke from chapter 1 fails in her mouth and the failure is the grief. This is at the level of the comparison.
- **Prose: 7.** "It hurt to hear." (6) Plain and easy to read, with a habit of captioning what the previous sentence already did.
- **The ending: 8.** "'I know what you picked. I was the other one.'" (16) It costs a death, a job and a marriage, and nobody is let off.
- **Clarity: 6.** "On Wednesday night she had asked Maylis who would hold her hand down" (10), against "On Wednesday night Maylis had pushed against Inguma and kept out of the chair" (9). Two different nights under one name in consecutive chapters. The rules of the chair are explained three times and the climax still needs a second read to see who wants the gate and who wants the blind.

Would I have finished it unpaid: yes. Would I press it on a friend: yes, on one who likes village comedy. Not yet on one who was promised The Good Place.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below The Good Place on comedy as a whole. Level with it on the switch. Above Crouch on what the doubling is used for.**

- **Below, on range.** The Good Place has four people wrong in four ways, and each says so out loud. This book has one comic register (minutes, forms, tallies, numbered grievances) shared by a dozen people, and a lead whose flaw is a single word. Sarthou, the woman at Hontaube, the young man from Pau, the lieutenant and Père Honoré are all funny in the same way.
- **Below, on the double act.** The Good Place keeps its sparring leads in one room. Here the two women talk to each other, on their feet, in four short scenes (5, 8, 12, 16). The one that works as comedy is the first: "'Then it's carried, or it's a tie, and in a tie the chair decides.' 'There's nobody in the chair.'"
- **Level, on turning a gag into grief.** "Either", Kévin's fingers, the oven, "Here", the bingo, "Put it in forty-two", "Still in the box": every running joke is cashed in the last four chapters as loss, and none of them is cute when it comes. The Good Place does this once or twice a season. This book does it eight times.
- **Above Crouch.** In Dark Matter the other self takes the wife and it is horror. Here the other self ticks a box on a shopping pad, and gets the laugh the original never got: "Lesca had got it in ten minutes, with Maylis's mouth." The theft is played over a bowl of soup and hurts more.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The double act is kept apart, and the room where neither can lie is used once.** Chapters 5 to 8.
"When we're on our feet we can talk, and neither of us can lie." (5) "She could also take the jaw for a word or two, if she threw everything she had at it." (6)
Between the meeting in 5 and the meeting at the end of 8, the two women do not exchange a sentence. In that stretch each lives a day in the other's life and does what the other never would: one ticks a box and calls the mayor Guy, the other lets him talk for eleven minutes. Neither gets to say a word to the other about it. The passenger rule gives the spoon and the stair post, which are good, and takes away the argument, which would be better. The no-lying rule is a gift of a comic premise and pays once, in "I can't stand here and say I'll go, because in here it would have to be true."
**Do:** add a steel-room meeting after the council in 7. Each reviews the other's day, under the rule that neither can lie. Maylis has to say she liked the square saying good morning. Lesca has to say why her hand rang the timer on Larrère's question. It is the scene the first half sets up and the book does not have.

**2. Chapter 8 spends the best comic set-up in the book in thirty lines.** Chapter 8.
"At eight that evening it was bingo night in the village hall, and the keyholder was serving at the bar."
Bingo night is the template for the hall of the dead. It needs to be warm, long and funny, so that the same room with heads down is terrible. It gets a duck, a ham, one speech and "How's that spelled?", then stops. Chapter 11 later calls back to a joke the reader was never shown: "He's been one number short of the grass trimmer ever since bingo night." The same chapter also carries the landing, the letter to the prefect, the breakfast fight, the yellow warning, the café vote, the handover note, the second meeting and the first sight of the dead.
**Do:** split 8 in two. Give bingo night a full scene: the caller, Daguerre and his missing number, Lesca running a bar like a council, Coralie watching from the radiators. End the first half on Coralie shutting the spare room door.

**3. At the moment it matters, "Either" is given an excuse.** Chapter 11.
"Now it had Kévin Labat's name on it. If the gate moved, the sheet went to the prosecutor at Bayonne." Then: "'Either,' Maylis said."
For ten chapters the word is her flaw and the book's best joke. At five o'clock on the bank, with the man above ground and the key on her belt, she says it because a boy will go to prison if she doesn't. That is a reason, and a decent one. The Good Place never lets Chidi off like this: the indecision kills him and nothing else is to blame. Here the folder is. Chapter 16 knows it ("I kept that gate shut for a boy's name on a sheet of paper") but the reader has already forgiven her.
**Do:** take the excuse away before Jeannot asks. Bring Kévin onto the platform first and have him tell her the nets are his to answer for. Then she still says Either, and the word is hers alone.

**4. Maylis is not funny in herself.** Chapters 1 to 6.
Her whole line is "Either" in 1, 3 (twice), 5 (twice), 6, 7 and 11. Chidi's indecision is funny because it talks. Hers is silent, and the narrator does the work. She is funny only where she prices things: "She had worked the verges out at the speed of the village tractor. Inguma had done them on foot." (8) "She took more than that to the tip in a morning." (16) And when she is in the other body: "She had learned a great deal about road salt." (7)
**Do:** let her price things out loud in 1 to 3, to Jeannot and to Kévin. A woman with a dry tongue who still says Either is funnier, and sadder, than a woman with no tongue.

**5. Coralie's gift is shown once and then reported.** Chapters 5 to 15.
"At that table Coralie was the funny one, and Maylis was the one who laughed." (6)
By chapter 6 this is no longer true on the page. After the oven in 1 and the corridor in 3, every scene Coralie has in Maylis's village is a demand: "Tick one." "I asked where you'd be." "I'm asking where I am in this." The failed impression in 16 rests on one performance fifteen chapters earlier.
**Do:** give her one more turn, in the soup scene in 6. She has just heard that the window went down; let her do the window. Then the oven in 16 has two behind it.

**6. The death rule arrives inside a joke every time, and the rules are told three times.** Chapters 3, 5, 6, 9.
"After that she plays bingo with the rest of the dead, a card of numbers each and maize to mark it, like bingo night in the village hall." (3) "And if it can't fetch her home, she is found dead in her own bed. [...] All those in favour." (5)
The meeting in 5 is the right way to do exposition and half of it lands as comedy. Then the narrator repeats it with no joke: "It was the same as on Saturday. Lesca had stayed sitting in the chair, so she had come home inside Maylis's body" (6), and "By the first rule of the chair" (9). Because the rule has only been funny, Lesca's "Nil" in 8 costs less than it should.
**Do:** cut the restatement in 6 to two sentences and the one in 9 to none. Give the rule one straight moment in 3: a name, on a grave Maylis dug.

**7. The first third of chapter 7 is three plot scenes at summary speed.** Chapter 7.
"At nine she had watched Guy Larrère make a threat, and now she knew how it was done."
The resignation, the complaint and Maylis's own threat to Jeannot each get about two hundred words. Maylis learning to blackmail from the man who has just blackmailed her is a dark comic turn and is over in twelve lines. The only laugh is Sarthou's: "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station."
**Do:** play the Jeannot scene at length, at the counter, with the café listening and not hearing. The space comes from fault 2's split.

**8. The explanation comes after the punchline.** Chapters 1, 2, 6.
"People in Peyrebarthe said that to anyone who came late. They had been saying it for twelve years, since the June of the steel room." (1) "That morning she had written *Either* to save Coralie trouble, and it had made more." (1) "The chair was Lesca, and her name was on the stone." (2) "It was what she always said to him." (6) "It hurt to hear." (6)
The reader needs the information. They need it before the line.
**Do:** move each gloss ahead of the joke it explains, as set-up. Cut the ones that name a feeling the line has just produced.

**9. Josiane's rumour never reaches the one person it is about.** Chapters 6, 7, 11.
"It's adultery either way, and that one lives with a nurse." (6)
It crosses to the other village ("Josiane had got there first"), Larrère uses it as a threat in 11, and then it is gone. "By ten the care home knew you'd given Larrère three minutes." By two it knew the rest. Coralie never mentions it.
**Do:** one exchange at the soup in 6. It is a laugh, and it is one more thing Coralie is carrying when she says "I'd have this one again."

**10. The form with no line for this is on its seventh outing by chapter 15.** Chapters 10, 11, 14, 15.
"It had no line for a mayor who stood on her cross of tape with nothing more to read." (14) "'The form needs a third box.' 'It has two.'" (15)
Two boxes and no third is the book's subject, and the joke is right. But every official tells it the same way. The young man from Pau has two scenes and is a clipboard in both.
**Do:** make him wrong differently. He is young and she is the heroine on his sheet: let him be pleased to meet her in 10. Then asking for the medal back in 14 costs him something.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie at the oven**, in 1 and in 16. "In my tray," and then "The—".
2. **Kévin's fingers.** The buzz on the tractor in 3, "Five" in the yard in 13, "I wanted six, Maylis" in 16.
3. **The end of chapter 9.** The 2014 night, then Jeannot's numbered call: "And six. Bugger off." "She listened to all of it."
4. **The corridor outside room twelve** in 3: "Staff to wait. Staff not to join in." And the fact that on the last night she says her words twice, which by her own rule brings it early. Do not point at it.
5. **Larrère's window.** A few centimetres, then "all the way down, into the door", then a meeting chaired through a café doorway ("The president's on the other side of it"), then "This time the mayor had no car around him". And in the other village, "I'll be brief," followed by nothing.
