## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.5.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the care home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." (2) One sentence each, and the sum never changes shape afterwards.
- **Tension: 7.** "The winch was six hundred metres behind her. The red reflector was four hundred metres in front. The bank had six minutes left." (13) That is the level. Chapters 5 to 8 do not reach it, because the narrator keeps stopping to restate how the chair works.
- **Progression: 6.5.** "It was the same as on Saturday. Lesca had stayed sitting in the chair, so she had come home inside Maylis's body, and she controlled nearly all of it." (6) The third telling of a rule the reader has had twice.
- **Comedy: 7.5.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (7)
- **Characters: 7.5.** "'I don't cook Either,' she said. 'There's no recipe for it. Soup or duck.'" (1) Coralie is alive from her first line. By chapter 14 four other women talk like her (fault 4).
- **Switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" (16) The joke from chapter 1, broken off. Nothing is explained.
- **Prose: 6.5.** "The Port was forty houses and the care home, on the low ground behind the embankment. The embankment was a bank of clay built between them and the river." (1) Clear, and written at one speed for a reader assumed to need every noun handed over. The best pages (9, 13, 14) are at 8.5. The narration between them is at 6.
- **Ending: 7.5.** "She heard the passage, and the front door, and the car door shutting on a pillow." (16)
- **Clarity: 7.** Sentence by sentence nothing is unclear. The mechanism is: "If the other one is still sitting in this chair when that happens, she goes home with the one who woke." (5) It takes five tellings because none of them is trusted.

Would I have finished it unpaid: yes. From chapter 9 I could not have stopped.
Would I press it on a friend: yes, and I would tell them to get through chapters 1, 3 and 5 on credit.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch on velocity.** A Crouch chapter changes its sentence length with the pulse: fragments when the character runs, a long line when he thinks. Here the mean sentence is 9.6 words in every chapter (range 8.7 to 11.4), half of all sentences are eight words or fewer, and one in fifty reaches twenty-five. Chapter 1 (eggs) and chapter 13 (the bank breaks) both average 9.3. The clock stamps in 13 do the accelerating that the sentences do not.

**Below Crouch on trust.** His narrator never tells you what the scene just showed. This one does it about forty times (fault 1).

**Above Crouch on the object that carries the feeling.** The reflector "screwed on crooked", two fingers on a wrist, a pillow, a tick in one box. Crouch states the marriage; this book puts it in a shopping pad.

**Level with The Good Place on comic form, below it on voices.** The timer, the minutes, "through the chair", Jeannot's numbered points are rule-games played straight, which is how that show makes procedure funny. But its six people are wrong in six cadences. Here, from chapter 10, Coralie, Arlette, Maïté, Marie-Jo and Madame Darrigrand share one.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The narrator states the want after the scene has shown it. Whole book; structural.**
"It hurt to hear." (6) "She found that she wanted the pillow more." (15) "It was the right answer, and Maylis had not been the one to give it." (8) "She was ashamed of that now." (9) "She liked it. She slowed down to get more of it." (7) "She was scared." (00, 11, 13, after the clay has already moved under her palm). "Ticking a box didn't look hard. She wanted to be able to tick one and leave the other empty." (1) "That wasn't true. She wanted Coralie to stay awake and keep those two fingers on her wrist." (1) "Wanted" appears 51 times in narration.
The book is about a woman who cannot say what she wants. A narrator who says it for her, every time, removes the pressure the scenes build. The spoon tipped back into the bowl is the hurt; "It hurt to hear" is a caption.
Do: go through every scene ending and delete the sentence that names the feeling or gives the reason she did not act. Keep a gloss only where it carries a fact the reader lacks ("Maylis had asked for Coralie, once, and got her" stays; the two sentences after it go). Keep "She was scared" in 00 and 11 as the echo across the two women; cut it in 13 and its cousins in 1, 3 and 5. This is a cut of perhaps 600 words and it is the largest gain available.

**2. The rules of the chair are told five times, three of them by the narrator. Chapters 3 to 9; structural.**
Madame Etcheto (3), Lesca's reasoning (4: "Lesca had stayed sitting, and so Lesca had gone with her"), Lesca's three points (5), the end of 5 ("Lesca had stayed sitting, so Lesca came home with her"), the second paragraph of 6, and "By the first rule of the chair, whatever one of them had in her head came to the chair" (9).
The three-point meeting in 5 is the right telling: it is in a character's voice and it is funny. The others are the book checking that the reader was listening.
Do: let 5 be the only full statement. Cut the recap paragraph in 6 to its last two sentences ("So Maylis had one hand..."). Cut the "stayed sitting, so" sentence everywhere after its first use. Cut "By the first rule of the chair" outright.

**3. One metre. Whole book.**
"The care home was the last building before the gap. Its long side faced the street, and its short end stood under the bank. That end was dark." (13) Subject, verb, object, full stop, at the same length as "The shopping pad was on the kitchen table." (1) Sentences open on "She" 570 times and "The" 471. "Then" opens 90. Scenes end on a one-line button almost without exception. The stock turn "It wasn't X. It was Y" recurs: "It wasn't loud. It sounded like a truck"; "It didn't ring. It buzzed twice"; "It was not brown any more. It was the colour of the sky."
The proof that the book can do otherwise is chapter 14: "She watched her sentence travel back through the square and down the road at walking pace, row by row." That paragraph runs long and is the best in the book.
Do: in 11 to 13 break to fragments under the clock stamps and join the stage-direction pairs. In the reckoning scenes (10, 15, 16) let one sentence per page run past thirty words. End one scene in three on something other than a snap line.

**4. The women converge on one voice. Chapters 10 to 16.**
"Don't tell me what he was." "Don't give me the minutes." "Don't give me the measurement." (Coralie) "Don't give me him for that." (Lesca) "I'm not asking you why. Not once. I don't want to be told." (Arlette) "I've never wanted to hear which one it was. Don't tell me now." (Coralie) "You'll hear it at the café, so you can have it from me." (Marie-Jo) "I'd rather you heard it from me than from the café." (Madame Darrigrand, same chapter.) "I'll say my part before you ask for it." (Lesca) "I'll say what it is to me, and then I won't say it again." (Coralie) "I'll say so while I'm standing in it." (Arlette)
Every woman announces what she will say, says it once, and forbids the reply. It is Coralie's manner, and it is strong in her. In five mouths it becomes the author's.
Do: leave it to Coralie. Arlette already has her own (she talks to the stove and the plate); give her refusal in that. Marie-Jo counts chairs; let her line be about the count. Cut one of the two café lines in 16.

**5. The frame of chapter 9 breaks point of view to excuse the flashback.**
"Lesca was in her own bed, pushing up against Inguma to keep herself out of the chair, as Maylis had done the night before. [...] Lesca did not want to stand in that room and be asked again." Maylis cannot know this, and the narrator has not been omniscient before. Then: "Lesca had gone over it every night for twelve years, and it was worn as smooth as a stone step."
These two paragraphs stand in front of the strongest writing in the manuscript.
Do: "Its arms were cold. Lesca was not in it." Then one sentence in the manner of chapter 2 ("There was an afternoon in her head, and it was not her afternoon"), and go straight to "A phone rang beside Lesca's bed in the dark."

**6. Definitions by copula, and repeated.**
"The pawl was the steel catch that held the winch after every turn." (3) "the pawl, the steel catch that held each turn" (6) "the pawl slipped, the steel catch that held each turn" (9). "The blind was his duck-hunting blind, a cellar under a hatch out on the flood meadows." "Duck-hunting blind" is written out eight times, the last in chapter 15. "The chair was Lesca, and her name was on the stone." (2, explaining its own joke.) "He meant the medal." (14) "A siren started up at Sarthou's house, the kind you turn by hand." (13, mid-flood.)
In chapter 1 the gloss also changes tense and voice: "Ducks only come down at night onto standing water. A hand of water is enough, the depth of a palm laid flat."
Do: define each term once and in motion ("The pawl clicked over at each turn" is already the definition). Cut every second and third gloss. Put the duck paragraph in Jeannot's mouth, where its tone belongs.

**7. Two women and one pronoun.**
Chapter 1: "Maylis was still standing there in her raincoat. Coralie rubbed her wet hair with the towel, hard." Whose hair? A paragraph about Maylis ends, and the next begins "She broke eggs into the pan." Later: "She wanted to be able to tick one and leave the other empty. At ten she pulled the blanket off the bed", and the reader takes "she" for Maylis until Coralie speaks.
Chapter 6 overcorrects: "Lesca waited for the arm. Then Lesca got the left shoulder under the handle [...] Lesca kept the eyes on it [...] Lesca let the gate down [...] Lesca tied Maylis's handkerchief".
Do: name Coralie at those three places in 1. In 6 the book has already found the better device, the definite article ("the left hand", "the mouth", "the eyes"); let the parts be the subjects and drop half the names.

**8. The ledger of years and nevers.**
"In eighteen years nobody in that square had waited to hear what she had to say." "In sixteen years she had never ticked one of those boxes." "In eighteen years beside that car Maylis had never seen it do that." "In sixteen years Maylis had never got that laugh out of her." "in eighteen years she had never disobeyed that man." A count of years, winters or Decembers appears 48 times; "nobody" 74; "had never" or "never once" 20.
Each one is a small appeal for pity, and the same appeal.
Do: keep the counts that are plot (twelve years of 02:51, eleven on the landing). Cut two in three of the rest. "The window went down. It went all the way down, into the door" needs no eighteen years after it.

**9. Involuntary-action phrasing is spent outside the possession scenes.**
"She had gone back in and paid for them before she knew what she was doing." (5) "It had come out before she had a reason to give for it." (5) "This mouth was used to saying what it wanted, and it had said it before she could stop it." (7) "It was out before Maylis had thought about it." (16) "Her right hand went out before she had decided anything." (6)
In this book a hand that moves without being told means another woman is in the body. Used for ordinary impulse, the phrase makes the reader check who is driving, and by 16 it has stopped meaning anything.
Do: keep the construction for chapters 3, 4, 6, 8 and 12. Elsewhere she simply does the thing.
