# Round 19: Reader

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. About 45,500 words. Nothing else opened.

Verdict against the bar: a good book that does not reach it. Overall 7. From chapter 9 to the last page it is as good as the comparisons. The first fifth and chapter 8 are where it falls short.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "The hatch was twenty-five minutes away on foot. The bank had ten." (9) Forty houses, forty-two beds, one man and a marriage, all dated and measured. The lever that decides the daylight chance is a sheet of paper about eels, and it is too light for what it has to move.
- **Tension: 7.** "In her hand, the phone began to ring." (11) Chapters 11 to 13 are a 9. Chapters 3, 4 and 8 are a 5, and Inguma's clock cannot be counted by the reader, so its arrivals do not frighten.
- **Progression: 6.** "The cracked rib gave her two nights of pushing. On the Friday night it had no push left." (8) Three nights and a third possession go by in two sentences, while chapters 3 and 4 spend 5,000 words showing one event from both sides.
- **Comedy: 7.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (7) I laughed out loud four or five times: the oven, the care plan, Jeannot's six points, Hontaube's form, the tied vote with two hands up. Between those it is dry committee humour that I smiled at and did not laugh at.
- **Characters: 8.** "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'" (8) Coralie, Jeannot, Madame Etcheto and Kévin are people I would know in the street.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—' She stayed bent over for a moment, with her hand on the door." (16) Also "'Here,' said Monsieur Daguerre, to the roof of the van." (13) The same joke is used on both sides of the flood, and it holds.
- **Prose: 6.** "It was true. Maylis had asked for Coralie, once, and got her. After that she hadn't wanted to push her luck by asking for anything else. That morning she had written *Either* to save Coralie trouble, and it had made more." (1) Always easy to read and often exact. It also explains what the scene has just shown, and it names what she wants in nearly every scene.
- **The ending: 8.** "Then he stepped over the string in his waders and began to dig." (16) It costs Maylis her job, Coralie and a death, and it does not console her.
- **Clarity: 7.** "She had kept Inguma from fetching her for eight rings of a phone, and that had been long enough." (12) Sentence by sentence I was never lost. On the rules of Inguma I was lost three times, and here I did not know what "long enough" had done.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. I would have reached chapter 5 on trust, and from chapter 6 I wanted to.

**Would I press it on a friend?** Yes, with the sentence "it starts slowly, wait for the soup". That sentence is the distance to the bar. Nobody says it about Dark Matter.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch at getting me in.** In Dark Matter the man is taken in the first chapter and I am reading to find out what has been done to him. Here the two women first speak to each other in chapter 5, at about 10,300 words of 45,500, which is 23 per cent of the book. Before that I am given the idea (chapter 2, in bed, by deduction) and then shown the first contact twice (end of 3, most of 4).

**Level with Crouch, and for me above, at making me afraid for a marriage.** "'I'd have this one again.'" (6) Another version of me is better with my wife than I am, in my own kitchen, using my mouth. Crouch does that with a gun and a chase. This does it with a tick in a box and a laugh that Maylis has never got out of Coralie in sixteen years. Chapter 6 is the chapter I could not stop.

**Level with The Good Place at making a moral flaw funny and then lethal.** "Either" is Chidi's indecision: a running joke about eggs in chapter 1 and a death by chapter 13. "'I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it.'" (16) is the series' move exactly, and it lands.

**Below The Good Place at the reversal that re-reads everything.** The book's big secret, that Lesca saw the van, is on the table from the prologue and from Maïté's question in chapter 2. Chapter 9 confirms it. It does not turn anything over. The reversal that does re-read the book is the smaller one in chapter 10, that Coralie has known the minute for twelve years.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The first contact is told twice, and the leads do not meet until a quarter of the way in. (3, 4, 5)**
"'Come back across,' her mouth said. Maylis had not said it." (3) Then "'Come back across. I've left you on that landing eleven years.'" (4) Then, as the second paragraph of 5, "'Come back across. I've left you on that landing eleven years.'" Chapter 4 opens the same envelope chapter 3 opened, sits in the same chair, reads the mirror card and arrives at the same morning. Dr Kessler's speech about the two lamps is also given in 1 and again in 2. I knew what was coming for most of chapter 4 and read it fast.
What I would do: keep chapter 4's new things (the landing, the flood file, waking in a bed with two pillows, Darrigrand's step, the motion) and cut its mirror of chapter 3 to a paragraph. Start it where Lesca stays sitting. Drop the quotation at the top of 5. That brings the meeting in the steel room forward by about 1,500 words. If chapter 3 can lose the winch inspection or the café as well, the two of them are talking before the 20 per cent mark.

**2. Chapter 8 is nine days and seven scenes, and the scene the ending depends on gets one page. (8)**
"Coralie had the two of them in front of her, in one chair. A right hand was clamped over a mouth, and a left hand was pulling at it. It didn't take her long." It didn't take the book long either. In this chapter there is a third possession, bingo night, the landing, the letter to the prefect, the Sunday eviction, the river at the line, the petition, the association vote, the handover note and the hall of the dead. This is where I lost count of the days and of who was in the body. The Sunday kitchen is where Coralie picks Maylis. Chapter 16 rests on it: "'I chose you, Maylis, over somebody who asked.'" I had to turn back to find when she had.
What I would do: make it two chapters. The first is Saturday and Sunday (hatch, bingo, landing, letter, breakfast) and ends on "'Friday.'" The second is Tuesday and Wednesday (the line, the petition, the vote, the note, the hall). Give the three hands on the kitchen table room. Show the Friday night she runs out of push instead of reporting it.

**3. The daylight chance is refused for a reason too small to tear me. (11)**
"She believed every date Lesca had given her. The river had kept all of them." Then, four sentences later, "'Either,' Maylis said." At five o'clock Jeannot is on the bank, the handle is on the winch and it is light. What stops her is Kévin's name on a sheet. I was not torn, I was annoyed with her, and I think the book wants me torn at that moment and annoyed only afterwards (Madame Darrigrand in 16 says what I was thinking). The paragraph that weighs it does not mention Coralie at all, so the book is not even letting her weigh what I am weighing.
What I would do: either make the other pan heavier or let her have a reason to doubt. Put Kévin on the platform at five instead of at dusk, so that she has to do it to his face. Or let the gauge at five read under the file for the first time, so that for one hour the dates are in question. And put Coralie's name in that paragraph, so that the "Either" is said with her in it.

**4. I could never count along with Inguma, so its arrivals feel placed. (6, 7, 8)**
"She had worked out eight hours. She didn't know how fast Inguma counted." (7) Neither did I, at any point. A gravel lake by the lorry load takes fifteen hours. The verges take thirteen instead of eight. Eleven years of signatures take a Saturday. Each time it arrives at the top of the scene: on the stairs in 6, as the vote ends in 7, at the bar in 8, at breakfast in 8. After the second I stopped fearing it and started expecting it.
What I would do: give one rate early and keep to it. Arlette's line is the place: "She gave it the grass on the common, blade by blade, and it was at that till daylight." (4) If I can do the sum, then when Lesca gives it the lake I will be looking at the kitchen clock with Maylis. Then let one fetch come at a moment that suits nobody.

**5. The secret is out in chapter 2, and chapter 9 is built as if it were not. (00, 2, 9)**
"For twelve years Lesca had been afraid that somebody would ask that question twice." (2) With the prologue's "The number was already in it", that told me she knew. Chapter 9 then repeats the prologue almost sentence for sentence ("The clay moved under her palm") and ends on "Nobody in that village knew she had looked down the track first", which was not news.
What I would do: choose. If it is meant as a turn, take the "afraid" sentence out of 2 and let Maïté's question read as grief. If it is meant as dread, cut from 9 the sentences I have already read and keep what is new: the order by phone and the kitchen clock, Coralie's car in the yard, the crooked reflector, the four minutes, the wader. Protect chapter 10. "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." is the real reversal.

**6. I do not know what killed Madame Etcheto. (12, 14, 16)**
"A chair was pulled in to a table. Somebody had sat down to play with the dead, and it wasn't Lesca." (14) Read cold, that says the hall had a place laid for Lesca, she walked away from it, and it took room twelve instead. If so, Lesca's escape cost a life, and nobody says it. In 16 she claims only "Those two minutes are mine." If it is not so, and the old woman died of the river and of "Go to the blind first", then "and it wasn't Lesca" is pointing me at a trade that did not happen.
What I would do: decide, and have Lesca say which in the steel room in 16, where she cannot lie. The harder answer is the better one.

**7. Too many pieces of paper carry the threat in the middle. (7, 8, 11)**
"Under her three letters about the winch was a blank sheet. He pulled it out for her to see." (7) In the first three pages of chapter 7 there is an undated resignation, a blank sheet that will become Kévin's, a complaint against persons unknown and a counter-threat about nets. By 11 the folder also holds her letter to the prefect. I read the Sarthou scene twice to work out who now had what on whom. The resignation is also a weak threat: I have already been told the job goes to Hontaube in January.
What I would do: one folder, one sheet, one name. Keep Kévin's. I would lose the complaint and the Sarthou form, and let Jeannot's grudge come from the café in 6 instead, where Lesca is already saying too much with Maylis's mouth and could say what is under his floorboards.

**8. A trained carer sits thirteen hours beside an open-eyed body and rings nobody. (7, 8)**
"'Your pad said two,' Coralie said. 'It's seven. Thirteen hours, with your eyes open, and I haven't gone in to work.'" (8) The other Coralie rings Bayonne within minutes (14). I did not believe this one waited five hours past the note. It also throws away the most frightening thing in chapter 7. I am told three times that the one it cannot fetch is found dead in her bed, and I never see that bedroom.
What I would do: one short scene in the bedroom at two o'clock, Coralie with the pad and the phone. Or she rings, the doctor comes and finds nothing. Either way I see what Maylis is afraid of.

**9. The narrator tells me what she wants, and then tells me what I have just seen. (throughout)**
"At that table Coralie was the funny one, and Maylis was the one who laughed. In sixteen years Maylis had never got that laugh out of her. Lesca had got it in ten minutes, with Maylis's mouth. It hurt to hear." (6) The first three sentences did it. So also "She liked it. She slowed down to get more of it." (7) and "She wanted to say yes." (11). By the middle I could feel the "She wanted" sentence coming in each scene. The glossary does the same: the pawl is explained as "the steel catch" in 3, 6 and 9, and it is "his duck-hunting blind" in full as late as 14.
What I would do: keep the want where it contradicts what she does ("She wanted Coralie to stay awake and keep those two fingers on her wrist... and Maylis didn't ask"). Cut it where the scene has shown it. Explain each object once.

**10. The last meeting in the steel room gives me the verdict, and it comes out of order. (16)**
"'A woman is dead because of that, and it still isn't what I did.'" I had done that sum at "Forty-one" and again on Madame Darrigrand's doorstep. The two then swap instructions like items on an agenda ("'Sit down when she tells you.'" "'Answer her.'"). It is also Saturday night, and I have just read Lesca's Monday and Tuesday in 15, so "Since then they've taken back the medal, and the keys" sent me back to check the day.
What I would do: cut it by half. Keep "Those two minutes are mine", "I'd sign your sum" and the refusal, "Drowned", and Inguma going out by the door. Say in its first line that it is the night of the flood.

Smaller things I did not understand, for the record:
- Why the rain in Maylis's village keeps to the 2014 file day for day. Chapter 5 raises it ("Then it had been raining for two days before you did anything") and drops it.
- Why the one visiting gets the whole body except the right hand.
- Why a river has a tide. I was never told the sea is close.
- Why "Madame le Maire" takes a small m.
- At the bar in 8, why Maylis shuts her teeth on the words. I worked it out, but I had to stop reading to do it.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The oven.** Coralie doing Larrère into it in chapter 1 ("'In my tray,' she said, and shut the door on him"), and not being able to finish it in 16.
2. **Madame Etcheto and the care plan.** "'She reported him. She said it had been told twice and given two lots of stars, and it got through them early and came at one.'" (3) Everything that happens in room twelve works because I laughed outside that door first.
3. **The phone log.** Chapter 10 from "'The morning after,' Coralie said" to "'Because you'd have told me.'"
4. **Chapter 13 as it stands.** The clock times, the corridor of chairs, "'Then they'll want the bins out,'" Kévin counting five in his headlights, the hatbox on the wardrobe, "'Forty-one.'"
5. **The last page.** Letter No. 42 that "doesn't thank you", the second shovel where her father kept it, the waders over the string.

## 5. Chapter by chapter

Mark is how much I wanted the next one.

- **00.** A woman, a bank, a phone that rings once. I knew what kind of trouble this was in two minutes. **8**
- **01.** The thing on the wardrobe held me. The square and the postman charmed me, but I did not yet know what the book was. The last two lines pulled. **7**
- **02.** A second woman works out the premise lying in bed. Clever, and I was told it. The timer ringing as the widow opens her mouth is the first big laugh, and the last paragraph sets a date. **7**
- **03.** Lights, eels, the care home, a winch, the café: I felt props being laid out. The corridor outside room twelve is the best thing so far. **6**
- **04.** I had read the first half already from the other side. It comes alive when she wakes in the bed with two pillows, and the motion's last line is a real hook. **7**
- **05.** They meet, and the book starts. The meeting with nobody in the chair, then the chrysanthemums. **8**
- **06.** Could not stop. Soup ticked, the window going all the way down, "I'd have this one again", the post at the bottom of the stairs. **9**
- **07.** Three pages of paper I had to reread, then the other village: the cap, the laugh in the minute, two hands up for one vote. **8**
- **08.** Lost the days. Each scene is good and none has room. The hall of the dead deserved to arrive in a quieter chapter. **6**
- **09.** The night in full. I knew its secret already, but the reflector going out got me. Then nine rings and "'And six. Bugger off.'" **8**
- **10.** The tape, the eight chairs, then the phone log. This is where I began to care about Lesca. **8**
- **11.** The longest chapter, and I did not notice. Hontaube's form, "Either" on the platform, the hatch bolted, Kévin sent to the care home, the roof of the van bending in. **10**
- **12.** Eight rings. Fast and clear, until the hall, where I was unsure what had just been decided. **9**
- **13.** The best chapter in the book. I read it without looking up. **9**
- **14.** The sentence going back through the square at walking pace, the man who claps, "'You've left out the place.'" The chapter's last line is a soft end after all that. **7**
- **15.** The kitchen, the gutter, the gate, the landing, the form, the vote, the pillow: closure five times, each one right, all in one chapter. I read on because I was owed Maylis. **7**
- **16.** The steel room explains too much. Then silt, the bus, the bag on the bed, the grave. I was sorry it stopped. **9**

## What I will remember in a month

Coralie talking into the oven. A thing the size of a sheep that never turns round. "Not answered. Two fifty-one." A boy in floodwater counting his fingers and wanting six. A suitcase with no dust on it and a bus fare that has gone up twice. A man who was in no danger, digging.

What I will not remember: who could order the gate, what was in the folder, and what happened on which day of chapter 8.
