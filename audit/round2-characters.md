# Critic's report: Characters

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `23.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 6.** "That village had not been spared December 2014. It had not had it yet." It is the line that puts a life in play, and it closes chapter 8 of 23. Until then the stake is whether a woman is going mad, which a Crouch reader will not wait a third of a book for.
- **Tension: 7.** "She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it went." Chapters 17 to 21 are held as tightly as anything in the comparisons. Chapters 3 to 7 and 9 to 13 are not held at all; they are visited.
- **Progression: 6.** "Inguma came at 02:51, and Maylis let it take her." The same sentence, with the name changed, is in chapter 4. Each night delivers one item, and "wrote before she moved" comes seven times. The book advances by ration.
- **Comedy: 7.** "There's nothing here that lets me un-receive it. There's no column." The procedure is funny and the people are funny inside it. It runs on one joke (the minute, the rule, the form) and is still paying at chapter 15, which is longer than it had a right to.
- **Characters: 7.** "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything as much." The cast round the two women is at 8 or above. The two women are at 6: distinct in speech, hard to want things for, and never once in conflict with each other on the page.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Here,' said Monsieur Daguerre, to the roof of the van." The loto joke is carried into the flood and comes out as grief without a join. This is the one thing the book does as well as The Good Place.
- **Prose: 7.** "She wrote before she moved, with the notebook over her face." Plain and exact, and a voice of its own. It is also one voice for two heads, and it repeats its own moves.
- **The ending: 7.** "He fetched it. Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." The last image is a 9. The chapter before it is a 5: the confession is shared out three ways and the mayor is paid the same night.
- **Clarity: 5.** "*1. When the other one gets up and I am still sitting, I go with her.*" The rules of the chair have to be given as numbered findings because the scenes did not make them plain, and I still cannot say what happens if both women stay seated. Two Coralies, two Kévins, two Marie-Jos and one date need a reader who takes notes.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** From chapter 8, yes. I would have stopped in chapter 6 or 7.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who likes a village told deadpan, yes, with a warning about the first seven chapters. On a friend who likes Crouch, no.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below the bar, by one clear step.** Above Crouch in the surrounding cast and in the wife. Below Crouch in the lead. Below The Good Place in what the doubles do to each other.

1. **Crouch's double takes the wife. This one never meets her.** In Dark Matter the other Jason is in the house with Daniela inside thirty pages, and every chapter after is fear for that marriage. Here each woman has her own Coralie, so nothing can be taken from anyone. The takeover that Lesca plans in chapter 14 would put a stranger in the wet Coralie's bed, and the book does not look at it. Lesca lists "a rib", "a village" and "half a hand" among what she would take, and leaves out the woman in the bed. The engine is in the book and is never switched on.
2. **The Good Place makes its people change each other in the same room. Here they minute each other.** The two women alter each other through a wall, by notebook, and meet once for five words. The villagers watch, count and write it down (Lafitte's list, Larrère's register). The exceptions are the best things in the book: Kévin taking the chair handles, Jeannot carrying Castéra, Jeannot in the grave.
3. **Where it beats Crouch: nobody round the lead is furniture.** Crouch's secondary people serve the chase. Coralie, Kévin, Jeannot, Marie-Jo, Sarthou and Madame Etcheto are each wrong in their own way, in The Good Place's sense: Coralie kept a landing so as not to know, Kévin looked up at the camera, Marie-Jo cannot say a name, Sarthou left a line out of a report. That is the standard the two leads are not held to.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The double never touches the marriage. (Chapters 14, 15, 16.)**
"Downstairs a telephone rang. A woman crossed the kitchen and lifted it, and it was Coralie's step." Lesca is in the other woman's body for nine breaths and Coralie is on another floor. In the mirror scene: "In the doorway a woman in a dressing gown said, 'Fourteen minutes,' and it was Coralie's voice", and Maylis is fetched back before she can turn her head. Twice the book has the wrong woman in the bed and both times it keeps the wife out of reach. What we get is the aftermath, a slip of the tongue over coffee ("I want her in my office at nine"; "Are you on earlies tomorrow?"). Those are good, and they are comedy. The fear is missing.
*Do:* give one crossing to Coralie. Lesca, in Maylis's body, with the wet Coralie beside the bed, taking her pulse as in chapter 1, and Lesca speaking with that mouth to a woman who has shared that bed for sixteen years and whom she has kept across a landing for eleven. Let Coralie know something is wrong before Inguma fetches Lesca back. Then make Lesca's motion in chapter 14 say what it is: that she would be taking her. This is the structural change the book most needs, and it costs one scene.

**2. The two women meet once, and one of them says nothing. (Chapters 20, 21.)**
"Neither of them spoke." Then, from the other side: "She looked at it for as long as it was held out." The relationship the whole book is built on has one scene, told twice with the same furniture, and in it Maylis is given no line and no act but stepping back. By then she knows everything ("She had been at the gate in good time, with a telephone in her hand, and had chosen"). Lesca does not know that she knows. Nobody in either village has ever looked at Lesca with the whole of it, and the one person who can is silent.
*Do:* give Maylis one sentence, or one act toward her. The honest one is the fact: "You rang once." Lesca hears her own account from the only witness who was her. Then the five words are an answer and not a broadcast, and chapter 21 stops being a second copy of chapter 20.

**3. The narration turns its back at the moments that would commit a character. (Chapters 17, 18, 22, 23.)**
"'Which were you counting?' Lesca did not answer." Earlier: "nothing in what came told Maylis which of them had turned the handle." And at the close: "Maylis heard her on the landing, and a door, and did not listen for which." The first refusal, Coralie's in chapter 18 ("Don't say it now"), is character: she has built eleven years on not hearing it. The others are the author declining. The single act that makes Lesca who she is has no inside. We have lived in her head for eleven chapters and are not told whether she turned the handle for forty houses or for one car in a yard.
*Do:* in chapter 22, let her answer Maïté. Either the answer, or "I don't know, and I have had twelve years to know", said aloud, which is a harder confession than the one she gives. Keep Coralie's refusal exactly as it is. And decide the door in chapter 23.

**4. The confession is shared out before it has landed, and Lesca is paid the same night. (Chapter 22.)**
"'Then that's the gendarme, and you,' she said. 'And I'll give you the third. He rang you from here.'" Lesca says "I drowned him", and eleven lines later the widow has handed her two co-defendants and a villain. The scene becomes Larrère's exposure. Three hours on, Lesca tells Coralie she sent a river onto a woman of ninety-four, and Coralie's whole answer is "Then she'd said her words" and a pillow carried back. Inguma is gone as well. The woman who sent the message gets absolution, the wife and a clear wardrobe in one evening. The woman who obeyed it gets a flooded Port, a broken side and a grave to dig.
*Do:* part the two reveals. Let Lesca learn of the telephone first (from Sarthou's page and the clock) and confess anyway, so that she cannot use it. Give Maïté's reaction to "once" a scene to itself. And make the dry Coralie take a night over room twelve before the pillow moves.

**5. Maylis's wish for the sash is asserted in one sentence. (Chapter 15.)**
"She found that she wanted it with the stone in." This is the turn for her half of the book: a woman who writes Either on a shopping pad wants a life in which she drowned a man, her mother will not enter her office and Coralie sleeps across a landing. It is told to us while she counts road signs. Everything before it that looks like wanting is staged as leakage (the signature, "You have three minutes", the mayor's pen in her teeth), so the reader cannot tell desire from contamination. Because the wanting is thin, the giving up in chapter 21 is thin: "Maylis stepped back from the chair, and left it to Inguma."
*Do:* one scene before chapter 15 in which Maylis uses authority on purpose and likes it, with no bleed to blame. The seed is there, after the blackmail: "It was the first thing in Peyrebarthe that had been done because she said so." Follow that with pleasure instead of four chapters of not looking at his boots.

**6. Maylis is built from other people's verdicts on her. (Chapters 1, 3, 7, 9.)**
Coralie: "Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you." Kévin: "So I don't know what you want." Arlette: "I've stopped asking you things." Marie-Jo: "It's the one thing I ever asked you, Maylis." Jeannot: "Nobody asks you, do they?" Five people deliver one diagnosis in the first nine chapters, and the narration gives her nothing of her own to set against it. The first sentence of her interior that is a want is "*I want that*", at the end of chapter 7. A woman seen only by what others say she lacks is being described from outside.
*Do:* cut the chorus to two (Coralie and Marie-Jo, who have earned it). In chapters 1 to 5 let the reader see one thing she wants and will not say, so that her blankness is a decision the reader is in on. The ninth string bought on her own card, and the climb along the loader arm with a cracked rib, are that thing already. Say what they are to her.

**7. Maïté and Larrère are turned into plot by an affair the page never makes human. (Chapters 13, 22, 23.)**
"He came when Jeannot's van went down the lane, on a duck night. He'd done it for four years." It is Maïté's only interior fact, and it arrives as a speech. Before it she is a handbag and a radiator, or this: "She was a wife in a housecoat, with her husband alive at the café counter." After it she is a suitcase on a bus. She is the one person in the book described as a wife and a widow and never as a person; we never learn one thing she wanted from Guy Larrère, who up to that page is a man talking through four centimetres of window. And the charge against him is blurred. His order was shut, which kept Jeannot dry. What he is guilty of is ordering forty houses to sit under a failing bank for the sake of three hundred hectares, and that is not what he is caught for. He is caught for a kitchen. "That goes in the file": what file, for what offence?
*Do:* give Maïté one line that is hers about why (thirty-one years of a man in a hole in a meadow is a reason, and she half says it). Make Sarthou's charge the order, with the kitchen as the proof of what Larrère knew when he gave it.

**8. Two Coralies, one voice. (Chapters 9, 21, 22; and throughout.)**
The wet Coralie: "Don't give me the measurement." "Don't give me the minutes." The dry Coralie: "Don't give me the minutes." Both stand "with one hand on the frame". Both carry a pillow to the box room and one carries it back. The dry Coralie begins with a weapon of her own, the title ("Madame le maire has her committee at ten. She might sleep tonight"), and has dropped it by chapter 6. One has run a home for years and kept a secret for twelve; the other is a care assistant waiting behind a fruit bowl. They should not sound alike, and the reader's attachment, made in the kitchen of chapter 1, is divided between two women it cannot tell apart by ear.
*Do:* keep the title in the dry Coralie's mouth to the end, and let the office show in her (she gives instructions; the wet one asks questions). Give the catchphrase to one of them only.

**9. Every villager is one tag, and every change is the tag breaking. (Chapters 9, 14, 15, 19, 23.)**
"He had left the little off. She heard it go." "Then he put the window down as far as it went. She had not seen that done in eighteen years." "She took her bag into her left hand, which she did not do in that room." "He stood with his shoulders up, and they stayed up." The method is exact and by the fourth use it is visible as a method: the reader waits for the prop to move and is told it has. It makes the cast easy to hold and it stands in for behaviour.
*Do:* leave it for Jeannot and Larrère, where it is funniest. For Maïté and Kévin let the change be only the thing done (the bus; the chair handles), and cut the sentence that points at the tic.

**10. The death falls where the book can best afford it, and is signposted. (Chapters 3, 19, 20.)**
"'It's the end room,' Coralie said, low. 'Under the bank. She will, too.'" Before it: "a window with the bank in it". After it: "in twelve then, as now, at the dyke end". By chapter 19 the reader knows who is marked. She is ninety-four, comic, and ready; Coralie, Arlette, the baby and Jeannot all come through. The night never makes the reader afraid for Coralie, who is in that building: she is found in the first minute, in command, reading a list.
*Do:* cut two of the three signposts. And keep Coralie out of sight for a page in chapter 21, with the dyke end dark and the list unanswered, before the lit yard.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The kitchen in chapter 1.** Coralie doing Larrère into the oven, strapping the rib so that Maylis can laugh, and "Eggs is what Either gets in this house." It is the only scene of the marriage happy, and the rest of the book borrows against it.
2. **Coralie and the telephone, chapter 18.** "Because you'd have told me." "I wanted you in the house. I thought I could have the one without the other, if I kept a landing between." The best scene in the book and the only reversal that re-reads the heroine.
3. **Kévin's fingers.** The buzz at four in the flood, "Five", said "as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another", and "I wanted six, Maylis" on the step of the van.
4. **Madame Etcheto entire, and Monsieur Daguerre's "Here".** The report on Bixente and the two lots of stars; the duck on her knees; the sandbags counted aloud; and, the morning after she drowns in one village, the front row in the other: "Her lips were moving."
5. **Jeannot Castagnet by number.** The six-point tirade at five in the morning, the letter that cannot be un-received, No. 42 brought by hand, and the laugh in the minute's silence over a tank of diesel, which is the moment Lesca first has him as a man and not as a stone. With it, Marie-Jo on the doorstep: "You don't stop speaking to a person for doing nothing. You stop when they've done something."
