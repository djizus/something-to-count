# Critic's report: Comedy (round 2)

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `23.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "It was twenty-five minutes on foot to that hatch. The bank had ten." (17) One man against forty houses and a home, stated in a sum a child can hold. It arrives at chapter 8; before that the stakes are a cracked rib and a job.
- **Tension: 6.** "That village had not been spared December 2014. It had not had it yet." (8) The best line of the first half, and it comes a third of the way in. After it the tension is a gauge reading per chapter.
- **Progression: 5.5.** "each night's alarm took the room when she had been given one thing." (10) The book states its own rationing. Chapters 9 to 16 advance by one fact a night, through the same procedure.
- **Comedy: 6.5.** "'It's the gate. There's something in the slot. In seventy-one it was a ewe.' … 'It'll have sunk,' he said." (5) The wit is exact. It has one register, and about one laugh a chapter.
- **Characters: 7.5.** "It's four sides, and it doesn't thank you." (23) Each villager is wrong in a way of their own, with a way of talking to match. The two leads are the least funny people in the book.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 7.** "'Here,' he said. 'I know you are.'" (21) Faultless there. Through the middle the switch runs on a timer: comic scene first, solemn scene last, every chapter.
- **Prose: 7.** "He said it as a man tries a door he hopes is locked." (10) Precise, and cast from a mould that shows by the tenth use.
- **The ending: 8.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (23) The man who was to drown gets into a grave of his own accord. Every running joke has been paid in the dark by then.
- **Clarity: 5.** "For three minutes she had both figures, and she could strike out neither." (2) Nor can the reader. Two villages, one cast, and some thirty running figures to keep apart.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes, but I would have stopped at chapter 2 without the eggs behind me, and again at 6. From 17 I could not stop.

**Press it on a friend?** On a friend who likes dry village comedy, yes. On the friend the bar describes, who loves The Good Place and reads Crouch in a night, no.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below.**

1. **Volume and range.** The Good Place lands a joke every twenty seconds in four or five modes: verbal, physical, visual, character rant, absurd escalation. This book has one mode, the minute-book deadpan, and uses it about once a page in the good chapters and not at all in the fifteen visits to the steel room. A Crouch reader gets no comedy from Crouch and expects none, but gets the premise by page thirty. Here the premise is named at the end of chapter 5 ("There are two of us. She is the mayor.") and becomes dangerous at the end of chapter 8.
2. **The premise is not used as a comic engine.** The Good Place takes its idea and runs every embarrassing consequence of it. This book has two versions of one woman leaking into each other's hands and mouths, and each leak is allowed one beat before she recovers her composure. The two women never share a comic scene. Their only meeting is silent.
3. **Level, and in one respect above: the joke as fuse.** The three-minute timer, "Here", the counted fingers, "I'll be brief", "took note", the car window lowered a few centimetres: each is planted as comedy and detonates as plot or grief. The Good Place's reversals reset the board at no cost. Here the funniest joke in the book (an old woman who gives the thing on her chest stars to count) is paid for with her death in room twelve. And the comedy has the right target: nobody answering for anything is what is funny in chapters 1 to 11, and it is what floods the Port in 21.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The swap farce is rationed to one beat an incident. (8, 9, 11, 14, 15)**
"'You have three minutes,' Maylis said. She heard it as it left her. She put the cap back on with her teeth, stood the pen in its stand and got up out of the chair." (11)
The keyholder sits at the mayor's desk, says one line, and the scene is over. The mayor rakes the square for half a page. The keyholder wakes with the mayor's river in her head and gets one sentence out ("I want her in my office at nine"). Chapter 14 then discovers that each can wake in the other's bed, and spends it on nine breaths looking at a lampshade. This is the largest comic asset in the book and it is never played.
*Do:* give each woman one full daylight scene in the other's village, as farce, between 14 and 16. The material is already there. Put Lesca, with eleven years of chairing behind her, into the yellow waterproof on the Tuesday of the yellow warning, counting road signs for a man who talks to her through a gap in a car window. Put Maylis, who has never given a straight answer, into the kitchen where three councillors ask the mayor to keep quiet at the stone. Then chapter 20's refusal is of something each has tasted, and the moral point (what "you're told" costs, what the sash costs) is learned in the body and is funny.

**2. Every chapter has the same shape, and comedy never gets the last word. (4 to 17)**
"The panel was there, with both lamps lit. Inguma had stayed behind on the bed. The card said what it had said." (6)
Daytime scene, daytime scene, then "went in at half past four", lamps, warm chair, card, one thing given, alarm, "wrote before she moved", an italic line. Fifteen times. The comic scene is always the warm-up and the steel room always the main act, so the reader learns that the laughs are not where the book thinks its business is. Only chapter 17 ends on a comic scene (Jeannot's numbered rant at five in the morning) and it is the best chapter ending before the flood.
*Do:* cut each visit to what is new in it. Move at least five crossings to the middle of a chapter and end those chapters in the village. Let more crossings be comically useless, as in the opening of 8: "a hall in her head, and a ham, and Marie-Jo Labat calling her by her first name. She gave the three of them one line in the notebook, since none of them explained the others."

**3. The reader is kept behind on Guy and Maïté, so the bedroom farce plays to an empty house. (13, 15, 22)**
"'He's sat at home every night since, in the kitchen, like a dog in a doorway. Nobody can get near the house. You'll keep away from it. That's not advice.'" (15)
On a second reading this is a jealous lover warning off a rival who is not one, while the café minutes the wrong affair and the husband sits up over a net. It is the best farce situation in the book. On a first reading it is a mayor being obscure, because the reader learns of the lover in chapter 22. Farce needs the audience to know who is in the wardrobe.
*Do:* let the reader know by chapter 13. The suitcase on the wardrobe is nearly enough; add what Maylis can see from the kitchen (two bowls, a coat, a car in the back lane). Keep for 22 only what belongs there: that he rang the order from that telephone, under that clock.

**4. The lead's comic flaw goes solemn after chapter 1. (1, then 9 to 23)**
"'It's twenty minutes to Bayonne,' she said." (15) Also "'I've the ponies to do'" (13) and "'Upstairs'" (15).
"Either" is a perfect comic flaw and chapter 1 plays it for a laugh with eggs. From then on the same deflection is played five times as pain, in the same two-hander with Coralie, and never again as comedy. The one other comic use is Marie-Jo's "You're still thinking about it" (7).
*Do:* let "Either" keep causing public, visible, funny trouble in the village up to the flood: at the bar, at the association, with Sarthou's statement ("by telephone" is the right idea and is over in a line). The last "What do you want, Maylis?" lands harder if the question has been funny as recently as chapter 19.

**5. The mayor's world does its comedy in summary until chapter 8. (2, 4, 6)**
"The café had spent that month deciding which of the two things it held against her more." (6)
A first-rate line, buried in a paragraph of backstory about cameras, prison and a vacant post. Chapters 2, 4 and 6 carry twelve years of history (dyke, lake, cameras, sash in a carrier bag) in narration, with the jokes inside the narration. The first live comic scene in Lesca's village is the raking in 8; the first full one is the budget in 10. A reader who came for comedy has had three even chapters with a kitchen timer and a tile.
*Do:* play one of those histories as a present scene in chapter 2 or 4, at the length and standard of the budget scene. Cut the rest to what the scene cannot show.

**6. The laugh in the minute's silence does not make the reader laugh. (14)**
"He had not offered a centime. He had gone home and written to the mairie about the ruts. / She laughed."
This is the hinge of the mayor's story: comedy itself becomes the offence. But it is the third telling of the ruts and the diesel (1, 8), and by now it raises a nod. The reader watches her laugh from outside, so the scene is about a faux pas and not about being caught enjoying a dead man.
*Do:* give her a Jeannot she has not had before, in his voice, funny enough that the reader laughs in the silence too, a line before Maïté turns round.

**7. Three wrong guesses and a ledger of figures put the reader to sums. (6, 7, 13, and throughout)**
"*She went down the track and knocked. He is alive there because of it.*" (6) "*He is dead there. She drove into him.*" (7) "*She didn't run him down. She went for him. She got to the tonne too late.*" (13)
Each is a false inference the reader must take on and then strike out, on top of forty houses, forty-two beds, forty-one letters, nineteen turns, nine strings, ninety-one players, eleven ponies, eleven plates, fifty-four bags and nineteen bags. Counting is the theme, and it has a cost: a reader keeping accounts does not laugh.
*Do:* keep one wrong guess for each woman. Keep the figures that pay (nineteen, forty-one, 02:51, forty-two) and drop or round the rest.

**8. Physical comedy is reported after the event. (9)**
"Something hit her under the right arm. It was the wheel. The mower had stopped dead with its engine labouring"
The keyholder, asleep on a ride-on mower, takes out the mayor's bollard, which Castagnet has been trying to have down for years, in front of the café. The book tells it from inside a woman who was asleep, in three sentences, and gets its laugh afterwards in dialogue ("I'll not tell him that. He'd try it"). The same happens at the stop line in 13. The book has one comic mode because it declines the others.
*Do:* play the mower pass live and long, from the square's side, as the book already does when it wants to ("What they had was a woman stopped at a line").

**9. The best routine never returns. (1)**
"'There's the question of the eggs. It isn't settled, and you're not to worry about it.' She looked at the oven shelf. 'In my tray,' she said, and shut the door on him."
Coralie doing the mayor into the oven is the warmest and funniest thing in the book and tells us why anyone would stay sixteen years. She is not allowed to play again. Her lines stay sharp ("Don't give me the measurement"), but she becomes the organ of patience, and in Lesca's village she is funny only in a 2014 flashback.
*Do:* bring the voice back once, changed, late: at the back door of the home on the Friday, or on the Sunday morning when she cannot do it. Either pays.

**10. Introductions and glosses land on the entrances. (7, and 1, 2)**
"It was Sarthou. He was the gendarme for Peyrebarthe and two communes more, out of the brigade at Urt, which is what gendarmes call their station. He was Jeannot Castagnet's cousin, and he had the last house of the Port, hard against the dyke, behind a privet hedge he kept at chest height."
This stands between a man calling "Here" for a strimmer and the same man taking the priest's microphone. The joke waits while the narrator files him. The same habit glosses the tonne twice, the mairie, the commune, the loto, the prefecture and the brigade, each in mid-scene. The duration simile ("for about as long as it takes to count a chair", "to read a number plate", "to shuffle and deal") and the "as a man who" simile are funny four times and a mould after that.
*Do:* show Sarthou at his hedge in chapter 1 or 3 so the loto can run at speed. Gloss each term once, at rest. Keep the three best of each simile.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The eggs.** Chapter 1 from "I don't cook Either" to the third ticked box.
2. **Madame Etcheto's count, and its bill.** "it had been told twice and given two lots of stars, and it got through them early and came at one" (3); "Nineteen. That's few. It counts quickly." (19); the line through the last name (21).
3. **The winch meeting and its return.** All of the dyke scene in 5, down to "resolved, by seven votes, to seek an estimate", and Jeannot at the sill in 21: "It's up a hand."
4. **"Here" and the fingers.** Monsieur Daguerre from the loto to "I know you are." Kévin's "Five", said "as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another", and "I wanted six, Maylis."
5. **"I'll be brief."** The timer cutting him off at "did you" (18), and the garden gate where he is given all the time he wants and nothing comes (22). With it, the budget carried round a sleeping chair (10) and the letter the mairie cannot un-receive (11).
