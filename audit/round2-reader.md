# Round 2: Reader

Read as an ordinary reader: no French local government, no physics, no Basque, no duck shooting. Chapters 1 to 23, in order, in full. Nothing else opened.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** Concrete and countable once they arrive, but they arrive at the end of chapter 8. "That village had not been spared December 2014. It had not had it yet." Before that line the stake is a cracked rib and a registered letter.
- **Tension: 6** (9 from chapter 17 on). For eight chapters this is what a scene resolves to: "The association resolved, by seven votes, to seek an estimate." Funny, and no reason to turn the page.
- **Progression: 6.** The alternation shows most discoveries twice. "On Monday morning the other one left the chair first, and Lesca went with her." Next chapter: "On Tuesday the other one got up first, and Maylis went with her."
- **Comedy: 8.** "There's nothing here that lets me un-receive it. There's no column."
- **Characters: 8.** "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything as much."
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Nineteen,' Madame Etcheto said. 'That's few. It counts quickly.' Nobody called."
- **Prose: 7.** At its best: "It was the sound of a lorry tipping gravel, a long way to her right, and it did not stop." At its worst it hides the noun from me (fault 7).
- **The ending: 8.** "'Forty-one,' Coralie said." The employee's ending is a 9. The mayor's is a 6 (fault 3).
- **Clarity: 5.** "Each one was a little more than one, like a photograph of somebody who has moved, a shoulder and then the same shoulder beside it." I never learned what that hall is, and it is one of a dozen things I carried unexplained.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes, but I came close to stopping in chapters 1 to 3 and again in chapter 6. From the card in chapter 4 I was curious. From the end of chapter 8 I was committed. From chapter 17 I did not get up.

**Would I press it on a friend?** Yes, with the sentence "give it four chapters." A book that needs that sentence is not yet at the bar.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, on the way in. Level or above, on the way out.**

- **Below Crouch on speed of entry.** In Dark Matter the man is taken in the first chapter and the reader knows what kind of book it is. Here I knew there were two women at the end of chapter 5, and knew what was at risk at the end of chapter 8, a third of the way through. A Crouch reader puts this down during the registered letters.
- **Below The Good Place on legible rules.** That show stops and tells me the rule, usually through someone being pedantic, and then breaks it. Here the rules of the crossing are never said by anyone. I assembled them from two notebooks, and I assembled about ten of them (fault 5).
- **Above both on what the choice costs.** Crouch's endings are chases. This one gives the same sum to two women, lets one drown a man and the other lose an old woman by trying not to, and refuses to say which was right. The private reveals in chapters 18, 21 and 22 (the call log, "Castagnet, house", the clock on the wall) made me go back to earlier chapters. That is the Good Place reversal, done three times in the last quarter.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The clock starts at chapter 8. (Structural. Chapters 1 to 8.)**
"That village had not been spared December 2014. It had not had it yet."
This is the sentence the book runs on, and it is 35 per cent in. Until then I had a monster, a card and a great many meetings. The clue is already on the page in chapter 2 ("It began to rain on the twenty-fourth of November 2014", in a chapter dated 24 November, after a chapter where it rains all day), but it is buried in a one-minute speech and I did not connect it. Maïté even says "It was raining by this date, that year" and I still read past it.
*Do:* let the reader have it by the end of chapter 2, before either woman does. End chapter 2 on the date and the rain, not on the missing meadows. Then every gauge reading in chapters 3 to 7 frightens me while the village argues about grease. Cut the mayor's wrong theory in chapter 6 (that the other one "went down the track and knocked"); it costs a chapter and is struck out in chapter 8.

**2. The secret is not marked as a secret, so chapter 17 does not reverse anything. (Structural. Chapters 2 and 17.)**
Chapter 2, in public, to a committee: "Out on the meadows there was one man. A door in a bank is opened from the platform, Guy, and I was the one on the platform. I opened it."
Chapter 17: "Nobody in that village knew that she had looked down the track first."
From chapter 2 I believed she had knowingly traded one man for forty houses and that the whole village knew it. So for fifteen chapters I did not know there was anything left to find out, and Maylis's two wrong guesses ("She drove into him", "She got to the tonne too late") read as her being slow, not as suspense. When chapter 17 showed the van in the headlamps and the single ring, I thought: yes, I knew.
*Do:* in chapter 2 make the public version plain. The village believes nobody could have known he was out; the speech already has the line ("No one came to tell him") in chapter 18, eleven chapters too late. Put it in the mouth of the mayor in chapter 2, and let one person in the room not believe it.

**3. The mayor's public ending is thrown away. (Structural. Chapter 22.)**
"That morning Lesca had woken with her door standing open on black water, and her chair empty in a hall. She stopped reading."
Chapters 16, 18 and 20 are built on whether she will stand at that stone and what she will say: three councillors in a kitchen, a tied vote, a rehearsal on a cross of tape, Coralie's "I'll know which you did." Then the speech takes eleven lines and I cannot tell what six thousand people saw. Did she stop mid-sheet? Did anyone notice? What did Larrère do under his tree? The chapter then also has to carry Sarthou's notebook, the confession, the affair, the bull, the garden gate and the pillow. It is the most overloaded chapter in the book and comes straight after the best one.
*Do:* split it. Give the stone its own chapter and show me the square when she stops. The kitchen, the ladder and the gate are right as they are and need the room.

**4. The mirror makes me read each discovery twice. (Structural. Chapters 4/5, 13/14, 14/15.)**
Both read a card in the box. Both see the wall gone and the hall. Both cross for a few breaths and are fetched back. Both resolve to stay. Inguma comes by day five times (mower, bridge, crossroads, committee, kitchen table).
*Do:* break the symmetry. When one woman has shown me a thing, the other's chapter should start after it. Of the five daytime visits, keep the crossroads, the committee and the kitchen, each of which changes what someone else knows. Cut the bridge in chapter 12; all it yields is "*A fixed lamp holds by day as well*", which nothing later needs.

**5. Too many rules, none of them said plainly, and the hall never explained. (Chapters 3 to 16.)**
"Each one was a little more than one, like a photograph of somebody who has moved."
By chapter 16 I was holding: count your fingers; read the card twice; get up at four and lie down again; hold a lamp past the alarm; the chair is warm from the other one; if she gets up while you sit, you go with her; Inguma fetches you back; Inguma comes by day "for what was not hers"; a thing gone over all day crosses whole; a wound can cross. Then the last night uses almost none of it: they stand, Inguma has the chair, one of them speaks. And I still do not know what the endless hall of doubled people is, or what sitting in its empty chair would have meant.
*Do:* cut the rules the ending does not use. Give the hall one plain sentence from someone. Kévin is the only person in the book who talks about method; let Maylis tell him what she saw and let him say what it is in café language.

**6. The mayor's past decisions are told as summary, and that is where I skimmed. (Chapters 2, 4, 6.)**
"Lesca had put it to the council as thirty-six against four, and it had carried."
The lake, the new dyke, the cameras and the festivities money are four versions of the same sum, each delivered as a paragraph of back history with dates. It is the idea of the book, and it reached me as a file.
*Do:* the Sarthou telephone call in chapter 12 ("My kitchen. Is it under the bank, or is it this side of it?") does in ten lines what the page of chapter 6 does not. Dramatise each decision once, through the person it cost, and cut the paragraph.

**7. The prose withholds the noun. (Throughout; worst in chapters 6, 15, 17.)**
"There was no van in it. Nobody in the square that morning had said the word."
"Six was two words. She had last heard them from him across a grave."
"One of you has stopped in that house, Maylis, and I don't think it's Coralie."
Which word? Which two words? Stopped what? Each of these is a place I read twice and went on without knowing. Once or twice it is style. At this rate it is a reader being kept out.
*Do:* say the word once. The chapter 22 line "It was the word the council had sat on three times and not put on the stone" works because "drowned" has just been said aloud.

**8. Too many numbers. (Chapter 1 worst; throughout.)**
"Of the forty before it, twenty-six had concerned the level of the water in the low meadows."
Forty-one letters, twenty-six about water, letter 17, nineteen turns, nine strings, ninety-one players, eighty-eight chairs, forty houses, forty-two beds, thirty-six against four, six thousand, six hundred, three thousand, four hundred a kilo, two hundred a night, eleven ponies, thirty-one members, fifty-four bags, forty-three signs, plus a gauge reading in centimetres in most chapters. Counting is the book's subject and it pays off in "Forty-one". But I cannot tell on first reading which numbers I am meant to keep.
*Do:* protect five: 02:51, nineteen turns, forty-two beds, ten minutes against twenty-five, and the letter numbers. Turn the rest into "most", "a few", "a table of them".

**9. I could not picture the gate or the tonne until late. (Chapters 1, 2, 5.)**
The tonne is "the half-buried cabin he shot from" in chapter 1, "his hide out on the meadows" in chapter 2 and "A concrete one, with a cellar" in chapter 12. I did not understand that a man in it is underground beneath a hatch, and so could be drowned without knowing, until chapter 17. The gate drains the meadows in chapter 1 and floods them in the disaster, and I had to work out why. The one clear picture ("Shut, the gate kept the river leaning on the dyke") is in chapter 2 and describes a place that is no longer there.
*Do:* one plain look from the dyke in chapter 1: river high on this side, meadow low on that, the gate between, one track, a cellar with a hatch at the end of it.

**10. "24 November" is claimed and never tested. (Chapters 16, 22.)**
"I think it was being kept on my side, and I think I've let it across."
The mayor comes to believe her night carried its rain into the other village. If true, she caused the flood and the death, which is the largest thing in the book. Nothing confirms or denies it; the priest sidesteps, she writes "Delivered", and it is not mentioned again. I do not know whether the book believes her.
*Do:* decide. Either give it one consequence she has to say aloud to someone, or take it out and let the five words be what she delivered.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 21, all of it.** The clock times, the van in the dip, the key thrown back along the crown, Jeannot's three points about the winch in the middle of a flood, Kévin counting five, room twelve, the list.
2. **Madame Etcheto giving Inguma things to count, and Monsieur Daguerre's "Here".** The care plan in chapter 3 and the sandbags in chapter 19 are the same joke, and the second time it is the saddest page in the book.
3. **The meeting across the chair.** Two women, one open palm, five words, no conversation. Any more said and it becomes a different, worse book.
4. **Coralie.** "Eggs is what Either gets in this house", the boxes on the shopping pad, the pillow, and in chapter 18 "Because you'd have told me."
5. **The things that are set up as jokes and paid as blows.** Larrère's window lowered "a few centimetres", then a garden gate with "no glass in it" and "Nothing came." The timer that cuts him off at "did you". Letter No. 42 brought by hand, and the second shovel.

**What I will remember in a month:** a thing made of leaves and rivets that only ever shows its back. An old woman telling it to count the stars. A deaf man calling "Here". A mayor who will not put his car window down. Flowers for a widow whose husband is in the café. A boy in flood water counting his fingers and getting five. "Forty-one."

**What I will not remember:** anything about the lake lease, the Hontaube merger, the festivities budget, or what the rules of the chair were.
