# Round 2: Thriller lens

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `23.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else opened but the brief.

Verdict against the bar: below Crouch as a thriller. One chapter (21) is at his level. The book around it is slower to frighten and gentler in what it charges than any of the three Crouch titles.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." (2) The sum is exact from the second chapter. It stays a figure until chapter 20 turns it into rooms, which is eighteen chapters late.
- **Tension: 6.** "She meant to be awake when the telephone rang, with it in her hand, and to have the engine started before the voice had finished her name." (19) The heroine knows the hour and waits for it. Before chapter 8 the reader is afraid of nothing but a rib.
- **Progression: 7.** "That village had not been spared December 2014. It had not had it yet." (8) The rules of the crossing arrive one at a time and in the right order. The sentence that starts the clock comes in chapter 8 of 23.
- **Comedy: 7.** "The association resolved, by seven votes, to seek an estimate." (5) The comedy is procedure, and the procedure is what later kills. The moral problem itself is never the joke.
- **Characters: 8.** "'I don't cook Either,' she said. 'There's no recipe for it. Soup or duck.'" (1) Coralie is the reason to fear for anything in this book.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." (21) Kévin counting five fingers in floodwater is the book's best joke and its worst moment in one line.
- **Prose: 7.** "It was not loud. It was the sound of a lorry tipping gravel, a long way to her right, and it did not stop." (21) That exactness is the norm. Marked down for a dozen similes of the form "as a man…" and for dated back-history dropped into the middle of scenes.
- **The ending: 6.** "She read it as typed." (22) Chapter 21 is a 9. The chapter after it skips the scene the book has pointed at since chapter 2, and the mayor ends better off than she began.
- **Clarity: 6.** "Six was two words. She had last heard them from him across a grave." (17) I never learned the two words. The same withholding hides the cause of the second flood (fault 7).

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. From chapter 8 without effort; chapters 3 to 7 on credit from chapter 2.

**Press it on a friend?** On a friend who reads slowly, yes. On a Crouch reader, no. I would have to say "nothing is at risk until chapter 8", and that sentence loses them.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below.**

1. **Speed of the fear.** In Dark Matter the husband is in the wrong world, and the reader afraid for the wife, inside the first tenth. Recursion has its first death in chapter 1. Here the reader knows there are two villages on the first page of chapter 2, which is fast. The women take until the end of 5 to say so, and the danger (the flood is coming to the other village) is not stated until the last line of 8. For seven chapters the premise is a puzzle, not a threat.
2. **Chapter ends.** Crouch cuts on an act or a reversal. Here six chapters end on a line of her own handwriting (4, 5, 7, 9, 14, 16) and two more within a paragraph of one (6, 13). Three of those lines are guesses the reader can already correct. The ends that work are acts or questions: "On her left the rubber seal of the door gave" (10), "What have you been turning, Maylis?" (11), "Forty-one" (21).
3. **The marriage.** Crouch frightens you for a marriage by making the double want the wife. Neither woman here wants the other's Coralie, though one Coralie still shares the bed and the other has slept across a landing for eleven years. The fear for the couple is an envelope to Bayonne, which has nothing to do with the premise.

**Level or above, on two things.** The flood in 21 is as good as a Crouch set piece and better built, since every running joke in the book is an instrument in it. The reversals re-read what came before them as The Good Place's do: Coralie's "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one" re-reads eleven years of landing, and the kitchen clock re-reads every car window. None of them changes the premise, as that show's first-season finale does. They are secrets, not turns.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The clock starts in chapter 8.** Chapters 1 to 8. "That village had not been spared December 2014. It had not had it yet."
This sentence makes the book a thriller, and it arrives a third of the way in. Until then Maylis risks a rib and a job. The gauge is read in 3 ("a metre sixty under her father's red line") and 5 with nothing to tell the reader it is a countdown. Lesca has the means in chapter 2: she wakes to "the sound of rain on a roof" and the same afternoon tells Maïté the 2014 rain began "From the twenty-fourth. It's in the file." She does not connect them for six chapters. A Crouch reader stops in chapter 3.
*Do:* bring the recognition forward to the end of 4 or 6. Or put the reader ahead of both women by chapter 3, with one gauge reading that audibly matches a figure from Lesca's one-minute speech. The loto in 7 is a different scene once the reader knows the twelve at the radiator table sleep under that bank.

**2. The fight for the chair is promised for seven chapters and never happens.** Chapters 14 to 21. "The hand would go to whichever held on longer." (14) / "Maylis stepped back from the chair, and left it to Inguma." (21)
Each woman resolves to take the other's life ("*That the mayor remain seated*", "*I mean to stay in the chair. That is where the sash is*"). Each is then talked out of it before the night: Lesca by her own two columns in 16 ("She could not go there to forget"), Maylis by the vision in 17. By 02:51 neither wants it, so the meeting is two people declining. The most frightening idea in the book, two wills in one bandaged hand at a winch, is described twice and never staged.
*Do:* let one of them sit. Have Lesca hold through the first ring, so that Maylis wakes in the van with a second will in her right hand, one reaching for the handle box and one for the ignition. Thirty contested seconds at 02:53 would do it. Lesca's letting go then becomes the act, and the five words become what she says as she does it. If the renunciation must stay as written, cut the deflations in 16 and 17 so that both still want the chair when they see each other.

**3. Maylis sits ten hours at the gate with the whole night in her head.** Chapter 19. "She had the other one's night in her head from the first ring to the cellar, and she knew what she would hear."
She is on the dyke at five. Jeannot stops beside her van, then goes down the track. From then until 02:53 the tonne is twenty-five minutes away with no clock running, and she does not walk to it. She waits for a call she knows is coming, then tries to do in eight minutes what she had all evening for. Chapter 23 does not see it either: her own accounting starts "At five to three I had my hand on that bank." A thriller reader spends 19 shouting at her.
*Do:* close the window on the page. Either she goes down at dusk and he shuts the hatch on her ("Don't knock either" is already his line), or she tries the winch early against orders and it stops at nineteen, which makes the oldest gag in the book the reason the choice still exists at three. If her obedience is the point, say so. Marie-Jo's "The rest I've told you, and you've done it" is waiting to be used.

**4. The commemoration is skipped, and Lesca pays in private.** Chapter 22. "She read it as typed." / "Maïté laid the wreath. She was not kissed."
Since chapter 2 the mayor's strand points at Saturday at the stone: the prefect, six thousand people, a camera, the tape cross, a whole chapter (18) on whether she may open her mouth, Marie-Jo's "You'll do it at the stone next", Coralie's "I'll know which you did". The ceremony then takes about twenty lines. She reads the evasion as written, Inguma does not come, and "She stopped reading" is not followed up. The confession moves to a kitchen with one witness. She keeps the medal, the office and the speech. The same night Inguma is gone and the pillow comes back across the landing. The bill for her five words is paid in another village by a woman she wheeled past alive that morning.
*Do:* play the ceremony at full length, with the choice at the third sheet made at the microphone. Then take something from her: the line said aloud, the sash, or the pillow held back a night. One of the two rewards in the last scene has to wait.

**5. Each crossing is told twice, and the women discover what the reader already has.** Chapters 4/5, 6/7, 13/14, 14/15, 20/21. "*There are two of us. She is the mayor.*" (5)
The reader has known this since the first page of 2. The card in the box is staged in 4 and again in 5. The medal is planned in 6 and received in 7. The endless hall is in 13 and 14. "The other one got up first" opens 14 and 15. Maylis's three wrong readings (7 "*She drove into him*", 9 "*It never came here*", 13 "*She got to the tonne too late*") are no suspense, because chapter 2 gave the answer.
*Do:* show each crossing once, from the side that learns most, and give the other side a line. Let Maylis's wrong guesses be about the one thing the reader does not know either, which is the call at 02:51.

**6. The last section of every chapter is the same section.** Chapters 4 to 13. "Maylis went in at twenty-five past four." / "The alarm went at six." / "She wrote before she moved."
Nine of the ten chapters from 4 to 13 close with: lie down, go in, be given one thing, alarm, note. The thriller is rationed to the last page and an alarm clock ends it each time. By chapter 7 the reader has learned to skim the day to reach it. The crossings that happen in daylight are tenser than any of these: the signature in front of the courier (5), the mower into the bollard (9), the red light with a loaded lorry behind (12), the tractor at the stop line (13).
*Do:* move at least four of the night revelations into the day, as bleed with witnesses. Break the alarm.

**7. The cause of the second flood is said sideways, down a telephone.** Chapter 16. "I think it was being kept on my side, and I think I've let it across."
This is the book's largest claim: the mayor's twelve bad years have exported her flood to another village. It makes her answerable for everything in 19 to 21. She sees the dates line up in 8 and "wrote nothing under them". It surfaces eight chapters later as a hedge to a priest, who answers with a joke about baptism, and returns as one line in a third column. Maylis never learns it. A reader can finish without registering that Lesca made it rain.
*Do:* make it a numbered finding in 8 or 10, as blunt as the four in 14. Let it drive the plan to cross: she is going over to stop what she started, not only to save a man.

**8. No one is dangerous until the last twenty pages.** Chapters 1 to 22. "'I'll be brief,' he said. […] Nothing came." (22)
Larrère is a comic obstacle behind a window for twenty-one chapters. The kitchen clock is well planted, but nothing in the book makes the reader afraid of him, and his exposure costs nobody anything: Maïté hands it over, and Sarthou reads it off a screen. It is not clear what he loses if the gate opens, so "shut" reads as habit.
*Do:* give his order a stake the reader can see by chapter 9 (the unmetered pump, the staff file, a threat he can carry out), and let him use it on Maylis on Friday.

**9. Chapter 18 runs the clock backwards and ends on a test firework.** Chapters 17/18. "It lit at the first match."
Chapter 17 ends on Friday at five in the morning with Jeannot on the line. Chapter 18 opens "On Thursday". At the steepest part of the climb the book steps back a day, and the last chapter before the night ends on a pyrotechnician's matches.
*Do:* end 18 on the bedroom: "I'll know which you did", or the bare wardrobe. Move the bull test earlier or cut it to a line.

**10. Scenes stop for dated history and glosses.** Throughout, heaviest in 2, 4 and 6. "In 2016 the State had offered to rebuild the Port's defences at its own cost, on its own line." (6)
Most scenes in the mayor's strand open on, or are interrupted by, a pluperfect paragraph of years and resolutions. Terms are glossed in mid-line ("the brigade at Urt, which is what gendarmes call their station"). The facts are needed. Their placement halts the scene each time.
*Do:* put the history in mouths that are arguing, as with Sarthou's "You've not asked me for anything since you asked for my house." Gloss once, in chapter 1, and trust it.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 21, whole.** The timestamps, the van in the dip, "She had given it ten minutes. It had held sixteen", the list on its cord, Kévin's "Five", the closing count.
2. **The first page of chapter 2.** The repeated opening sentence, and "Jeannot Castagnet had sent the mairie twelve. He had been dead for twelve years less eighteen days."
3. **The winch that stops at the nineteenth turn.** From the cracked rib in 1, through seven men on a platform in 5, to "It's up a hand. A hand" in 21.
4. **Coralie's 02:51 in chapter 18.** "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." And her reason for never asking: "Because you'd have told me."
5. **The last page.** Letter No. 42 delivered by hand, and Jeannot stepping over the string into the grave.
