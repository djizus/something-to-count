# Critic report — lens: Characters

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

Overall against the bar: **7.3**. Chapters 11 to 13 and most of 16 stand at the bar. Chapters 1 to 5 do not, and the central pair is weaker than the people around it.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes — 7.** The one that makes a reader afraid arrives halfway: "On the night the river came through the bank, Coralie would be at the care home, right under it." (8). Before that the stakes are a rib, a winch and a wage.
- **Tension — 7.5.** At the bar from 11 to 13: "She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it broke." (13). Slack in 3, 5 and the first half of 8.
- **Progression — 6.** The same discovery is made twice, from each side, in consecutive chapters: Maylis reads "*right*" in the steel room (3), then Lesca reads "*left*" and reasons it out again (4): "If she were dreaming the card, the word would have changed."
- **Comedy — 7.5.** "Eggs, then. Eggs is what Either gets in this house." (1). "Eleven years I've been timed like an egg." (7).
- **Characters — 7.5.** "I know what you picked. I was the other one." (16). The minor cast is at 8.5, the central pair at 6.5.
- **The switch between dark and comic — 8.** In the flooded yard, mid-lift: "'Five,' he said. He looked at the hand a moment longer. Then he put it under the woman's knees and lifted her in." (13). The running joke becomes the worst line in the book without changing a word of its form.
- **Prose — 7.** At its best exact and unshowy: "The driver looked at the coins and at the suitcase, and let her on." (16). At its worst it labels: "It hurt to hear." (6).
- **The ending — 7.5.** "Then he stepped over the string in his waders and began to dig." (16) is right. The steel-room scene that opens the same chapter hands out the verdict before the reader can reach it.
- **Clarity — 6.5.** The rules are patched as needed: "By the first rule of the chair, whatever one of them had in her head came to the chair, even when she was not sitting in it. So Maylis was given the whole of that night." (9).

**Finished unpaid?** Yes, from chapter 6. In chapters 2 to 5 I was reading on credit from the prologue.
**Press it on a friend?** On the friend who loves a village comedy, yes. On the Crouch reader, no: they would stop in chapter 3.

## 2. Against the comparisons (Characters)

**Against Crouch: above in the minor cast, below in the doubles.**

- Above. Nobody in Dark Matter or Recursion outside the central couple is as alive as Jeannot Castagnet ("And six. Bugger off."), Maïté with her fare "ready in her glove, in coins, and it was not enough", or Kévin ("I wanted six, Maylis."). Crouch's secondary people are functions. These are people.
- Below. Dark Matter makes the other Jason a threat by the end of its first act because the reader has already sat in the kitchen he is stealing. Lesca steals the same thing, a bed and the woman in it, and it carries half the menace, because the reader has never seen what she lost (fault 3) and because the theft is played as committee farce and never weighed (fault 4).
- Level. Neither woman is ever described from outside as a woman. They are known by a tall L, a t crossed low, a scar across a palm, a forearm held across a rib. That is better practice than Crouch's and it should stay exactly as it is.

**Against The Good Place: level on the individual flaw, below on the pair and on the antagonist.**

- Level. Maylis's "Either" is Chidi's stomach-ache, a real moral defect that is funny on a shopping pad and lethal on a phone ("'Both.'" 13). Lesca's sum is its opposite and equally wrong. Each is wrong in her own way, and the body-sharing turns the two wrongs into physical farce (the spoon tipped back into the bowl, the t crossed low, the hand that rings the timer).
- Below. The Good Place's people change each other in every scene they share. Maylis and Lesca share four scenes in sixteen chapters, all four run as meetings (fault 2).
- Below. Michael wants things. Guy Larrère has a window, a folder and a catchphrase (fault 6).

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The hinge is not hers. (11, set up in 8) — structural.**
"Now it had Kévin Labat's name on it, and if the gate moved, the sheet went to the prosecutor at Bayonne. [...] 'Either,' Maylis said."
Since chapter 1 her defect has been fear of owning a choice: "if she said the pawl, and the pawl failed with the river up, it would be her pawl" (3). At the one moment the book exists for, daylight, the key on her belt, the man standing on the bank, the text gives her a hostage and a noble reason. Chapter 8 has already proved she can say the word: "'Open,' she said again. 'Friday, at two.'" and "She could give up her own job, but she couldn't give up Kévin." So at five o'clock she is not failing; she is being blackmailed, and protecting a boy. That excuses her. It also makes six months for eels outweigh forty-two beds with Coralie among them, which no reader will accept as her honest sum. The book then asks the Port to condemn her ("You had the key") for something the reader has been told to forgive.
Do: keep the folder as pressure, but take it out of the causal sentence. Remove Larrère's car from the bank in that scene. Let Jeannot's direct question, "Shut, or open?", get the reflex she gave six men on the same planks, with nothing to hide behind. Jeannot says "Then it's shut" and goes down before she can take it back. Then rewrite her line in 16 ("I kept that gate shut for a boy's name on a sheet of paper") so that she refuses that excuse herself.

**2. The two women never have it out. (5, 8, 12, 16) — structural.**
"'Any other business?' Lesca said. 'No.'" (16)
The relationship in the title is conducted as minutes: three points, items, a tie the chair decides. It is funny in 5 and it is still the form in 16, when one of them has killed a man and the other a woman. The room has a rule made for a confrontation, "When we're on our feet we can talk, and neither of us can lie" (5), and it is used once ("I can't stand here and say I'll go"). What Maylis feels about Lesca's life is real on the page, "She liked it, and she slowed down to get more of it" (7), then it is turned into a fee, "For the day in your chair I want the timer", and cancelled offstage: "She was ashamed of that now." (9). She never says it to the only person who would understand it. Lesca never tells Maylis what the soup was. Neither says what it is to look at her own face having done better or worse. The only charged exchange is three words, "You rang once" (12), and it shows what the rest lacks.
Do: one steel-room scene, at the end of 8 or in 16, where the meeting form breaks and the no-lying rule is used against each of them in turn. Maylis has to say she wanted the sash and the square; Lesca has to say what she was going to do at the top of the stairs. Cut "Any other business?".

**3. Lesca's loss is a subordinate clause. (2, 4) — structural.**
"She had gone over there with her pillow one night in the February after the flood, after a week of shouting, and the pillow had stayed." (4)
That sentence is all the reader has of eleven years before Lesca writes "*and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it*". Maylis's household gets the eggs, the oven and the bandage in chapter 1, which is how Crouch does it: the kitchen first, then the theft. Lesca's Coralie is absent from chapter 2 entirely and gets a doorway in chapter 4. So the motion reads as a plot turn, and "She had crossed for that sound, and for a bed with two pillows on it" (12) asks the reader to feel a want it was only told about. The scene that would do it exists, the phone log in 10, but it comes after the theft has been attempted twice.
Do: put the mayor's house in chapter 2. One evening: two doors, a landing, Coralie giving an order about soup and being answered back, the light under the spare-room door. Keep the phone log where it is.

**4. The stairs are never weighed, and Lesca is paid anyway. (6, 15)**
"I went to her door, and I had never come to yours. I'm asking you." (15)
In 6 Lesca walks another woman's body up to that woman's partner while its owner hangs on to the stair post with a cut hand. The Coralie over there names it exactly: "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." (8). After that nobody does. Lesca presents it to her own Coralie as a credential, and it is received as comedy: "'Twice in a week,' Coralie said. 'It's four steps from your door to mine, Madame le maire, and they've taken you eleven years.'" Maylis's whole reckoning with it in 16 is "'Two minutes, because you wanted my bed.' 'Yes.'" The book audits the gate to the centimetre and lets the body go. Lesca then gets the pillow on the last line of 15. She is the softened one.
Do: the Coralie of 15 is the same woman who has lifted too many of them. Let her hear what "with the other one's mouth" means and react to that before she makes the joke about four steps. Put the stairs on Maylis's agenda in 16. The pillow can still come back; it should come back to a woman who has been told what she tried.

**5. The verdict is handed down, and the guilt is shared out until nobody holds it. (16)**
"A woman is dead because of that, and it still isn't what I did."
The chapter opens: "Maylis wanted to be told that it was her fault, and only one person knew enough to say so." That person then tells her she is the better woman. Around it, everyone claims a slice: "Those two minutes are mine." "The van in the dip was mine, and so was saying both." "That part is mine." Five people take a share of room twelve inside one chapter. The effect is a comfort the dead woman's corridor did not offer. Madame Darrigrand on her doorstep does the job in six lines and does it better: "At three Sarthou carried her out over his head, and you were a kilometre away, fetching a man out of a dry hole."
Do: cut Lesca's speech from "No. You won't sign it" to "behind it as well" down to its refusal. She can decline to let Maylis sign the sum; she cannot acquit her. Cut "I don't know what they would have bought." Keep Coralie's share, which is character, and Darrigrand's, which is the verdict.

**6. Larrère is a mechanism. (1, 3, 6, 7, 8, 11, 15)**
"A gate is a door in a bank. It is not a tap." (2, 7, 7 again, and "You don't open it because the house is full" in 11)
He is the antagonist in both villages and the reader is never told one thing he wants. He has the window gap, "I'll be brief", the folder. His affair is a car in a lane with its lights off. Yet he holds the strongest argument in the book and never makes it: Lesca has to say it for him, "His order was to keep the gate shut. If it had stayed shut, your husband would have stayed dry." (15). His one human moment is given to silence: "He said nothing."
Do: give him the argument, in his own mouth, at the council in 7, in place of the third "door in a bank". Give him one sentence about Maïté that is not about a file. A man who is right about the dead man and wrong about the bank is a Good Place character. A man who only obstructs is furniture.

**7. Maylis has no mother. (11)**
"The light was going, and her mother's ponies had gone up onto the higher ground."
Arlette lives across the yard (4) and is one of the best voices in the book: "I'm not a visitor. I'm her mother." (10). "I'm not asking you why. Not once." (14). In Maylis's village she is six words. She has ponies on the meadows her daughter means to flood, she lives across the yard from a daughter who loses her job, her partner and her name in one weekend, and she never opens her mouth. It leaves Maylis with a thinner world than Lesca's (Lesca has a mother, a Coralie, a Marie-Jo, a Maïté, a council) and makes the lesser woman look lesser by neglect. It also wastes the only place where the reader could see what Lesca's lease took.
Do: one scene in 8 or 11. The other Arlette, with her ponies still on the common, saying what she thinks of a daughter who writes "Either".

**8. Until chapter 7 Maylis only refuses. (1 to 6)**
"Somebody had to tell the village, and she would rather it was the one who signed things." (5)
Her acts in six chapters: she writes Either, turns her back on Kévin's van, says Either to six men, ticks both boxes, carries a pot of flowers, tips a spoon, holds a post. Lesca deduces, crosses, plans, warns, flirts and drives. That is the design, and its cost is that the reader, like Coralie, prefers the other one ("I'd have this one again") for the whole first half. Maylis's first chosen act is blackmailing Jeannot in 7, and from there she is the book.
Do: move one act of hers forward. The envelope in 3 is the place: she opens it to test a dream; let her do something with what she finds that Lesca would not have done.

**9. Everyone speaks in the same capped epigram. (throughout, worst in 14 to 16)**
"'It's four when you're the one counting,' he said. [...] 'When it's your house it's one, and they knock it down.'" (4). "I know that word. I've cooked it." (16). "'I wasn't giving,' Maïté said. 'I was telling you whose kitchen it was.'" (15). "Don't give me the measurement. I could. I'm not going to." (16). "She's got sixty chairs to do. She isn't leaving the area." (14).
A statement, then a turn that closes it. Coralie, Maïté, Arlette, Marie-Jo, Darrigrand and both Lescas all do it. Each has a handle (Jeannot numbers, Sarthou says "I shall", Marie-Jo talks to the wall), but under the handle it is one voice, and it is nobody's in the village.
Do: let two of them fail to find the line. Maïté in her kitchen in 15 is the candidate: twelve years of silence should not come out finished.

**10. The page shows the feeling and then names it. (throughout)**
"It hurt to hear." (6). "She found that she wanted the pillow more." (15). "She was ashamed of that now." (9). "Maylis wanted to be able to do that. She didn't want it done for her." (5).
The stated want is the right device for Maylis in chapter 1, where the narrator says what she will not ("Maylis wanted the duck"). It becomes a tic when it is applied to Lesca and to reactions the scene has already delivered. In 6 the spoon is tipped back into the bowl twice; the reader does not need "It hurt to hear" three lines earlier.
Do: keep the stated want only where Maylis is suppressing it in speech. Cut it everywhere a hand has already done the work.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie's oven.** "'In my tray,' she said, and shut the door on him." (1), and its return: "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" (16).
2. **Coralie on the landing.** "I'd have the one who answers. [...] I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." (8).
3. **Room twelve, both times.** The two of them shaking against the corridor wall (3), and "There was a hatbox on it, and nothing else." (13).
4. **Jeannot's two answers.** The nine rings and "And six. Bugger off." (9), and the letter: "It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you." (16).
5. **Kévin's fingers.** "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much." (16).
