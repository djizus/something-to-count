Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. From chapter 11 I could not have stopped.

Would I press it on a friend? Yes, on a friend who watched The Good Place for the ethics. To one who watched it for the jokes I would hand chapters 1 to 8.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below The Good Place as comedy, by one point. Level with it on the switch. Above Crouch, who does not attempt comedy.**

What puts it there:

**Level: the flaw is the joke is the catastrophe.** Maylis is Chidi. The Good Place makes his indecision funny (the almond milk, the stomach ache) and then shows it as the thing that ruined everyone near him. This book does the same with one word and does it more rigorously. "Either" costs a dinner (1), then a repair (3), then Coralie's question (5), then the only jaw-strength she has (6), then a police file with one name in it (7), then the gate (11). When she finally refuses it and says "Both", it kills a woman, and Coralie names it: "That was Either, with the river in it." Every running gag in the book is paid this way, as plot and then as grief: "I'll be brief", the gap in the window, "Here", the five fingers, the boxes. The Good Place does not pay its running jokes that completely.

**Below: the moral problem is funny at its edges and solemn at its centre.** The Good Place did this exact dilemma. In its trolley episode the problem is argued, then driven, then splattered, and the argument itself is the comedy. Here the circumstances of the dilemma are funny: the verb for the stone ("It reads that she opened him"), the motion to take the other woman's bed, the form that "needs a third box". But when the two women finally argue the ethics, in the first scene of 16, the book puts its comic idiom away. See fault 2.

**Below: the book stops making comic scenes at chapter 8.** The Good Place is still landing jokes in its last ten minutes. Here the three comic engines are the café, the council table and two women fighting over one body. All three run for the last time in 8. After that the book cashes callbacks. It cashes them superbly, but a callback is a withdrawal, and nothing is paid in after the midpoint.

**Below: one register, and a narrator who explains.** Every joke is dry, procedural understatement. Only Coralie is funny on purpose and only Kévin is light. The Good Place has five registers and never annotates a callback. This narrator annotates most of them. See fault 7.

**Below: the reversals re-read the book, but none of them is funny.** Three do real work. Coralie has known the minute for twelve years (10), which re-reads the landing and every "Madame le maire". Nine minutes to three is the time of the call (9), which re-reads the first page. The kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice (9, 13, 15) re-reads every word he says about the gate. All three are delivered grave. The Good Place's great reversal arrives as a laugh.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. The café leaves the page at chapter 8, and chapter 16 reports the village's verdict second-hand, twice (16, structural)

> "You'll hear it at the café, so you can have it from me," she said. "For twelve years, when a man came in late, they told him he was still in the box. I've said it to you myself, in this hall. Last night a man came in late, and they told him he'd had the key and gone for a walk."

And forty lines earlier, from Madame Darrigrand: "I'd rather you heard it from me than from the café."

The café is the funniest room in the book. Chapter 6 is the best comic chapter because of it: the pad, "It takes a small m", Josiane's theory, "Which do I put?". Its last scene is the association meeting in 8. In the back half Lafitte is a man at an upstairs window in somebody else's report, and the village's judgment on Maylis reaches her through two women who each announce they are saving her from hearing it there. The book retires its oldest joke ("Still in the box, were you?") and replaces it with a crueller one, and it does this offstage. It is the best dark joke in the manuscript and nobody says it.

What I would do: put her in the café on that Sunday. The book has already set it up: on Sundays the café sells bread from a crate on the counter (5). She goes in for bread. A man comes in behind her, late. The dealer says the new line without looking up from the cards. Then he looks up. Lafitte has the pad. Jeannot's stool is empty, because he is at home writing forty-two. Josiane sells her the bread. Cut Marie-Jo's report to the line "I was in this hall at five on Saturday morning with the urn on", and keep Madame Darrigrand, who is saying something different. This gives the back half the one full comic scene it lacks, and it makes the village's verdict funny without making it small.

### 2. The last argument between the two women drops the form the book built for it (16, structural)

> "So you were right, that night. Turn the handle first, and everyone behind the bank lives. The houses, the care home, the one in room twelve. I've been in that room now, and I'd sign your sum."
> "No. You won't sign it. I was right about the houses, and I can show you that in a file, to the centimetre."

This is the book's ethical resolution, and it is two speeches of accounting. In chapter 5 the same two women in the same room argued as a meeting, and the form was the joke: "Then it's carried, or it's a tie, and in a tie the chair decides." / "There's nobody in the chair." By 16 all that is left of it is "Any other business?", one line from the end. That line proves the idiom was available.

The scene is already a comic situation that has not been allowed to be one. Two versions of one woman are competing for the blame. Each claims her share by the minute ("Those two minutes are mine", "The van in the dip was mine, and so was saying both"). One prices it like a job and the other minutes it.

What I would do: run it as the last meeting. Maylis moves that it was her fault. Lesca rules the motion out of order, because the mover never had a five o'clock of her own to compare. Maylis prices her share the way she prices verges. The vote is one against one with an empty chair that, for once, neither of them wants. Keep every admission and every figure exactly as written, including "I've hidden behind that sum for twelve years, and I'm not having you behind it as well." The procedure should fail to hold what they are saying. That failure is the scene.

### 3. Bingo night, which five later scenes stand on, is thirty lines and never calls a number (8, structural)

> "At eight that evening it was bingo night in the village hall, and the keyholder was serving at the bar."

The dead of Peyrebarthe play bingo. That conceit carries the hall of the dead (8, 12), the sandbags counted to Monsieur Daguerre's "Here" (11), the trestle tables and the urn at "Forty-one" (13), and "Twelve. One, two." (14). The living bingo night it copies is a summary between a timed walk and a scene on a landing. No number is called on the page. The caller's manner, "first whole and then in parts", is first heard among the dead in 12. Coralie's line in 11, "He's been one number short of the grass trimmer ever since bingo night", refers to something the reader was never shown.

Chapter 8 has eleven scenes: the walk, bingo, the landing, the letter, the kitchen table, the shift change, the yellow warning, the petition, the vote, the handover note, the steel room. Its comic set-pieces are cut to their punchlines. The best throwaway in it is told after the fact: "Lesca had given Inguma eleven years of her signatures to count, and it had counted them."

What I would do: split 8 after the letter to the prefect. Give bingo a full scene. The caller calls each number whole and then in parts. Monsieur Daguerre calls "Here" and misses the grass trimmer by one. The duck is won on the page. And the caller calls "Twelve. One, two." in life, so that in 14 the same words are a callback and the reader knows whose room it is before Coralie says so. Pay for the length by cutting the rule recaps (fault 6).

### 4. The pillow comes back in one reported sentence, and Coralie says nothing (15, structural)

> "That night Lesca was in the bedroom when Coralie carried her pillow back across the landing and laid it on the wall side of the bed."

Lesca crossed between worlds for this. The motion was written for it. Coralie has refused it three times in this chapter alone, with lines as good as any in the book ("I'm not carrying it in to the mayor"). Then it happens in a subordinate clause, and the funniest speaker in the manuscript is given no line. The chapter is five short quiet scenes, each ending on a silence, and the fifth silence is the payoff.

What I would do: give it a scene of ten lines and give Coralie the last word. She has called Lesca "Madame le maire" in every scene for sixteen chapters as a way of keeping her off. The vote has just taken the title away. What she calls her now is the payoff, and it is owed on the page.

### 5. The false affair is set and never sprung (6, 7, 11, structural)

> "A woman takes flowers to the wife because she's after the husband or because she's after the wife. It's adultery either way, and that one lives with a nurse."

This is a proper farce engine. The village suspects the wrong affair while the real one is parked in the back lane with its lights off. It is funny in 6 and funnier in 7 ("Josiane had got there first"). Then Larrère uses it once as a threat in 11 and it is gone. It never reaches the one person it would hurt. In 6 Coralie reports what the care home has heard ("By ten the care home knew you'd given Larrère three minutes") and what the café has said ("you've got a mayor in you"), and does not mention that her partner of sixteen years is said to be after a Castagnet.

What I would do: one exchange at the soup in 6. Coralie reports the care home's version and asks which Castagnet. Lesca answers with Maylis's mouth, and answers truthfully, and it is the wrong answer. That costs eight lines and gives the false belief a victim. It also sharpens Jeannot's "You were told the way to my kitchen as well" in 11, which at present has to carry the whole suspicion alone.

### 6. The rules are given as a speech and then given again as narration before each scene that uses them (5, 6, 7, 8)

> "It was the same as on Saturday. Lesca had stayed sitting in the chair, so she had come home inside Maylis's body, and she controlled nearly all of it."

That opens the funniest chapter in the book, and three paragraphs of it pass before anyone speaks. The reader had this one chapter earlier, from Lesca, as point Two of three, and Maylis had already compressed it into the only version anyone needs: "So the one who stays sitting rides the one who wakes." The rule is stated in 4, twice in 5, in 6, in 7 and in 8.

Lesca's three points in 5 are themselves a lump: three long paragraphs with one interruption. The comedy of an agenda is in the points of order.

What I would do: in 5, have Maylis break into each point with a practical question, the way she works out the price of a job, so that the rules arrive as an exchange. Then open 6 on "What time does the director of the care home get in?" and let "In your what?" do the explaining. Move the one new fact, that Maylis can take the jaw for a word, to the moment she takes it.

### 7. The narrator explains the callback (throughout; worst in 13, 15, 16)

> "It buzzed twice against his chest, the way it had buzzed on the dashboard of a tractor under the plane trees." (13)

The reader who has got this far remembers the tractor. Kévin spreading his fingers in his own headlights needs no caption, and the caption arrives a line before the gesture and spends it. The same habit elsewhere:

- "She gave him his three minutes and more, and no timer rang." (15)
- "he took the handles out of her hands without asking, the same way he had once climbed into her tractor bucket." (13)
- "This time the mayor had no car around him." (16)
- "The last time six men had turned to hear what she wanted, on the bank, she had said Either." (8)
- "This was what Lesca gave Guy Larrère his three minutes with." (7)
- "It was the picture she had woken up with a week ago, a crowd waiting for her to begin." (7)
- "The chair was Lesca, and her name was on the stone." (2)
- "It hurt to hear." (6)
- "He meant the medal." (14)

Each of these gags has been planted at least three times by the point where it is explained. The book's best payoffs are the ones with no caption: Coralie bending to the oven in 16, and Maylis feeding her the line about Larrère's car to get her to do it.

What I would do: cut all ten. If any payoff does not survive the cut, the plant is too far back, and the answer is one more plant. Kévin is the case: he is absent from Maylis's village from 3 to 11. One beat of him counting his fingers in 8 would let the caption in 13 go.

### 8. The book's best comic invention arrives as the last clause of a rules speech, and nobody reacts (3)

> "After that she plays bingo with the rest of the dead, a card of numbers each and maize to mark it, like bingo night in the village hall."

Thirty lines earlier Coralie had her forehead against the wall, helpless, over a care plan. Now a woman of ninety-four tells her that the dead play bingo, and her next line is "Me? When has it ever sat on me?" The speech also explains itself: Madame Etcheto would not say "like bingo night in the village hall" to two women from the village.

What I would do: break the speech before that sentence. Give the bingo its own line, cut the simile, and give Coralie one question about it, asked straight and answered straight.

### 9. The chapter's last line is given away two scenes early (4)

> "The rest had a bed in it, and a name."

The line it is pointing at is the funniest dark sentence in Lesca's half: "*That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*" The reader is told its two nouns sixty lines before reading it.

What I would do: cut the sentence. "She changed the full stop to a comma and went on in the same ink to the end of the line" is all the tease it needs.

### 10. The one place a joke comes too soon after a dark beat (12)

> "'I saw your van,' she said. 'I rang once.'
> He put a kernel on a card."

This is the first time in twelve years that Lesca says it to the man himself. It gets two lines. Four lines later she is declining her seat among the dead in the voice she uses for the prefecture. The joke is right and must stay exactly as written. It is what she does instead of feeling it, and in 14 she stops doing it. But a deflection needs something to deflect from, and the confession has not been given the room.

What I would do: one more beat before her knees bend. She waits for him to look up. He marks a second card. The caller says her name again. Then the apologies.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **"I'll be brief", start to finish.** The gap in the window (1). Coralie doing him into the oven and shutting the door on him (1). "You have three minutes" (6). The eleven minutes with no bell (7). "I'll be brief", and then nothing, at the garden gate (15). Coralie bending to the oven in 16 and getting as far as "The—".
2. **Madame Etcheto's care plan.** "Staff to wait. Staff not to join in." (3), Bixente studying his mop, and its return in Coralie's mouth in 16.
3. **Kévin's fingers.** "I'd fly. Wouldn't you? Straight up over the church. Then I'd do my mum's gutter." (3). "Five." in the yard (13). "I wanted six, Maylis." (16).
4. **Jeannot's arithmetic.** "I'll number that." (6). The six points ending "Bugger off" (9), and "Who's dead?" as the way he answers a phone. Number 42, delivered by hand because he will not pay to register it.
5. **The square after the sentence (14).** Tastet turning the sheet over to see whether there is more on the back. The man beyond the last loudspeaker who claps because he thinks she has finished. "A child asked what the lady had done. Somebody told the child." "She's got sixty chairs to do. She isn't leaving the area."
