# Critic report, round 20 — lens: Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. 45,826 words. Nothing else opened.

Verdict for this lens: below the bar by about one point. Chapters 00, 09, 12, 13 and 14 are at it. The dialogue is at it throughout. The narrator between the lines of dialogue is what keeps the rest at 6.5.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.5.** They are countable and they are paid. "'Then which do you want?' ... 'The gate, or Castagnet? Say which, Maylis. For once.' / 'Both.'" (13)
- **Tension: 7.** Nothing before chapter 9 is frightening; from 11 to 13 it does not let go. "She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it broke." (13)
- **Progression: 6.** The book keeps stopping to restate where it is. "It was the same as on Saturday. Lesca had stayed sitting in the chair, so she had come home inside Maylis's body, and she controlled nearly all of it." (6, second paragraph, telling the reader what chapters 4 and 5 already told)
- **Comedy: 7.5.** It is carried by speech, and the speech is exact. "'Eleven years I've been timed like an egg.'" (7)
- **Characters: 7.5.** Each one is audible in a line. "'I don't cook Either,' she said. 'There's no recipe for it. Soup or duck.'" (1)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** The same joke is used for both and the join does not show. "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—' / She stayed bent over for a moment, with her hand on the door." (16)
- **Prose: 6.5.** Clean, easy, and over-captioned. "Lesca had been mayor for eleven years, and she had been good at it, and she had just watched eleven hands take it away. She found that she wanted the pillow more." (15)
- **The ending: 7.5.** The last image is right. The pages before it say the verdict six times. "Then he stepped over the string in his waders and began to dig." (16)
- **Clarity: 7.5.** Nothing is obscure, and that is bought by repetition. The lapses are in the joins: "After that she meant never to lie still under Inguma again, and the cracked rib gave her two nights of pushing. On the Friday night it had no push left." (8; "it" has to be worked out)

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. From chapter 9 I did not stop. Chapters 3 to 5 and 8 I read on credit.

Would I press it on a friend? On one friend who likes village comedy with a death in it. Not yet on the Crouch reader, who would be told the rules a second time in chapter 5 and a third time on the first page of 6.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch on pace of sentence, level on ease, above on what a sentence is made to carry. Level with The Good Place on speech.**

1. **Crouch explains a mechanism once.** The box in Dark Matter is explained by a man under pressure and the next time it appears it is used. Here the chair is shown working in chapter 4, set out as three numbered points in chapter 5, recapped in the second paragraph of 6, restated in 7 ("The one it couldn't fetch was found dead in her own bed in the morning. The old woman in room twelve had said so") and cited as a rule in 9 ("By the first rule of the chair…"). That is the point of difference on pace. It is a prose fault before it is a plot fault: the same information in the same plain declarative, four times.

2. **Crouch states feeling outright too, but in first person, in the present, in the middle of the action, where it reads as a pulse.** This book states it in the past tense, after the beat, in the narrator's voice, where it reads as a caption under a picture. "She wanted", "Maylis wanted" and "Lesca wanted" occur 37 times. The four chapters where the word does not appear at all are 00, 09, 12 and 14, and they are the four best-written. In those, and in 13, the prose is level with the comparison, and its returning sentences ("the clay moved under her palm"; "screwed on crooked, and it shone red") do something Crouch's disposable sentences do not.

3. **The Good Place gives each person a way of talking that is also the way they are wrong.** This book does that at the same level: Larrère's "I'll be brief", Castagnet's "I'll number it", Sarthou's "I shall require it", Marie-Jo's "through the chair", Maylis's "Either". The 02:51 and the verb *left* both re-read what came before. On speech the book is at 8.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The narrator captions what the scene has just shown. (All chapters except 00, 09, 12, 14; worst in 1, 4, 5, 8, 15, 16.)**
- "It was true. Maylis had asked for Coralie, once, and got her, and after that she hadn't wanted to push her luck by asking for anything else. That morning she had written *Either* to save Coralie trouble, and it had made more." (1)
- "In sixteen years Maylis had never got that laugh out of her, and Lesca had got it in ten minutes, with Maylis's mouth. / It hurt to hear." (6)
- "It was the right answer, and Maylis had not been the one to give it." (8)
- "She could give up her own job, but she couldn't give up Kévin." (8, after the folder is held to the glass and she falls silent)
- "She tried to look behind the sum for the woman who had made it, and all she found was a handle, turning. She had the sum and nothing else. She did not know why she had done it." (10; the image lands and two sentences then repeat it)
- "She had been ordered to stay by the gate, and in eighteen years she had never disobeyed that man. She found that she could." (11, after she has already walked off the bank)

What is wrong: every one follows an action or a line of speech that has made the point. The reader is given the feeling, then told which feeling it was. It is the one habit that separates the weaker chapters from the stronger.
What I would do: one pass with one test. If the sentence before it already carries the want, cut the caption. Keep a stated want only where it supplies a fact no action can ("Maylis wanted the duck" in 1; "Lesca wanted to be looked at by the one who didn't know" in 10). I count about sixty to cut. No scene needs rewriting.

**2. Mechanics are explained three and four times. (4, 5, 6, 7, 9; also 3, 6, 9, 13, 14, 15.)**
- The chair: as above, five statements.
- "The pawl was the steel catch that held the winch after every turn." (3) "…the pawl, the steel catch that held each turn" (6) "…the pawl slipped, the steel catch that held each turn." (9)
- "Larrère was in Jeannot's kitchen, on Jeannot's phone." (13) The screen has just said "Castagnet, house" and the kitchen clock is ticking behind his voice. The reader has it. Then chapter 15 hangs the clock on the wall, has Maïté say it, and adds "She had heard that clock once before, down a phone line, in the dark."
- "In this village the river had never come into room twelve, so she was alive, and she was ninety-four." (14)
- "Lesca asked because she didn't know who he meant, but Maylis knew. On Sunday she had seen his folder on Maïté Castagnet's sideboard, and two bowls on the rack." (6, one chapter after the folder and the bowls)
- "This was the moment Lesca's plan had needed. The gate could go up now, in daylight, with nobody on the meadows. The only thing missing was the order." (11, announcing the book's central choice before it is played)

What I would do: explain each thing once, at the point of first need, and afterwards use it. Cut the recap paragraph that opens 6. Cut "By the first rule of the chair". Reduce Lesca's three points in 5 to what chapter 4 did not show: that neither can lie standing up, and that the one not fetched is found dead. Cut the 13 sentence about the kitchen and let chapter 15's clock be the confirmation. Cut the 11 announcement.

**3. Chapter 16 delivers the verdict six times, and Lesca states the book's argument. (16)**
Maylis says it in the chair ("And so was five o'clock… I kept that gate shut for a boy's name on a sheet of paper"), then Sarthou, then Madame Darrigrand ("You had the key"), then Marie-Jo ("he'd had the key and gone for a walk"), then Coralie ("You had the key." / "I had the key."), then Jeannot ("they're saying you went for a walk"). The narrator adds "The rest of it was true." And Lesca: "But I've hidden behind that sum for twelve years, and I'm not having you behind it as well."
What is wrong: each is good and the sum is a round of testimony. Because Maylis has convicted herself in full on the first page, nobody after her tells the reader anything. Lesca's line is the theme said aloud.
What I would do: in the chair, cut Maylis's inventory to "Forty-one" and the offer to sign the sum, so that five o'clock is first said to her by Madame Darrigrand from a doorstep. End Lesca's reply at "You rang nine times." Keep Marie-Jo, because "That's what late means now" changes the village's oldest joke. Cut "The rest of it was true."

**4. Chapter 8 is written as a précis. (8)**
Ten scenes across a week in 3,500 words, joined by summary: "After that she meant never to lie still under Inguma again, and the cracked rib gave her two nights of pushing." "Lesca had given Inguma eleven years of her signatures to count, and it had counted them." "The letter told him that the bank would break on the night of the eleventh, and it asked for his order to raise the gate in daylight…"
What is wrong: the narrator changes register here, from telling scenes to reporting that scenes took place. A crossing, a count of eleven years of signatures and a letter over the mayor's head all happen in subordinate clauses. The chapter ends on a logistics question ("So who holds my hand down then?") straight after the hall of the dead, which is the stronger close.
What I would do: split it at the landing. The bingo night, the landing and the letter make one chapter; the red line, the petition, the vote and the note make another. End the second on the card: "LESCA, MAYLIS." Give the letter two sentences of its actual text in place of the report of it.

**5. The way into the best passage in the book is the worst-written half page in it. (9)**
"Lesca was in her own bed, pushing up against Inguma to keep herself out of the chair, as Maylis had done the night before. On Wednesday, in the steel room, Maylis had asked her whether she knew the man was out on the meadows, and Lesca did not want to stand in that room and be asked again. / But Lesca was lying awake, going over her night in 2014. By the first rule of the chair…"
What is wrong: the chapter is in Maylis's head, and the narrator leaves it to report what a woman in another village is doing and why, then cites a rule to license the flashback.
What I would do: "Its arms were cold. Lesca was not in it." Then one sentence, that the night came to her anyway, worn smooth. Then "A phone rang beside Lesca's bed in the dark."

**6. The planted verb is paid twice, in the wrong order, and the better payment never reaches Maylis. (15, 16)**
"'It was *left*. He was left out there.'" (15) Then, a chapter later in reading order: "'What was the verb?' / 'Drowned.'" (16)
What is wrong: the question has been open since chapter 2 and chapter 5 ("What would the word be?"). The reader gets Maïté's answer, which is the one that rhymes with the card, and then hears the question asked again and answered with a weaker word. Maylis is carrying a card that says *left* in her chest pocket in that very scene, and on the last page she buttons Castagnet's letter in beside it. Nothing is made of it.
What I would do: in 16, Lesca answers "Left. His widow had it." Nothing more. The card in the pocket does the rest.
Same chapter, last five lines of 15: the vote, the caption about the pillow and the pillow itself go by in eighty words of summary. Give the pillow its own short scene or end on Arlette not asking.

**7. The two women speak differently and are narrated identically. (2, 4, 10, 15 against 1, 3, 5, 7)**
"She wanted that card in her hand, and she reached out her right hand towards the shelf." (4, Lesca) "She liked it, and she slowed down to get more of it." (7, Maylis)
What is wrong: outside dialogue the cadence is one cadence: subject, verb, "and", subject, verb. There is no semicolon in the book and seven dashes, all for interrupted speech. That discipline is why it reads easily, and it works in action. In reflection it is flat, and it is the same flat for a mayor who itemises and a keyholder who prices jobs. The book knows how to do it: "*That the mayor remain seated*" is Lesca's wanting in Lesca's form.
What I would do: no rebuild. When fault 1 is cut, most of the sameness goes with it. For what stays in Lesca's chapters, put her wants in her forms (an item, a sum, a line in a file) and leave the plain verb "wanted" to Maylis, for whom wanting is the trouble.

**8. Four stock constructions. (Throughout.)**
- "the way she/he/you…" as simile or speech tag, 31 times. As a tag it is a formula: "in the voice she used for reading a dose off a chart" (5), "the way she read figures out at a council meeting" (8), "the way she read from a patient's chart" (10), "in the voice she used for the prefecture" (12).
- "She knew that smell." (1) "She knew that folder." (5) "He knew that hand." "She knew that sound." (7) "Maylis knew that cold." "Maylis knew those heads." (8) "She knew the car." (11) "Lesca knew that movement." (12)
- The withheld name: "It was Guy Larrère's car." (7, and again in 11) "It was Kévin Labat, and he was…" (11) "It was Jeannot." (13). About a dozen of these.
- "In eighteen years…/In sixteen years… she had never…", four times in chapter 6 alone.
What I would do: keep "She knew that smell" (1) and "Lesca knew that movement" (12), which bracket the book, and rephrase the six between. Keep one chart-voice tag for Coralie and one prefecture voice for Lesca. Name people when they are seen unless the delay is the point; in 13 it is ("It was Jeannot"), in 11 it is not.

**9. People the reader has known for twelve chapters are reintroduced by full name, and one sentence is used twice. (6, 7, 8, 11, 13, 16)**
"Guy Larrère" in full 38 times, often as a speech tag two lines after the last ("'And who says so?' Guy Larrère said." … "'No,' Guy Larrère said.", 11). "Jeannot Castagnet's face" (13) and "Jeannot Castagnet came up the path" (16), when he has been Jeannot to her since chapter 1. And: "Jeannot Castagnet turned on his stool with his glass." (6) "Jeannot Castagnet turned on his stool with his glass." (8)
What I would do: full name on first entrance in a chapter where the formality is the joke (the vote in 8; "said Jeannot Castagnet" closing 6). Otherwise Larrère and Jeannot. Change one of the two stools.

**10. Consistency of spelling and usage.**
- The typed speech, "the same every year", is quoted two ways: "*Jean Castagnet was at his blind that night*" (10, read off the third sheet) and "'Jean Castagnet was at his duck-hunting blind that night'" (14, read off the same sheet).
- "Wednesday night" names two different nights. The meeting is "At nine minutes to three on Wednesday the ninth" (8). Chapter 9: "On Wednesday night Maylis had pushed against Inguma and kept out of the chair" (the night after). Chapter 10: "On Wednesday night she had asked Maylis who would hold her hand down" (the meeting).
- British spelling and vocabulary throughout (colour, metres, laboured, fuelled, pavement, pram, windscreen, wheelie bin, "towards" 20 times and never "toward"), with three American words that recur: "flashlight" 25 times, "dish towel" 7, "truck" 9. Choose. "Blind" is also the American word; if it stays, the rest should not be half-British around it.
- "The stone was beside her right knee, and the two names on it were level with her knee." (14)
- Three counts share the numbers forty, forty-one and forty-two: houses and letters; survivors and letters; beds and the last letter. In 16 "the forty-one" are people at line 53 and "Forty-one I paid to register" are letters at line 211. If letter 42 is meant to stand beside the forty-second bed, nothing on the page says so; if it is not, the reader is sorting numbers on the last page. Nineteen likewise serves the days of rain, the turn the winch sticks at, the bags of grit and Coralie's age when she started work.
- "Nobody in that village knew she had looked down the track first. One man knew." (9) The second sentence withdraws the first.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The borrowed body's definite article, and the count of rings.** "Lesca told the mouth to open. It was stiff, and it was hers to use." (4) "The right hand let go of the key and knocked the lever out of gear." (12) with "One ring." to "Eight." The grammar does the possession. No explanation is needed while it is in use.
2. **Chapter 9 from "A phone rang beside Lesca's bed in the dark" to "Under the water the flashlight found the sole of a wader", and the sentences of the opening that return word for word in 9, 11 and 13.** "…and the clay moved under her palm." "…screwed on crooked, and it shone red."
3. **Larrère's window.** "a few centimetres" seven times; then "The window went down. It went all the way down, into the door." (6); then "This time the mayor had no car around him." (16)
4. **Coralie at the oven door, both times.** "'In my tray,' she said, and shut the door on him." (1) and the attempt that stops at "'The—'" (16).
5. **The corridor to room twelve and the count that follows.** "Nine was open." "Ten was open." "Eleven was open." "Twelve was shut." through "There was a hatbox on it, and nothing else." to "'Forty-one,' Coralie said." (13)
