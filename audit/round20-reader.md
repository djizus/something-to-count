# Round 20: Reader

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else opened but the brief.

Overall: **7**. Good, and one point under the bar. The second half is at the bar. The first half and the fairness of the central choice are not.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 6.5.** Clear and countable ("The hatch was twenty-five minutes away on foot. The bank had ten."), then undersold at the moment that matters, when forty houses are weighed against "*Labat, Kévin.*" on a sheet.
- **Tension: 7.** From chapter 9 on it does not let go ("In her hand, the phone began to ring."). Before chapter 4 the only clock running is a shopping pad.
- **Progression: 6.** Chapter 8 covers eight days and thirteen scenes, and reads in places like notes for itself: "the cracked rib gave her two nights of pushing. On the Friday night it had no push left."
- **Comedy: 7.** Steady smiles, four or five real laughs: "Eleven years I've been timed like an egg."
- **Characters: 8.** Everyone is wrong in a way that is theirs: "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station."
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** In the flooded corridor: "'It's Saturday.' 'Then they'll want the bins out,' he said." And the oven impression that Coralie starts in chapter 16 and cannot finish.
- **Prose: 7.** Easy to read at every sentence, never showy; it explains its hardware too often ("the pawl, the steel catch that held each turn", three times).
- **The ending: 7.** Chapter 14 is a 9 ("the loudspeaker on the bank road said 'drowned him' after the square had finished hearing it"). Chapter 15 hands Lesca her pillow in one line. Chapter 16 has the right last image and a fogged verdict.
- **Clarity: 6.** I had to stop and work out who wanted what at the climax: "The right hand let go of the key and knocked the lever out of gear."

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. After the end of chapter 4 I would not have put it down, except in chapter 8.

**Press it on a friend?** Yes, with the words "give it four chapters". That warning is the gap between 7 and 8.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Crouch: below, by one step.**

- A reader of Dark Matter knows this engine: the version who got the career comes for the bed of the version who kept the marriage. This book does one thing better. Daniela does not notice for a month. Coralie notices in thirty seconds with two fingers, and refuses on principle: "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." That is above the comparison.
- Crouch shows his premise by an event. Here Lesca reasons it out in bed and is right first time (chapter 2). And Crouch never lets a reader say "why doesn't she just". I said it twice in chapter 11.
- Crouch's rules, once stated, bind. Here the death rule is announced four times and then declined with a joke.

**The Good Place: level on the moral problem, below on laughs per page.**

- It is the trolley problem with a winch, a committee and a duck hunter, and the book does not tell me the answer. Lesca pulled the lever and one man died. Maylis ran for the man and one woman died. That is funny and not small.
- The reversals do re-read what came before: the 02:51 on the clock in chapter 1, Coralie's "Nine minutes to three. I've known that for twelve years", the kitchen clock ticking behind Larrère's voice.
- But the biggest one is spent on page one (fault 6).

**What I did not understand**

- Whether the gate could have been raised at all on the night (fault 2).
- What Lesca's walk out of the hall of the dead cost, and whether Madame Etcheto paid it (fault 3).
- Why the visitor drives and the host keeps only the right hand. I accepted it; I was never told.
- Why the flood comes back twelve years on to the day. The book refuses to say ("Nobody was to blame for a dry one"), and I accepted that too, but a Crouch reader will want one sentence more.
- Which night is which in chapters 8 to 10: "Wednesday" is used for the small hours of the ninth and for the night after it.

**Where I skimmed.** The association at the winch (chapter 3). Darrigrand's step (chapter 4). The first half of chapter 9, because I had read it in 00. Most of chapter 8 between the landing and the vote.

**Where I could not stop.** The end of 4. All of 6. The phone call that ends 9. Coralie on the bed in 10. From the reflector in 11 to "Forty-one" in 13. All of 14.

**What I will remember in a month.** A thing made of leaves and rivets that sits on you with its back turned. "Either", and eggs. A car window that opens three centimetres. The dead playing bingo with maize. A confession arriving late from the second loudspeaker. Kévin counting five fingers in the flood and wanting six. A pillow carried across a landing.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. Friday at five does not weigh enough (chapter 11). Structural.**
"She believed every date Lesca had given her, because the river had kept all of them. At three in the morning it would come through the bank at the patch." And two paragraphs later: "'Either,' Maylis said."
Jeannot is on the bank beside her, it is daylight, the handle is on the winch. She believes with certainty that Coralie and forty-two old people will be under the river at three, and she stands down to keep a boy out of court for eels. I did not believe it, and I was angry at her in the wrong way: not "she is weak" but "nobody would". The lever then dissolves anyway: "There is no other sheet."
Do: give her a doubt of her own to stand on. It already exists, two chapters late: "She had built that patch herself in April, one wheelbarrow at a time." At five let her think her patch may hold where Lesca's old bank did not. Then Either is pride and fear together, Kévin's sheet is the excuse she reaches for, and chapter 16's "I kept that gate shut for a boy's name on a sheet of paper" becomes a harder thing for her to say.

**2. Nobody asks whether the winch would have turned (chapters 13 and 16). Structural.**
Jeannot, two arms, shoulder under it: "at nineteen it stopped as if it had hit a wall... The gate's up by a hand." Then Maylis in 16: "Turn the handle first, and everyone behind the bank lives."
A fit man could not raise it. She had one arm and a rib that had just gone again. So either her choice did not kill Madame Etcheto and the whole of chapter 16 blames her unjustly without one person noticing, or it could have been raised and Jeannot's failure needs a reason. The winch has been "in the minutes" since 2011; it is the book's best running grievance, and the ending drops it.
Do: put it in forty-two. Jeannot's letter says the handle stopped at nineteen for a man with two arms. Maylis reads that and refuses it, because she never tried. That keeps her guilt, convicts the association, and costs ten lines.

**3. The death rule is waived with a joke (chapters 12 and 14). Structural.**
The rule, chapter 3: "If it cannot fetch her, she is found dead in her own bed in the morning." The payment, chapter 12: "The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies... She took her hand off the chair, stood up straight and walked."
The joke is good and in character. But the threat has hung over five chapters, and when it comes due she declines it and is fetched home regardless. Then: "Twelve. One, two." and a chair pulled in. I did not know whether I was meant to think her refusal put Madame Etcheto in that seat. Lesca does not know either ("I don't know yet what that cost"), and the last meeting in the steel room never settles it.
Do: keep the apologies, and make the hall answer. The voice stops calling her name only when it calls another number, in that scene, while she is still walking. Then in 16 one of them says out loud what the empty chair was.

**4. The climax is hard to follow and hides its own reversal (chapter 12).**
"Lesca had looked down that track once before, and that time she had reached for a phone. This time she kept the foot down."
That is the only line that tells me Lesca is driving to the blind, and it comes halfway through the fight. Maylis is fighting for the winch ("'Coralie,' she said"). Each woman is doing what the other one stands for. It is the best idea in the book and it goes by as left hands, right hands, gear levers and a count of rings. Four minutes later Maylis drives down the same track herself, and the book gives the change of mind one clause: "Maylis thought she could do better."
Do: one plain sentence at the start of the fight for each aim. In 13, let Maylis notice that she is doing the thing she pulled the handbrake to stop.

**5. Chapter 8 is three chapters. Structural.**
"After that she meant never to lie still under Inguma again, and the cracked rib gave her two nights of pushing."
A return, a track timed, bingo night, the landing, a letter to the prefect, a fight at breakfast, a shift change, the river at the line, a petition, a vote, a handover note, a meeting in the steel room and the hall of the dead. The landing scene and Jeannot's vote are among the best things in the book and neither has room. I lost which day it was twice.
Do: break after the breakfast fight ("Coralie got up and took her bowl to the sink"). Fold the Saturday walk to the hatch into a line. Give the landing the space the council gets in 7.

**6. The opening spends the secret, and chapter 9 says it twice (00 and 9).**
"Water was coming through the bank. It ran down the clay in a sheet as wide as a door and crossed the road in her headlights." That is in 00, and again in 9, nearly word for word, with half a page around it.
After 00 I knew she had turned her headlights down the track and that "The number was already in it." So when Maïté asks "Did you know he was out?" in chapter 2, I knew, and for seven chapters the book kept a secret from its village that it had not kept from me.
Do one of two things. End 00 at "For three breaths she held it, and she did not fit it to the winch." Or keep 00 whole and cut chapter 9's night to what 00 held back: the reflector, the four minutes it went on shining, the sole of the wader.

**7. She knows the minute and does nothing with the hours before it (chapter 11).**
"If she was sitting on his hatch at three, nobody was at the winch, and Coralie was under the bank. She did not go down."
At 03:03 she gets him out in two minutes by knocking and pulling. She is awake at 01:12 with ninety minutes in hand and a fifty-minute round trip. Why not then? The book closes "sit on the hatch until three" and leaves "fetch him at one" wide open.
Do: close it or use it. She sets out at one and the dip turns her back in the dark; or he will not lift the hatch twice in a night. One short paragraph.

**8. The premise is deduced, not found (chapters 1 and 2).**
"So there were two Peyrebarthes. They had been one village until that afternoon in the steel room, when one lamp had come on here and the other one there."
Lesca goes from a stray memory to parallel villages in one night, "one item at a time, the way she did for the council", and is right on every point. Chapter 1 also stops its monster on the first page to explain the experiment ("Twelve years earlier, in June 2014..."). The premise is on the table by the end of chapter 2, which is fast enough, but I was handed it.
Do: let her hold it in chapter 2 as a guess she distrusts, and let the two cards in chapter 4 be the proof. That scene is already the proof; chapter 2 just should not have been certain first.

**9. Chapter 16 is a queue of people telling her (chapter 16).**
"'I'd rather you heard it from me than from the café.'" Two pages on: "'You'll hear it at the café, so you can have it from me.'" And "You had the key" from Madame Darrigrand, then twice from Coralie.
Larrère, Sarthou, Darrigrand, Marie-Jo, Coralie: five verdicts in a row before anything happens. By the fourth I was reading for the end of each speech.
Do: keep Darrigrand's accusation whole. Cut Marie-Jo to what is new, which is what "late" now means in the café. Let Coralie say "You had the key" once.

**10. The book keeps explaining what it has taught me (throughout).**
"The pawl was the steel catch that held the winch after every turn" (3). "the pawl, the steel catch that held each turn" (6). "the pawl slipped, the steel catch that held each turn" (9).
The same with "duck-hunting blind", in full, to the last chapter, even in a widow's mouth: "who was supposed to go round the duck-hunting blinds and warn them." No woman says that about her husband's blind in her own kitchen. I needed each gloss once. The third time I felt talked down to.
Do: explain once, at first use, and then say "the pawl" and "the blind".

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The confession and what follows it (chapter 14).** "Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him." The sentence travelling back through the square at walking pace, the man who claps, the child who asks, and Marie-Jo's "Somebody has to stack them."
2. **Kévin's fingers.** Set up under the plane trees, paid at four o'clock in the yard ("'Five,' he said"), and closed with "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much." Best line in the book.
3. **Coralie's tools.** The shopping pad and its boxes, "I don't cook Either", two fingers on the wrist, the oven door, and "Don't give me the measurement."
4. **Chapter 6 entire.** The window that goes all the way down, "She's got a mayor in her", the spoon tipped back into the bowl, the hand on the stair post.
5. **Jeannot.** The numbered answer at five in the morning ending "And six. Bugger off.", his hand staying up after the vote, letter No. 42 that "doesn't thank you", and the second shovel.

Also leave alone: chapters 3 and 4 arriving at the same sentence from the two sides, and rain or frost in the first line of a chapter to tell me which village I am in.
