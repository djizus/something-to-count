# Round 20 — Thriller

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes — 8.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the care home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." Countable, dated, and by chapter 11 both sides of the sum have faces.
- **Tension — 7.** "The winch was six hundred metres behind her, and the red reflector was four hundred metres in front. The bank had six minutes left." Chapters 11 to 13 run at 9. Chapters 2, 3, the first half of 10, and 14 to 15 run at 5.
- **Progression — 6.5.** "So I'll go down on Friday the eleventh, because you've said it." Every warning tightens the knot, which is the best mechanism in the book. Against it: events told twice, recaps at chapter heads, a detour on flood day, a long tail.
- **Comedy — 7.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station." Funny in the people, slower than the comparison.
- **Characters — 8.** "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them."
- **The switch between dark and comic — 8.** "Twelve. One, two." The bingo joke of chapter 3 becomes the death notice of chapter 14 with no change of voice.
- **Prose — 6.5.** "It hurt to hear." Fast and concrete in action; in reflection it tells the reader what the scene has just shown, and it does so in nearly every scene.
- **The ending — 7.** "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much." It costs more than Crouch charges. It takes three chapters to collect.
- **Clarity — 6.** "After that she meant never to lie still under Inguma again, and the cracked rib gave her two nights of pushing. On the Friday night it had no push left." I read that twice, and I read the van fight in chapter 12 twice to work out who was steering towards what.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. From the stairs at the end of chapter 6 I would not have stopped. Before that, chapter 3 was the risk.

**Press it on a friend?** On a friend who loved The Good Place, yes. On a friend who wants Dark Matter, no, not as it stands.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch, by about a point, as a thriller. Level with him on the flood night. Above him on what the ending costs.**

- **Speed and bodily danger: below.** Crouch has his man at gunpoint in the first chapter and in the wrong world within three. Here the first crossing is the last page of chapter 4, about a fifth of the way in, and nobody is in physical danger until chapter 13 of 16. Until then the fear is of an appointment. The river gauge carries it well ("A metre and a half under the line", "fifty-five under", "forty under", then the bootlace of water at Sarthou's step), but it is one kind of fear for twelve chapters.
- **The set piece: level.** The timestamps in chapter 13 do what Crouch's one-line paragraphs do, and the corridor ("Nine was open… Ten was open… Eleven was open… Twelve was shut") is as good as anything in Recursion.
- **The usurper: below.** Crouch makes a reader afraid for a marriage by letting the other man have the house and the wife. Here the reader is afraid for exactly two chapters: from "I'd have this one again" (6) to "You'll go back down those stairs" (8). After that Coralie can tell them apart and the bed is safe.
- **Cost: above.** Crouch gives the family back. This book drowns the old woman who taught the heroine the words, floods the Port, and puts the pillow in a car.
- **Against The Good Place on reversals: below.** One reversal re-reads the book: the kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice in chapter 9 turning into "Castagnet, house" on the screen in 13. The main secret does not, because the opening has already shown it.

**Where the reader is afraid, and of what.** Of the date, throughout. Of Lesca in the body, chapters 6 to 8. Of not being fetched, for one scene in 7 ("Nothing came at five past two. Nothing came at half past"). Of the water, 13. Never for Jeannot: he is too funny to drown, and the book knows it.

**Where a Crouch reader puts it down.** Chapter 3, at the men oiling the winch. If not there, the tape-measuring at the top of chapter 10. If not there, after "Forty-one."

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The central choice is spread over three moments, and the one that decides everything hangs on the lightest lever. (11, 13)**
"'Either,' Maylis said." At five o'clock, in daylight, with Jeannot on the bank and the handle on the winch, she keeps the gate shut because "Now it had Kévin Labat's name on it, and if the gate moved, the sheet went to the prosecutor at Bayonne." She believes the date completely. Prison for eels, for a boy who is not in the scene, does not weigh against forty-two beds, and half an hour later the same woman walks off her post: "in eighteen years she had never disobeyed that man. She found that she could." Then the choice at 02:55 is made at the patch as a sum of minutes ("Three minutes to get there. Two to get him up the ladder. Three back"), and the choice at the dip is three sentences of logistics.
*Do:* move Kévin's net-pulling scene forward so he is on the platform at five, an arm's length from the handle, not knowing. Then "Either" is said in front of the boy it protects and the man it fails, with the car watching. At 02:55, she drives from the patch to the track and must pass the winch with the handle on it; the text does not look at it. Make her look. That is the opening page's three breaths, taken the other way.

**2. The book's two own monsters are let off. (8, 12, 14)**
The usurper yields in under three hundred words: "She took the left hand off Maylis's fingers and laid it open on the steering wheel. In the steel room the mayor stood up." And the rule that has hung since chapter 3 ("If it cannot fetch her, she is found dead in her own bed in the morning") is beaten by a joke: "The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies… she will not be taking this seat." She wakes on a stretcher: "Bayonne has had you for three hours and found nothing."
*Do:* make the hall charge for the seat. At the top of 14 she should not be put down until the place set for her is filled: the name called and called, then "Twelve", the chair pulled in, and only then released. One sentence of causation. Her two minutes at the wheel are then paid for by the old woman in front of the reader's eyes, and the rule has bitten.

**3. The tail. (14 second half, 15)**
The plot question closes at "Forty-one." The book then runs three more chapters, about a fifth of its length. Chapter 14 holds while the third sheet is unread, but chapter 12 has already announced the outcome: "She has a ceremony at eleven and a third sheet to put right." After the wreath it becomes paperwork: "He stood among the chairs with his hat on, holding three keys, a sash and a summons." Chapter 15 is five scenes that each shut one thread; its last beat before the pillow is "Madame Ducasse wrote down eleven, and one abstention."
*Do:* cut "and a third sheet to put right" from 12. End 14 at the medal case or at Marie-Jo's "Somebody has to stack them." Reduce the summons, the suspension, the Bayonne statement and the vote to one page between them. Keep Maïté's kitchen, Larrère at the garden gate, and the landing whole.

**4. The secret is on page one, and chapter 9 hands it over again as a download. (00, 9)**
The opening ends "It rang once." So when Maïté asks "Did you know he was out?" in chapter 2, the reader already knows. Chapter 9 then repeats the opening's sentences ("It ran down the clay in a sheet as wide as a door and crossed the road in her headlights") and delivers the night by rule: "So Maylis was given the whole of that night." To make that possible the narrator steps outside Maylis to report what Lesca is doing in another bed: "Lesca did not want to stand in that room and be asked again." A midpoint reveal that tells the heroine what the reader has held for eight chapters is not a reveal.
*Do:* keep the opening; it is the best hook in the book. Cut chapter 9's replay down to what is new: Larrère's call and the clock, Coralie's car in the yard, the reflector, the four minutes, the sole of the wader. Drop the paragraph explaining where Lesca is.

**5. Chapter 10 opens with a roll of tape on the day of the flood. (10)**
"He paced from the steps to the stone, tore off two strips and stuck them on the planks beside it, in a cross." Chapter 9 ends at five on Friday morning with Jeannot alive and swearing; chapter 11 starts at ten past five. Between them the reader is sent to the dry village for a seating plan.
*Do:* open the rehearsal at "The front row belongs to the care home" and go straight to the eight strips. Keep Arlette's ponies and the call log untouched.

**6. Chapter 3 is five errands before the premise moves. (3)**
Lights, finger-counting, room twelve, the winch inspection, the café, and only then the chair. "'It needs grease,' he said. 'It's had grease since 2013. I buy it.'" The café scene exists to say "The two villages merge in January", which Larrère says again in chapter 6.
*Do:* cut the café scene. Halve the inspection, keeping the six men turning and "The keyholder has no preference."

**7. Rules arrive when the plot needs them. (6, 10, 11, 12)**
"She could also take the jaw for a word or two, if she threw everything she had at it" appears in chapter 6, the chapter that uses it. "The thing that sits on you doesn't need you asleep" is said a page before the van. That a third person can set the count for you, that the creature can be sat on, that a rider can be in the van and "still sitting on Inguma" at once: each turns up at the moment of use.
*Do:* move, do not add. Put the jaw in the first crossing in chapter 4. Put "wide awake, both times" in Kévin's first speech in chapter 3.

**8. Lesca solves the premise lying down. (2)**
"Nothing else she could think of fitted the winch, the letters and the raincoat." Three clues, one page, the right answer, no price. The proof scene two chapters later (the card read twice, "The t was crossed low") is far better and arrives already explained.
*Do:* leave her in chapter 2 with three things that do not fit and a suspicion. Let the card be the proof.

**9. The cause of the rain is raised and dropped, and the experiment never pays. (5)**
"'Then it had been raining for two days before you did anything.'" "'I want somebody to blame.'" A high-concept reader is told the question matters and then told there is no answer. Dr Kessler and her university do not return.
*Do:* cut Lesca's theory that she brought the weather, or let the explanation of the minute in chapter 9 ("the time it came at was not hers") reach as far as the rain. One or the other; not a tease.

**10. Recaps and repeated glosses slow every restart. (6, 9, 10, throughout)**
"It was the same as on Saturday. Lesca had stayed sitting in the chair, so she had come home inside Maylis's body, and she controlled nearly all of it." The pawl is explained three times ("the steel catch that held each turn"). After-the-beat sentences explain scenes that worked: "It hurt to hear."; "That morning she had written *Either* to save Coralie trouble, and it had made more."
*Do:* one clause of recap per chapter head, one gloss per term, and strike the sentence after the beat wherever the beat landed.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 13 from "02:53" to "Forty-one."** The wheelchair going sideways "like a crab", the unlit corridor between van trips, the room numbers, the line through the last name.
2. **The right hand.** Tipping the spoon, locked on the post at the foot of the stairs, crossing the t "low". The ending of chapter 6: "Are you coming up?"
3. **Jeannot's contrariness as the plot's engine.** "I forbid you to go" producing the oath; her threat producing "Shut"; "And six. Bugger off."; the dry cellar; a fourth page that "doesn't thank you".
4. **The kitchen clock.** Heard behind the order in 9, on the screen as "Castagnet, house" in 13, on Maïté's wall in 15.
5. **Kévin's five fingers at four o'clock in the water,** and "I wanted six."
