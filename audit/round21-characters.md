# Round 21 — Characters

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 6.5.** "'I was in no danger, and I'll say so to anyone.'" (17). The life the book spends twelve chapters on was never at risk, Coralie is never credibly in danger, and the one who dies is ninety-four and walked back to her room on Coralie's arm.
- **Tension: 7.5.** "The winch was six hundred metres behind her, and the red reflector was four hundred metres in front. The bank had six minutes left." (14). From the end of 12 to the end of 14 it does not let go. Chapters 7 to 9 run on council procedure and I was not afraid of anything in them.
- **Progression: 6.5.** "Maylis threw herself at the jaw, and got it." is in 6 and again, word for word, in 13, with the same fight in 4, 8 and 9. The two women in one body learn nothing new about each other after the stairs in 6.
- **Comedy: 7.5.** "'And six. Bugger off.'" (10). The people are funny in themselves, which is the hard part.
- **Characters: 7.5.** "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'" (8). Coralie, Jeannot, Kévin and Madame Etcheto are alive. Maylis is alive in about a third of her scenes and a thesis in the rest. Lesca is alive and let off.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'" (14), in a flooded corridor, pulling Monsieur Daguerre out backwards. The same joke from bingo night, now carrying weight. That is how The Good Place does it.
- **Prose: 7.** "That wasn't true. She wanted Coralie to stay awake and keep those two fingers on her wrist, but Coralie had to be up before six, and Maylis didn't ask." (1). Clear and easy to read, and it tells me what a character wants and why she does not say so, in that same three-part sentence, a couple of dozen times.
- **The ending: 6.** "'Move over, Maylis.'" (16), three days after "'Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him.'" Maylis's ending is an 8. Lesca's is a 5.
- **Clarity: 7.** "'I was in this hall at five on Saturday morning with the urn on,'" (17). I do not know whether Marie-Jo is accusing Maylis or standing with her, and it is the village's last word on her.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. From the end of 3 I was not going to stop.
**Press it on a friend?** No. I would lend it and say the last two chapters let one of the two women off.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Above Crouch on the people. Below The Good Place on what the two leads do to each other. Net: half a point under the bar.**

- **Above Crouch: the other self is not the villain, and the partner is not the prize.** In Dark Matter the second Jason is a thief and Daniela is what gets stolen. Here the one with the better title is the one with a drowned man on her hands, she is half the book's point of view, and when she comes for the other one's bed Coralie takes a pulse, works out there are two women in one wrist, and sends her downstairs on principle. Crouch never lets the wife refuse the swap for the sake of the person being swapped out.
- **Above Crouch: one body, two wills, shown by a hand.** The right hand that tips the spoon back into the bowl, goes for Coralie's keys, rings the timer and crosses a t low does more character work than any paragraph of Jason's interior.
- **Level with The Good Place: everyone is wrong in their own way.** Larrère through four centimetres of window, Jeannot numbering his grievances, Sarthou billing the village for laying its own sand, Marie-Jo speaking through the chair to a wall, Josiane with "It's adultery either way". And the reversal in 10 re-reads "No one came to tell him" the way a Good Place reveal re-reads a season.
- **Below The Good Place: the four in that show change each other face to face, at length.** These two meet in the steel room four times for perhaps five pages in total, conduct each meeting as an agenda, and settle the book in twenty-five lines. The premise is two versions of one person. The relationship between them is the thinnest in the book.
- **Below Crouch: fear for the marriage.** Crouch makes one evening warm and then takes it. This book makes two scenes warm (the oven in 1, the corridor in 3) and then gives the couple nine chapters of cross-examination over a shopping pad. When Coralie leaves in 17 I was on her side and felt it as her release. I did not feel what Maylis lost.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. Lesca's ending is cheap, and somebody else pays her bill. (16, structural.)**
"'I don't know yet what that cost. I heard twelve called, and a chair pulled in.'" Then in 17: "'I wouldn't take it. So it called twelve.'" Lesca refused her seat among the dead with the best joke in the book, a woman in the other village drowned to fill it, and Lesca's whole reckoning is those two sentences. In her own village she loses a sash and a medal, the Monday at the gendarmes and the Tuesday vote go by in two paragraphs of summary, the widow gives her a gutter, and Coralie is in her bed by Tuesday night. The woman who knowingly drowned a man ends with the woman. The one who tried to save everyone ends alone with a spade. If that asymmetry is meant, Lesca still has to be seen paying.
*Do:* The scene exists and is wasted. In 16 Lesca stands in front of the living Madame Etcheto, who asks "'Where was it?'", and Lesca answers "'It was with me'" and says nothing of what her seat cost. Make her say it to that face and take what the old woman says back. Dramatise the Tuesday vote or cut it. End Lesca's book on the Saturday image, "she left her door open and the lamp on", and not on the pillow.

**2. The last meeting of the two women is the shortest scene they have, and it comes first. (17, structural.)**
"Neither of them had a motion for that." Sum, stairs, sash, seat, verb: five closures in five exchanges, placed before Maylis has walked through the silt, been told "'You had the key'", or heard a car door shut on a pillow. She goes into the last meeting with nothing yet lost. And the mirror the whole book builds is never looked at by the two people who made it: Lesca had Coralie's car in the yard and a van on the track and turned the handle; Maylis had the same two and drove to the van. Coralie says it ("'I was the other one'"). The two women never do.
*Do:* Move the meeting to Sunday night, after Coralie has gone, and the grave to Monday morning; the burial is Tuesday, so the dates hold. Give it the length of the meeting in 5. One exchange about Coralie, no more, and no explaining.

**3. Lesca disappears as a point of view for six chapters, during the worst thing she does. (5 to 10, structural.)**
"Lesca kept Maylis standing on the landing, in front of that door, until the light went out under it." (8). Her motion in 4 is the strongest want in the book: "*and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*" Then she acts on it, on the stairs in 6 and the landing in 8, and both times I am behind her, in the wrist. I never get her face when Coralie laughs at her joke after eleven years, or when Coralie says "'I'd have you tomorrow'" and takes the pillow back. Keeping to the victim's side is right for the stairs. On the landing it means the book's hungriest person is refused out of sight.
*Do:* Give the landing in 8 to Lesca. Maylis is the pull in the wrist.

**4. Maylis's choice at the dip is a sum, not a choice between people. (14.)**
"Coralie had a list, a minibus, Kévin's van and ten minutes' warning. Jeannot had a bolted hatch and nobody coming but Maylis. She got out and went on." That is Lesca's method exactly, and the book does not seem to notice. Nothing in her body knows she is leaving Coralie under the bank, so "'I was the other one'" in 17 tells me what the scene meant after the fact. The line that would connect it to what she wants is two pages earlier and left hanging: "Maylis thought she could do better." That is the sash. She says "'I wanted your sash'" in 17 as if it were a separate matter.
*Do:* Cut the sum. Put one thing she does there: three steps back towards the lamp over the winch, and then she turns. Let "do better" stand as the reason, and let 17 name it as the same want.

**5. The marriage in Maylis's village stops being shown after 3. (5 to 12.)**
"'I asked where you'd be,' Coralie said, and she went up to the spare room." (10). Every scene between them from 5 onward is Coralie asking and Maylis failing, and most end on Coralie leaving a room. Maylis never once looks at Coralie the way Lesca does: "warm from the bed, with her hair flat on one side", "rough at the tips from the soap at the care home" (4). Those are Lesca's eyes. Maylis notices a wrist-hold and a pad. So when she says "'Stay'" I had no picture of what she was asking to keep.
*Do:* The dressing change in 10 is eight lines; make it the third scene where they are good at each other, before it sours. Give Maylis one moment of watching Coralie work in the care home corridor in 12 and wanting her, without the narrator saying "wanted".

**6. The widow hands Lesca an excuse the same evening. (16.)**
"'Twelve years he's sat across a table from you and let you carry it.'" Eight hours after Lesca tells the square she drowned Jean Castagnet, his widow's one private scene with her turns to Guy. Maïté is never angry at Lesca at all. Lesca refuses the gift ("'don't give me him for that'"), but the scene has already moved the weight. And the garden gate does all of it better ten lines later: "'You knew he was out there, Guy'", "'I'll be brief'", and nothing.
*Do:* Cut the kitchen speech about Guy. Keep the question, "'I don't know'", and "*Left*". Let Lesca learn about the kitchen phone from the top of the ladder, with her hands full, when Maïté says it to him.

**7. Maylis is run on one word, and the narrator explains it every time. (1, 3, 5, 7.)**
"But if she said the pawl, and the pawl failed with the river up, it would be her pawl." (3). Before that: "if Maylis ticked that box it would be Maylis's doing" (1). Same reason, same construction, and I had it the first time. Eight spoken or written Eithers by 12, and Coralie, Arlette, Sarthou, Lesca, Larrère and Jeannot each take a turn diagnosing it. She is alive exactly where the word is absent: buying grave flowers for a living man without knowing why (5), laughing through the minute's silence at letter seventeen (7), "'I want that'" about a kitchen timer (7).
*Do:* Keep the narrator's reason once, in 1. Cut it in 3. Cut the Either to Sarthou in 7 and the breakfast one in 5, where ticking both boxes five lines later does it better. Save the word for the platform in 12, where it costs something.

**8. Coralie speaks the design, and has no life that is not service to a Lesca. (6, 7, 17.)**
"'I'd have this one again.'" (6). "'I'd keep that one in the house.'" (7). "'I chose you, Maylis, over somebody who asked.'" (17). Each is the author's point in a clean sentence, and the first two are the same sentence in two kitchens one chapter apart. In 11 her articulacy is earned: she has had twelve years to find the words. In 7 and 17 it is commentary. And in both villages nearly everything she does is cook, strap, take a pulse, dust round an envelope or draw a box. Sixteen years, and the only memory of them is a sandwich on a wall. She has no family, no town she came from, no reason Bayonne is Bayonne.
*Do:* Cut "'I'd keep that one in the house'" and "'I chose you, Maylis, over somebody who asked'"; the sentences before them already say it. Give her one fact that belongs to her alone and attach Bayonne to it.

**9. Maylis sees her dead father and the book spends one clause on it. (9.)**
"Her father was sitting at the end of the second row, with his watch beside his card." He is where Either comes from, where the key comes from, the tall L, the four turns short. Larrère, Jeannot and Arlette each beat her with him, and in 12 "she was tired of being told." Then she is in a room with him and the sentence moves on to the empty chair. Lesca gets her beat with him in 13 ("He did not look up from his numbers, and she did not stop"). Maylis gets none.
*Do:* One beat in 9 on her way to the empty chair. She does something, he does not look up. No speech.

**10. The mayor's village is staffed by job titles, and the one man who knew is never used. (10, 15, 16, 17.)**
"One man knew she had looked down the track first. He was the priest" (10). Père Honoré holds Lesca's secret for twelve years and his whole part in her story is "looked at his shoes". Tastet, Ducasse and the young man from Pau are functions with names. The doubles are not always signposted: Monsieur Darrigrand sweeps a step in one village, Madame Darrigrand has a baby in the other; Sarthou is serving in one and retired in the other, told in a subordinate clause. And Marie-Jo's last line to Maylis (quoted under Clarity) cannot be read either way.
*Do:* Give the priest one line to Lesca after the square or cut the sentence that sets him up. Make Marie-Jo's line an accusation or a hand held out, not both.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The landing in 8.** Coralie taking the pulse to find out who is in the wrist, "'That's her. In this hand'", and "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'" Change whose eyes it is seen through if you take fault 3. Do not change a word she says.
2. **The phone log in 11.** "'Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one.'" and "'Because you'd have told me.'" Fault 8 does not apply here.
3. **Lesca among the dead in 13.** "'I saw your van. I rang once.' / He put a kernel on a card." and "'You can put that in the minutes as apologies received.'" Fault 1 is about what follows from this, not about this.
4. **The yard and corridor in 14, to "'Forty-one.'"** Daguerre's "'Here'", "'Then they'll want the bins out'", Kévin holding his hand in his own headlights and saying "'Five'", the two fingers on Madame Etcheto's wrist for the full half minute, the pencil line. And its answer in 17: "'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much.'"
5. **Jeannot from "Put it in forty-two" to No. 42.** The letter that "doesn't thank you", "'I was what you walked to. That goes in writing'", her father's shovel, and the last line dropping the "little": "'You won't get that down two metres with one arm, Lesca.'"

## 5. Chapter by chapter

- **00.** A woman, a bank, a number already in her phone. No person yet, only a question about one. **7**
- **01.** The postman takes the man in the car for the worker and the woman with the rake for nobody; the oven does Larrère. The marriage at its warmest, and over-explained. **7**
- **02.** Same bed, one pillow. Maïté asks "'Did you know he was out?'" and Lesca answers the tile. I wanted to know what she was not saying. **7.5**
- **03.** Kévin and Madame Etcheto arrive and the book has its two best villagers. Ends on a mouth saying what its owner did not. **8.5**
- **04.** Lesca holds Coralie's fingers with another woman's hand and writes a motion to take the bed. The first chapter where somebody wants something enough to do wrong for it. **9**
- **05.** The two meet and neither likes being the same height. Chrysanthemums to a living man's wife: Maylis at her most alive. **8**
- **06.** The window goes all the way down; Coralie laughs with her head back for the wrong woman; a hand on the stair post. **8.5**
- **07.** Three chapters' worth: blackmailed, blackmailing, a day as mayor. The laugh in the silence and Kévin with his cap in his hands are the best of it. The council runs long. **7.5**
- **08.** Bingo, then the landing. Coralie becomes the person I trust most in the book. **8**
- **09.** A vote, a mother, a note, a meeting. Arlette's "'Then you learned to write'" lands. Too much procedure between the good things, and a father passed without a look. **7.5**
- **10.** The night as Lesca lived it, and then nine rings and six numbered points. Reveal and joke in one chapter. **9**
- **11.** Eight strips of tape for forty-two beds, and what Coralie has known for twelve years. The best scene between Lesca and anyone. **8.5**
- **12.** Long, and earns it. "Either" on the platform is the one that costs. Ends with the phone ringing in a hand that cannot move. **9.5**
- **13.** Eight rings, a handbrake, five words, and a mayor declining to be dead. **9**
- **14.** The night. Every resident a person in one line. I held my breath at door twelve. **9**
- **15.** The sentence travelling back through the square at walking pace. Then Marie-Jo says her name and gives her chairs to stack. **8**
- **16.** A strong kitchen and a stronger garden gate, then Monday and Tuesday told to me in summary and a pillow I did not think she had earned. **6.5**
- **17.** The meeting is too short and too early. Kévin's six, the bus fare that is not enough, the car door on a pillow, and a man in waders stepping over the string. I wanted more of the first and nothing added to the last. **7**
