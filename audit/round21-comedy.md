# Critic report — Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes — 8.** "Friday was the eleventh. On the night the river came through the bank, Coralie would be at the care home, right under it."
- **Tension — 7.5.** "She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it broke." The clock works from chapter 12 on; before that the pressure is a gauge reading per chapter.
- **Progression — 7.** "That village had not been spared its flood. It hadn't had it yet." That is the premise arriving, at the end of chapter 2, and the first crossing waits until the end of chapter 4.
- **Comedy — 7.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" Good, and all in one register.
- **Characters — 8.** "'Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you, and that was me.'"
- **The switch between dark and comic — 8.5.** "'Twelve. One, two.'" A bingo call in chapter 8, a death in chapter 15, with no change of wording.
- **Prose — 7.5.** "Then they opened the door, counted one of her, paid her, gave her a sandwich and went back to their university." The timing is in the sentences. The mark is held down by speech patterns shared across the cast (fault 2).
- **The ending — 8.** "It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you."
- **Clarity — 7.** "But on Monday the river had been low, and when the river is low, a raised gate lets the water run off the meadows. So raising the gate had drained his blind." The reader is asked to hold a gate that both drains and floods, two villages, a hand that belongs to someone else and a demon with a counting rule. It can be followed, but not at the speed slapstick needs.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes.
**Pressed on a friend?** Yes, on one who likes dry village comedy. Not on one who wants the joke rate of The Good Place, because I would have to warn them first.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Against Crouch: above.** Crouch is not funny and does not try to be. This book uses comedy to do work he does with a physics lecture: the whole many-worlds premise is delivered in "counted one of her, paid her, gave her a sandwich".

**Against The Good Place: below on comedy, level on the moral problem, above on one thing.**

- **Below: range.** The Good Place has four people who are wrong in four different ways, and one of them is a fool. Here the village is funny in one way, the dry literal retort, and almost everybody who delivers it is right. Only Larrère, Monsieur Daguerre and the postman are funny by being wrong. Nobody is silly.
- **Below: escalation.** The Good Place runs its premise in public three times, each time with more people knowing. Here the public farce (a keyholder with a mayor in her) runs once, in chapter 6, pays once in chapter 9, and is then switched off by a sentence in chapter 12.
- **Level: the moral problem made funny without being made small.** A committee that cannot find a verb to cut between two names — "'Opened what? There's a name over it. It reads that she opened him.'" — is the trolley problem as a grammar dispute, and it is answered thirteen chapters later with *Left*. A Good Place reader will also recognise Chidi in Maylis at once. The book goes past him: when she finally chooses, the choice costs a life anyway, and "Both" is exposed as "Either, with the river in it".
- **Above: the running gags are the mechanism of the catastrophe.** "Either" shuts the gate. The dip in letter seventeen sinks the van. "That's my door" is room twelve. The comic gossip about which Castagnet she is after covers a real adultery in the same house. The card in her chest pocket says *left* from chapter 3. The Good Place's reversals re-read the plot; these re-read the jokes. Nothing in the text points at the card, and nothing should.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The book summarises exactly where a comedy would play the scene. (12, 7, 16)**
"She had tried the café first. The nine who signed on Tuesday had watched Jeannot's hand go up, and a vote was a vote."
That is the comic chorus refusing the heroine on the day of the flood, and it happens offstage in two sentences. It is also the scene that would make the village's share in room twelve visible, and it would give "'Had the key and went for a walk, did you?'" something to answer for. Three more of the same class: Larrère untimed for the first time in eleven years is reported ("He did the gravel lake, which she had signed for, and the four houses knocked down in the Port, and the cameras on the gates"); Maylis's morning in the mayor's cupboards is "She had learned a great deal about road salt"; and the removal vote, the last scene in the book's best comic arena, is "Eleven hands went up" with no timer on the table.
*Do:* play the café on Friday morning, short. The village has had her prophecy read back to it off Lafitte's pad; let it believe the mayor in her about everything except the river: the dealer's hand, the bingo numbers, Tuesday's weather. Give Larrère eight lines of his own voice unrung. Play the removal with the timer set to three in front of Lesca and none of it used. Pay for the length in chapter 12, where she decides not to go down the track twice more in summary ("She meant to go down a third time", "There was still time to go down the track and knock").

**2. One comic transaction: the rebuke with a dry tail. (whole book; worst in 9)**
"'I know where I am. I'm asking where I am in this.'" (Coralie) and, in the same chapter, "'I know how they voted. I'm asking why I had it from Josiane.'" (Arlette).
The same sentence in two mouths, forty lines apart. So are "'That isn't what I asked'" (Maïté), "'I asked where you'd be'" (Coralie), "'That's not a no'" (Maylis); and "Don't give me the measurement" (Coralie), "'Don't give me the minutes'" (Coralie), "don't give me Lafitte's pad" (Jeannot), "don't give me him for that" (Lesca). Darrigrand, Arlette, Marie-Jo, Maïté, Madame Etcheto, Madame Darrigrand and both Coralies all end their scenes by correcting one of the two heroines, in the same clipped shape, from a position of being right. It is funny for nine chapters and a pattern after that. The laughs are nearly all at the heroines' expense and nearly all verdicts.
*Do:* decide who in the village is funny by being wrong, by excess or by silence, and take the capping line away from at least three of the seven. Jeannot's numbered rant and Sarthou's "I shall require it" show it can be done. Break the two shared templates wherever a second speaker uses them.

**3. Coralie stops making Maylis laugh after chapter 3, and the oven has no middle beat. (1 → 17)**
"At that table Coralie was the funny one, and Maylis was the one who laughed."
The narrator says it in chapter 6. It was last shown in chapter 3. From chapter 5 Coralie is an examiner: boxes, questions, rulings. The marriage the reader is asked to fear for was defined in chapter 1 by a woman strapping a rib so her wife could go on laughing, and that laughter is not on the page again. The failed impression in chapter 17 — "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" — is the best thing in the last chapter, and it is reaching back sixteen chapters to a single outing.
*Do:* give her the oven door once more, around chapter 9. Larrère chairing a meeting from his driver's seat through an open café door is asking to be done in that kitchen. Then chapter 17 is the third time and the one that fails.

**4. Explanations parked inside the joke. (1, 2, 5)**
"The blind was his duck-hunting blind, a cellar under a hatch out on the flood meadows. Ducks only come down at night onto standing water, and a hand of water is enough…"
Four sentences of hydrology sit between Jeannot's "Last night I sat over mud" and "'I was told to raise it.'" In chapter 2 a paragraph on the history of Marie-Jo's sash sits between the timer going down and the verb exchange it sets up. In chapter 5 "They were the flowers people put on graves" arrives one line before "'Who's dead?'" says it better, and Marie-Jo then says it a third time.
*Do:* move each explanation ahead of its scene. The blind goes where the gate and the key are introduced; Marie-Jo's plastic bag goes at her first turn of the timer; cut the chrysanthemum gloss and leave it to Lafitte.

**5. The narrator makes the joke again. (15, 14)**
"His sheet had a line for a mayor who could not attend, but it had no line for a mayor who stood on her cross of tape with nothing more to read." Eight paragraphs later: "'I've got nothing on the sheet for this.'"
One joke, told by the narrator and then by the man. In chapter 14, in the flooded corridor: "The two of them had started laughing and couldn't stop, and the old woman had heard them" — a recap of chapter 3 at the door of room twelve, where the reader's own memory would do more.
*Do:* cut the narrator's version in 15 and keep the young man's. Cut the recap in 14 to the corridor and the brass number.

**6. The chorus has jokes and no people. (6, 9, 17)**
"'She's got a mayor in her,' the dealer said."
The man who also says "'Ask her what you're going to say tomorrow'" and "'Had the key and went for a walk, did you?'" has three of the best lines in the book and no name in seventeen chapters. Josiane is one syllogism, used by her in 6, by Larrère in 7 and again in 12. Lafitte is a pad. A reader would miss the pad.
*Do:* name the dealer and give him one thing he wants. Give Josiane a second note.

**7. Set-ups left unpaid. (4/16, 9, 17)**
"'All I ask is that nobody puts a glass down on it.'"
Darrigrand's step is set up in chapter 4; the bar and the crowd are on those plots in chapter 16; no glass. "'Ask her what you're going to say tomorrow'" is an invitation nobody takes up. And Josiane, who has "kept this counter thirty years", named the right house and the wrong woman, and is never shown finding out; she counts change.
*Do:* one glass, seen or moved. One question put to the prophet (see fault 1). One line for Josiane as the bus leaves.

**8. Bingo night sets a stage and does not turn. (8)**
"'Seventy-one. Seven, one.' / 'Here,' said Monsieur Daguerre, from his wheelchair."
Thirty-five lines of calling before anybody at the bar wants anything. The scene is visibly dressing the hall for chapters 9, 13 and 15. It is charming and it idles.
*Do:* start the fight for the jaw under the calling, so the numbers run over a struggle, and bring Jeannot to the bar a prize earlier.

**9. Lesca's forbidden word is never tested. (5 →)**
"'I gave that word up when I was thirty-one, and I won't have my own face say it to me.'"
The one word that would let her pass as Maylis is the one word she will not say, and she never has to. She announces herself at ten past six on her first morning and nobody is ever nearly fooled.
*Do:* once, in front of Larrère or Sarthou, make "Either" the only safe answer, and let her fail to get it out.

**10. Two gags over-milked inside the flood. (14)**
"'Here,' said Monsieur Daguerre, to the roof of the van."
A few lines after "'Here.' / 'I know you are.'", which was perfect and complete. And Jeannot's speech at the door runs eight sentences with a man waiting to be carried; the line is "I put it in number six" and the rest delays it.
*Do:* cut the second "Here". Cut Jeannot to the click count, the knee and number six.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie at the oven door** in chapter 1 ("'In my tray,' she said, and shut the door on him"), the bandage that lets the laugh out, and the impression that will not come in chapter 17.
2. **"Either" at the gate** — "'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. 'That's how the vote left it.'" — and Coralie's "I know that word. I've cooked it."
3. **Madame Etcheto, from the care plan to the call.** "She said it had been told twice and given two lots of stars, and it got through them early and came at one." "'With cards?'" "'That's my door.'" "'Twelve. One, two.'"
4. **"'The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies… You can put that in the minutes as apologies received.'"** And the drowned man putting a kernel on his card while she confesses to him.
5. **Jeannot's letters**, from "'Put it in forty-two'" through "'And six. Bugger off'" to No. 42 carried up by hand. Do not soften six.
