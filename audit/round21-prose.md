# Critic report: prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

Counts below are over the whole manuscript (44,168 words, 3,372 sentences) unless a chapter is named.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "One woman could turn the winch, or she could reach that hatch. She couldn't do both." (ch 8)
- **Tension: 7.** 8 in chapters 11 to 14, 5 to 6 in chapters 6 to 9. "The winch was six hundred metres behind her, and the red reflector was four hundred metres in front. The bank had six minutes left." (ch 14)
- **Progression: 6.** "Maylis threw herself at the jaw, and got it." That sentence is in chapter 6 and again, word for word, in chapter 13. The middle moves by repeating.
- **Comedy: 7.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (ch 7)
- **Characters: 8.** "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'" (ch 8)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" (ch 17). The joke from chapter 1 fails on purpose, and that is the grief.
- **Prose: 7.** Dialogue 8, narration 6. The narration can do "It was a morning's work for two arms, and she had one." (ch 17) and it can also do "She tried her right hand first, because she was right-handed." (ch 1)
- **The ending: 7.** Chapter 17 from the Port onwards is an 8. Chapter 16 drops to minutes: "On Tuesday at six Tastet took the chair of the village council, with one item in front of him, which asked the prefect to remove the mayor." The mayor's fall takes five sentences.
- **Clarity: 6.** "'Whichever of us wakes first wakes at home. If the other one is still sitting here, she goes home with her.'" (ch 5). The reader has to carry five rules of this kind, and chapters 6 and 13 ask them to track two owners of one body by which hand is named.

**Finished unpaid:** yes. From chapter 10 I did not stop. Chapters 6 to 9 I read on credit from chapters 1 to 5.

**Press it on a friend:** yes, with the warning that chapters 6 to 9 are the price of chapters 11 to 15.

## 2. Against the comparisons, for prose

**Below, by one point.** It is level or above from chapter 11 to chapter 15 and in every line of speech. It is below in the narrator and in the middle.

1. **Crouch changes his sentences when the stakes change. This book does not.** Mean sentence length in narration is between 11.3 and 15.8 words in all eighteen chapters. A sentence joined with ", and" or ", but" is about 30% of narration in every chapter (19% to 40%). Sentences of thirty words or more are 1% to 3%. Chapter 14, the flood, runs at 12.7 words a sentence. Chapter 2, a committee meeting, runs at 14.5. The timestamps in chapter 14 do the accelerating, and the sentences under them keep the pace of the council room.
2. **Crouch's reader is never ahead of the hero, and The Good Place's reversal re-reads everything before it.** Chapter 11 does the second thing at the level of the comparison: "'Nine minutes to three,' she said. 'I've known that for twelve years.'" re-reads the landing, the open door and every "Madame le maire". Chapter 10 cannot do it, because the opening page already showed the phone.
3. **The dialogue is above Crouch and level with The Good Place.** Each speaker has a syntax of their own: Larrère's agenda nouns ("The winch." "The lights."), Jeannot's numbering, Sarthou's "I shall require it", Marie-Jo's "through the chair", Coralie reading a sentence back like a dose off a chart. Cover the speech tags and every line can still be assigned. Crouch has nothing like it.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. The opening shows the answer, and the night is then told four times (00, 2, 10, 15)

> Then she took out her phone. The number was already in it. (00)

> 'Did you know he was out?' (ch 2)

> 'That's not a no.' (ch 9)

By chapter 2 the reader knows she looked down the track and rang somebody. For eight chapters the book asks a question the reader can answer, and chapter 10 confirms it in sentences already read: "She got out and put her hand flat on the bank beside the water, and the clay moved under her palm" is the opening's sentence with two words changed. The only new facts in chapter 10 are the reflector, the unwaited second ring, the four minutes and the wader. "Ninety" appears 12 times, "forty houses" 12 times, "No one came to tell him" 5 times.

**Do:** end the opening at "She looked down that track while the wipers crossed the windscreen twice." Cut the phone. In chapter 10, cut the approach to the bank to one clause and go from Larrère's call to the headlights. "It rang once" is then read for the first time in chapter 10, said aloud in chapter 11 and thrown back in chapter 13. Keep the word-for-word echo for chapter 14 only, where it is another woman on the same bank and recognition is the point.

### 2. The possession chapters are built from a kit of five sentences (6 to 9)

> Lesca waited for the arm. Then Lesca got the left shoulder under the handle and pushed with the legs. (ch 6)

> Cold came down the stairs (ch 6) / Cold came in under the council room door (ch 7) / Cold came against the bar (ch 8) / Cold came in under the kitchen door (ch 9)

Four fetches announced by the same three words. Four fights for the jaw in near-identical wording (ch 4, 6, 8, 13). "'Inguma, enauk—'" cut off at the bar in chapter 8 and cut off again at the kitchen table six hundred words later. Jeannot swears to be in his blind on the eleventh in chapter 6 and swears it again in chapter 8, and chapter 9 has to say "He's sworn it twice." "Lesca" is the grammatical subject 38 times in chapter 6's 2,500 words, because the narrator will not trust "she". This is where a Crouch reader puts the book down.

**Do:** one jaw fight in chapter 6, not two (keep the breakfast "Either", cut the one at Larrère's window and let the bandaged hand do the apologising alone). One public vow from Jeannot, in the hall. A different arrival for each fetch. Once a paragraph has said who is driving, write "she"; keep "the left hand" and "the mouth", which work, and stop naming Lesca at the head of consecutive sentences.

### 3. One tempo for a vote, a joke and a drowning (all, worst in 13 and 14)

> She climbed onto the top of the bank and pointed the torch at the river gauge. Eighty centimetres over the line, and falling as she watched. The river was coming down by itself, because it was going through a gap as wide as a house, into the Port. (ch 14)

> Marie-Jo had been Lesca's deputy mayor until a night in 2018, when they fell out over the budget for the village festival and Marie-Jo handed her sash back across the council table in a plastic bag. (ch 2)

The second is the right shape for its job. The first is the worst moment of Maylis's life, and it stops for a "because" clause. The figures are under section 2.

**Do:** in chapters 13 and 14, cut the causal clauses and the ", and" joins while she is moving. In the comic scenes, allow the long set-up sentence more often, so the short one after it lands. A reader should be able to tell the water from the council room by the shape of the sentences with the nouns covered.

### 4. The narrator explains the beat the scene has just landed (throughout)

> In the night she had told a woman in a van to go to the blind first. She could not ask that of her and then read this sentence. (ch 15)

The confession is the book's hinge, and the narrator gives the reason for it one line before it happens. The same habit:

- "Ticking a box didn't look hard, and she wanted to be able to tick one and leave the other empty." (ch 1)
- "Lesca had not meant to say that. In that room it came out." (ch 5)
- "At nine she had watched Larrère make a threat, and now she knew how it was done." (ch 7)
- "So he would never give that order, here or at home. That left the prefect." (ch 7)
- "Maylis had not known about Bayonne. She had never asked Coralie what she wanted." (ch 8)
- "Friday was the eleventh. On the night the river came through the bank, Coralie would be at the care home, right under it." (ch 9)
- "Lesca meant to drive this van to the blind. Maylis meant to keep it at the gate, for Coralie." (ch 13)
- "Minutes ago she had fought Lesca to keep this van off the track, and now she was taking it down herself." (ch 14)

**Do:** cut all nine. Nothing is lost in any case. Crouch does not annotate, and neither does the narrator of chapter 12, who writes "that was the reason she would give herself afterwards" and leaves it.

### 5. Both endings arrive as minutes and an agenda (16, opening of 17)

> At two the young man from Pau read out the prefect's order suspending her. Tastet was the deputy mayor, and she took off the sash and laid it in his hand with her keys. (ch 16)

The sash is what Maylis wanted in chapter 3 and admits wanting in chapter 17. Its surrender is a subordinate clause. The Bayonne statement and the vote that removes her are five sentences. Then the last meeting of the two women settles six items in about 230 words: the forty-one, the sum, the stairs, the sash, the place laid, the verb.

> 'I wanted your sash,' Maylis said, and she had not meant to say that either. (ch 17)

Chapter 16's last word is "Maylis", said to Lesca in bed. Chapter 17's first sentence is "On Saturday night Maylis lay down on her back in the middle of the bed", three nights earlier and in the other village. For a paragraph the reader does not know which woman or which night.

**Do:** give the sash six lines of scene. Cut the lieutenant's paragraph to a line. In the chair, drop the two items the reader already holds (the verb, from chapter 16, and "So it called twelve", from chapter 15) and write the stairs and the sash at the length of the chapter 5 meeting. Open chapter 17 on something only the keyholder's village has: the silt, or the forty-one.

### 6. The rules are restated in narration, and the one paragraph that carries the central coincidence is the hardest in the book (4, 10, 13, 15)

> It had come to the same bed in this village too, where nobody had made a call. For twelve years it had found Maylis asleep at the edge of the bed and nobody in the middle, and since the cracked rib it had sat on Maylis as well. (ch 10)

I read this three times. It also raises a question it does not answer: why it came to a bed in a village where nobody rang. Chapter 5 states the rules once, as a meeting, and it is funny. After that the narrator says them again each time one is used: "The alarm had woken Maylis and taken her home. Lesca had stayed sitting, and so Lesca had gone with her." (ch 4) "A place had been laid for her, and Inguma would not put her down until somebody filled it." (ch 15)

**Do:** in chapter 10 keep three sentences: the time on the phone, the time it comes, and "She had never had a time of her own." Cut the restatements in 4 and 15. In chapter 13 the reader needs to know before the van scene, not after it, that holding Inguma down counts as its failing to fetch. Give that to Madame Etcheto in chapter 3, in her voice.

### 7. The narrator steps out to define things in an encyclopaedia voice (1, 3, 16)

> Ducks only come down at night onto standing water, and a hand of water is enough, the depth of a palm laid flat. But on Monday the river had been low, and when the river is low, a raised gate lets the water run off the meadows. So raising the gate had drained his blind. (ch 1)

Four sentences of hydraulics in the present tense, between two lines of Jeannot. Also: "The pawl was the steel catch that held the winch after every turn." (ch 3), which follows a sentence that had already shown it clicking. And: "Every year the commemoration ended as a party, with a bar and a band on the bank, and at ten a bull made of fireworks would be lit and carried through the crowd." (ch 16), which spends the bull three paragraphs before it burns. "Because" appears 57 times.

**Do:** give the definition to the person who would give it. Jeannot would explain a hand of water to anybody, numbered. Cut the pawl sentence and the bull forecast outright.

### 8. Sixty per cent of scenes open like a logbook (throughout)

> At eight Lesca stood Maylis on the platform / At nine Guy Larrère stopped his car / At noon Lesca walked Maylis into the café / At seven there was soup (ch 6)

54 of 88 scene openings begin with a clock time, a weekday or the weather. Twelve of eighteen chapters do. In chapters 12 to 14 the minutes are the plot and the stamps are right. Elsewhere it reads as a diary, and it gives every scene the same first beat.

**Do:** keep the stamps where the date is the threat (the gauge lines, chapters 12 to 14). Open the rest on speech or action, as chapters 6, 7 and 11 already do: "'Nobody,' Lesca said."

### 9. Default beats (throughout)

> Coralie stood still, with the list in her hand. (ch 12)

> He looked at the seven, and then at the floor of the van. (ch 14)

"Looked at" 65 times, "looked" 107. "Then" opens a sentence 89 times. "Nobody" 71. A sentence ends in ", with" plus a noun phrase 102 times. "The way" plus a small comparison 28 times ("the way a dog does", "the way you hold a door shut"). "Had" 602. Each is invisible alone. Together they are why the narration reads as one voice at one distance.

**Do:** a pass for each. Half the looks can go, because the next line of speech shows what was seen. Recast one in two of the ", with" tails as their own sentence or cut them.

### 10. Usage and continuity

- **Contractions in narration follow no rule.** "she could not move" and "She wanted to scream and she couldn't" are eight lines apart in chapter 1. Totals: did not 28, didn't 41; could not 13, couldn't 23; had not 27, hadn't 5; was not 12, wasn't 17. It does not track which woman holds the point of view. Pick one rule.
- **"Truck"** four times (ch 4, 11, 14) in a text that says pavement, tarmac, torch, windscreen and wheelie bin. Lorry.
- **"Phoned"** three times (ch 1, 14, 17) against "rang" 35 times.
- **"Round"** as a preposition in narration twice ("Jeannot came round on his stool", ch 9; "wound it round", ch 7) against "around" 44 times.
- **"Three times you told me to put it in forty-two"** (ch 17). On the page she says it twice (ch 1, ch 6).
- **Jeannot's phone.** In chapter 10 he answers "outdoors", with "rain falling on a hood", then calls himself "a man in a hole in a meadow". In chapter 12 the phone went off "in the blind".
- **"So the one who stays sitting rides the one who wakes"** (ch 5). In a book about two women and one bed, "rides" has a second meaning. "Drives" is already the book's word: "she was the one driving" (ch 7).

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Each speaker's syntax, and all of Coralie's lines.** "'I don't cook Either,' she said. 'There's no recipe for it.'" (ch 1) "'It reads that she opened him.'" (ch 2) "'Eleven years I've been timed like an egg.'" (ch 7)
2. **The phone log in chapter 11.** "'Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one.'" and "'Because you'd have told me.'" This is the reversal that re-reads the book.
3. **The platform at five in chapter 12.** "'Either,' Maylis said. / 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. 'That's how the vote left it.'" With the reflector paragraph repeated from chapter 10 and the three-word test after it: "She was awake."
4. **Chapter 14 from the yard to the last line.** Monsieur Daguerre's "Here" to the roof of the van, "'Then they'll want the bins out'", Kévin counting five in his own headlights, the hatbox on the wardrobe, "'Forty-one.'"
5. **The last three scenes of chapter 17.** The oven door that will not do the voice, "the car door shutting on a pillow", Sarthou's "I do not record that I would have stayed", letter 42 that "doesn't thank you", and the second shovel.
