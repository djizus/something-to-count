# Round 21 — Reader

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else opened except the brief.

Overall against the bar: **7.4. Just under.** Chapters 12 to 15 are at the bar. Chapters 1 to 3 are not.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes — 8.** "*That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*" (ch. 4). A man, a street and a marriage are all at risk, and the threat to the marriage wears the heroine's face.
- **Tension — 7.** "She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it broke." (ch. 14). Chapters 12 to 14 are a 9. Chapters 1 to 3 are a 5, because nothing in them can go wrong yet.
- **Progression — 6.5.** "'The keyholder has no preference,' Larrère said, and the member who kept the minute book wrote that down." (ch. 3). That is the third *Either* in three chapters, and still nobody has crossed.
- **Comedy — 7.5.** "The window went down. It went all the way down, into the door. In eighteen years beside that car Maylis had never seen it do that." (ch. 6). The laughs come from people, not lines. There are fewer per page than the comparison.
- **Characters — 8.** "'I don't cook Either,' she said. 'There's no recipe for it. Soup or duck.'" (ch. 1). Coralie is alive from her first speech, in both villages.
- **The switch between dark and comic — 8.** "'Five,' he said." (ch. 14). A running joke about counting fingers becomes the moment a boy checks whether the flood is a dream.
- **Prose — 7.5.** "Lesca kept the eyes on it for longer than a cut needed." (ch. 6). It reads fast and plain. The body-part grammar of the possession scenes is clear every time.
- **The ending — 8.** "She heard the passage, and the front door, and the car door shutting on a pillow." (ch. 17). It costs a death, a job and a marriage, and the wrong woman keeps hers.
- **Clarity — 6.** "'A winch doesn't make it rain.' 'I want somebody to blame.'" (ch. 5). The book asks its own biggest question and then declines to answer it.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. I was at risk of stopping in the middle of ch. 2 and again in the middle of ch. 3. From the last line of ch. 4 there was no risk.

**Press it on a friend?** Yes, with the warning "give it four chapters". I would not give it to a friend who reads only Crouch. That reader stops before the first crossing.

## 2. Against the comparisons, as a reader

**Below** for chapters 1 to 3, **level** for 4 to 11, **level or above** for 12 to 15. Net: slightly below.

1. **Speed and legibility of the premise: below Crouch.** His rule can be repeated to a friend after thirty pages. Here I can repeat how the riding works ("the one who stays sitting rides the one who wakes", ch. 5). I cannot say why the flood has come to the second village, and the book has four separate briefings on its rules in chapters 3 to 5.
2. **Fear for the marriage: level, arguably above.** Crouch makes the impostor a better husband. This book does the same in one line, said with the wife's back turned: "I'd have this one again." (ch. 6). Then it tops it on the landing in ch. 8.
3. **Reversals that re-read what came before: level with The Good Place.** Nine minutes to three is the minute of the phone call (ch. 10). Coralie has known for twelve years (ch. 11). The clock ticking behind Larrère's order is in the Castagnet kitchen (ch. 10, ch. 14). All three sent me back through earlier chapters.

### What I did not understand

- **Why the flood comes now.** The second village had a dry 2014. Twelve years later its river follows the first village's file "day for day". Maylis asks why, in ch. 5, and gets a joke.
- **How the gate works.** Raising it drains the meadows when the river is low and floods them when the river is high. I held that by about ch. 5. I never understood why opening it on a falling tide in daylight fixes the night, and took it on trust.
- **Why the river has a tide.** Nobody says the sea is near.
- **Who can order the gate up.** Larrère is mayor and president in one village and only president in the other. The statutes are read out in ch. 7. I kept the gist: he won't, the prefect could.
- **Who won at the bar in ch. 8.** I read Madame Etcheto's finished prayer as a rescue. It is the opposite. I worked that out a scene later.
- **What "long enough" meant in ch. 13.** "She had kept Inguma from fetching her for eight rings of a phone, and that had been long enough." Long enough for what? The answer is in a joke in ch. 3.
- **Whether the lamp experiment matters.** The envelope is to be kept "until someone asked". Nobody asks.
- **"*Paid.*"** at the foot of the handover note (ch. 9). I did not know who had paid whom.

### Where I was bored, lost or skimming

- Ch. 1, the postman and the registered letter: one joke, forty lines, on the third page.
- Ch. 3, the Friday winch inspection.
- Ch. 4, the envelope and the card, which I had just watched in ch. 3.
- Ch. 7, the reading of the 1962 statutes.
- Ch. 9, the association vote, until Jeannot's hand went up.
- Ch. 17, the opening scene in the steel room.

### Where I could not stop

End of ch. 3 through ch. 6 without a break. The landing in ch. 8. Ch. 10 from the reflector to "Bugger off". Ch. 11 from "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile." Ch. 12 from the handle to ch. 14's "Forty-one", in one sitting.

### What I will remember in a month

The car window going all the way down. Coralie doing Larrère's voice into the oven, and in the last chapter starting it and not being able to finish. Two fingers on a wrist. Bixente reported for giving the demon two lots of stars. "And six. Bugger off." The red reflector going out. "Drowned him" arriving late from the second loudspeaker. "I wanted six, Maylis." The bus fare in coins that was not enough. A car door shutting on a pillow.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The first crossing comes after three chapters of load. (ch. 1–3, structural)**
"The blind was his duck-hunting blind, a cellar under a hatch out on the flood meadows. Ducks only come down at night onto standing water, and a hand of water is enough, the depth of a palm laid flat. But on Monday the river had been low, and when the river is low, a raised gate lets the water run off the meadows. So raising the gate had drained his blind."
Before a hand moves by itself, I have been asked to carry a sleep demon, a physics experiment, a registered letter, a winch, a duck blind, a commemoration committee, a kitchen timer, a verb, a gravel lake, eel poaching, lucid dreaming and a Basque prayer. In the same stretch the heroine does one thing three times: she says *Either*. The first person who wants something badly is Lesca, in the last line of ch. 4. That is where the book starts for a reader.
*Do:* cut three things. The postman scene by a third. The winch inspection in ch. 3 to its last exchange. And the second envelope-and-card discovery: ch. 3 has "She read the card, looked away, and read it again. *right*", and ch. 4 then spends a page on "She looked away… Then she read the card again. *left*". Keep Lesca's reasoning, give Maylis's in two lines, or the reverse. Also move the 2014 barn flashback in ch. 1 out of the middle of the attack. The demon is on her chest and the book leaves for four paragraphs.

**2. Four briefings on the rules, and the two rules that carry the ending are the two not stated. (ch. 3–5, 13, structural)**
"'Why would it come now?' 'It's the winch,' Lesca said. 'It stuck, and it put you on your back in that bed.' … 'A winch doesn't make it rain.' 'I want somebody to blame.'"
Kévin, Madame Etcheto, Arlette and Lesca's three-point agenda each explain the demon. None says why the second river keeps the first one's dates. The answer is on the page: the rain starts the first night both women lie under it, and "whatever one of us has in her head comes to the chair, and the other one gets it". The book offers it and takes it back as Lesca's blame-seeking. So at five o'clock in ch. 12, when Maylis doubts the flood will come, I doubt it for the wrong reason: I do not know the rule either.
The second rule is "a place that has been laid is filled by morning, by her or by another" (ch. 3). It is said once, inside a joke about bingo, ten chapters before it kills someone.
*Do:* let Lesca's answer stand, in the room where things have to be true. Cut "I want somebody to blame" or move it after Maylis has accepted the rule. Make "or by another" Lesca's fourth point in ch. 5, or give it to Arlette in ch. 11 when her daughter says she will sit on the thing.

**3. The choice the ending hangs on gets one clause. (ch. 14, structural)**
"Lesca had rung once and turned the handle, and Maylis thought she could do better."
A page earlier Maylis pulled the handbrake and said "Coralie" to keep the van at the gate. Now she drives it down the track. The text notices this ("Minutes ago she had fought Lesca to keep this van off the track, and now she was taking it down herself") but noticing is not a reason. Everything in ch. 17 rests on this turn: "I know what you picked. I was the other one."
*Do:* give the three breaths beside the winch what the five o'clock *Either* got in ch. 12. The reason exists and is hers: at five that morning she let a phone ring nine times and heard the man alive. Two sentences, at the window, with the handle level with her eye.

**4. The running forms run past the point of pleasure. (ch. 3, 7, 9, 17)**
"'Those in favour of the petition.' Three hands went up at the card table."
There are eight scenes built as a meeting with a motion or a vote: committee, inspection, the chair in ch. 5, council, the café, the chair in ch. 9, council again, the chair in ch. 17. *Either* is said about ten times. "A gate is a door in a bank" comes three times. Each is funny at its best ("Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station."). By ch. 9 I knew the shape of the scene before it began and skimmed to the result.
*Do:* lose the inspection as a meeting (fault 1). Lose the motion-speak in ch. 17 (fault 7). Keep one *Either* in ch. 3, not two. Say the door-and-tap line once in ch. 2 and once in ch. 12, where it is changed.

**5. I lost track of who was who in which village. (ch. 2, 4, 7, 11)**
"'That's not for a night's sleep,' she said. 'Nobody gives it that much to sleep, Maylis.'" (ch. 4)
About twenty-five named people in a short novel, most of them in two versions with different jobs. Kévin is a poacher in one and an ex-prisoner with a mower in the other. Coralie is a care assistant and a director. Sarthou is serving and retired. Larrère is mayor and ex-mayor. The narrator has trained me that "Maylis" is the other woman, and then Arlette and Coralie use it for Lesca. I stopped in ch. 4 to check which kitchen I was in.
*Do:* do not change the name; the last line of ch. 16 needs it. Anchor it the first time, in ch. 4, with a clause saying that her mother has never called her anything else. Open every scene in a crossing chapter with the weather, which already does this job where it is used: rain for one village, frost for the other.

**6. The water and the chain of command are harder than they need to be. (ch. 1, 5, 7)**
"A gate is raised on the written order of the president of the association. Or it is raised on the order of the prefect, sent down through the mayor. The keyholder turns the handle."
The whole plot is one gate, and I could not have drawn it until ch. 12. Larrère's door-and-tap speech is the nearest thing to an explanation and explains nothing.
*Do:* one plain picture, early, in Maylis's own words as the person who works it: river on one side, meadows on the other, lower; gate up, the river goes where the grass is. One clause that the sea is twenty minutes away and pushes the river up twice a day. Then the statutes can be cut to the two phrases the fingers land on.

**7. The last meeting in the steel room is a checklist. (ch. 17)**
"'Forty-one,' Maylis said. 'The one who wasn't in the hall was room twelve. So you were right, that night. Turn the handle first. I move that I sign your sum.'"
Twelve lines dispose of the sum, the stairs, the sash, the laid place and the verb. Two of those I had already been given in ch. 16 ("I heard twelve called", "*Left*"). It reads as the book tidying up.
*Do:* keep the two things neither woman meant to say (the stairs, the sash), and the demon leaving by the door. Cut the verb and "So it called twelve".

**8. The bar scene does not show who lost. (ch. 8)**
"Maylis held on, and the cold kept coming over the bar, and for one breath she had won."
Madame Etcheto then finishes the prayer and sets the demon counting maize. That keeps Lesca in the body all night and hands her the landing. It is the best irony in the chapter and nothing on the page marks it. The scene ends on Lafitte's spelling joke.
*Do:* half a line from inside Maylis as the cold goes away from the bar.

**9. Chapter 11 opens on a hook that needs a paragraph of explanation. (ch. 11)**
"'Nobody,' Lesca said."
It answers a question asked at the end of ch. 9, with all of ch. 10 in between. The next paragraph has to explain what the question was. Then ch. 12 goes back four hours. Around the climax I was reading the calendar as well as the story.
*Do:* open on Arlette in the office door, who is the answer. Let Lesca say "Nobody" to herself after her mother has gone.

**10. The envelope is a plant that never goes off. (ch. 1, 4, 17)**
"…had told her to write one word, seal it and keep it with her until someone asked."
"Nobody had asked her for the envelope since" (ch. 1) makes me wait for somebody to ask. Maylis carries the card to the last page. Nobody asks, and the university never returns. A reader who came for the science feels it was dropped.
*Do:* either let someone ask in the last chapter, in a line, or cut "until someone asked" and "nobody had asked her for the envelope since".

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 6, all of it.** The window into the door, "It takes a small m", the spoon tipped back into the bowl, "I'd have this one again", the hand on the stair post.
2. **The landing in ch. 8.** "That's her… In this hand." and "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them."
3. **Kévin's fingers.** The lesson in the loader bucket (ch. 3), "Five" in the flood (ch. 14), "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much." (ch. 17).
4. **The handle at five o'clock in ch. 12.** "At three it would be the river's doing. At five it was hers." Then "Either", and "Then it's shut."
5. **Coralie and the phone in ch. 11.** "'Why not?' 'Because you'd have told me.'" It explains the landing, the eleven years and the two bedrooms in six words.
