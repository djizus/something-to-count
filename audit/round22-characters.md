# Critic report: Characters (round 22)

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "One woman could turn the winch, or she could reach that hatch. She couldn't do both." (8) The two ends of the choice are both people the reader knows. But Coralie, who is the stake at the gate end, is never in danger on the page: when Maylis reaches the care home she is standing in the doorway reading a list.
- **Tension: 7.5.** "She stood like that, one step from her own bedroom door, with a pulse under her fingers and the left hand on her pillow." (8) The book is most tense on a landing, not on a bank.
- **Progression: 6.5.** "Coralie had the two of them in front of her, in one chair, and it didn't take her long." (9) The strongest threat in the book, a woman taking another woman's body and bed, is ended in twenty lines of breakfast with eight chapters to go.
- **Comedy: 8.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (7) The people are funny in themselves, each by one fixed habit.
- **Characters: 7.5.** "'I'd have this one again.'" (6) The cast is alive and the two women are distinct. The mark stops there because the one decision the book exists for is made without a want on the page (fault 1).
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'" (14) Monsieur Daguerre's bingo call, set up as a joke twice, becomes the first thing said in the rescue.
- **Prose: 7.** "Then the water rose up the door and covered it, and the red light went out." (10) Plain and easy to follow. Every speaker has the same gift for the finished retort (fault 8).
- **The ending: 7.** "'Good night, Maylis,' she said, and she went into the spare room." (16) Lesca's ending is an 8.5. Maylis's is a 6.5: what her choice cost has been shared out among four people before she reaches the grave (fault 3).
- **Clarity: 6.** "'Then it wasn't Inguma that came for her. It was you.'" (16) I had to stop and work out that the old woman took Lesca's seat among the dead. The same is true of why Lesca stands up in 13, and of what Maylis wants at the dip in 14.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. From chapter 3 I did not consider stopping.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On one who likes village comedy, yes. On one who came for Dark Matter, not yet: the choice at the centre does not land as hers.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Against Crouch: above on people, below on the protagonist's drive.**

- Above: the marriage is specific. Daniela in Dark Matter is what Jason wants back. Coralie is a person who refuses: "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." Two fingers on a wrist and a shopping pad do more than Crouch's family dinner.
- Level: the fear for the marriage in 6 and 8. "In sixteen years Maylis had never got that laugh out of her. Lesca had got it in ten minutes, with Maylis's mouth." That is Jason watching Jason2 in his kitchen, and it is as good.
- Below: Jason's want never blurs, so every choice he makes is legible at speed. At the dip the text gives Maylis no want at all, and the reader gets it three chapters later, for a different moment.

**Against The Good Place: level on the village, below on the central pair.**

- Level: everyone is wrong in their own way. Larrère governs through a gap in a car window. Jeannot numbers his grievances. Josiane has a theorem ("It's adultery either way, and that one lives with a nurse"). Coralie knowing the minute for twelve years re-reads every scene on that landing, which is a reversal of the right kind.
- Below: Eleanor and Chidi change each other by liking each other. Maylis and Lesca meet four times and trade procedure and guilt. Neither ever looks at the other with envy, tenderness or grief for more than a line, so the last meeting is an audit.

**The questions set for this lens.**

- *Distinct?* Yes. One writes *Either*, the other says "It takes a small m." They cannot be confused for a sentence.
- *Wanted-for?* Yes, in plain statements ("Maylis wanted the duck"), except at the one place it matters most.
- *Softened or excused?* Lesca, no: "I wouldn't have stopped." Maylis, yes, by the plot and not by herself (fault 3).
- *Described from outside as a woman?* No. Their bodies are a rib, a palm, a fist. The village says "little Lesca" and "the village's girl"; the narrator never does.
- *Who would be missed?* Jeannot, Madame Etcheto, Kévin, Larrère's window, Maïté. Not missed: Marie-Jo until her last two lines, Tastet, Fernand, Père Honoré.
- *Which relationship carries the book?* Maylis and Coralie. It pays, at a price, in 17. The one that pays best is the other: Lesca and the Coralie who knew.
- *Does the reader care who lives?* Jeannot, entirely, from "And six. Bugger off." Madame Etcheto, yes. Coralie, less than the book needs, because she is never at risk in view.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. Chapter 14, with 13 and 17. The central choice has no want on the page.**

> "Coralie had asked her to say which, for once.
> Maylis turned her back on the lamp and went on."

This is the moral choice of the book: the van is stuck, "both" is over, the winch is six minutes behind her and the man four hundred metres ahead. A book that has stated every want in one flat sentence ("She wanted Coralie to stay awake") goes silent here. The reader is told that she picks and not what she wants.

Two things make it worse. Two pages earlier she fought the other way: "Lesca meant to drive this van to the blind. Maylis meant to keep it at the gate, for Coralie." She pulls the handbrake and says "Coralie", then drives past the winch on one sentence ("Maylis thought she could do better than that"). And the confession in 17 ("I drove past the handle to be the one who did") covers the drive past the handle, when both still seemed possible. It does not cover the dip. "I had to pick one. I picked" is the same silence, said aloud.

What I would do: decide what she wants at the dip and say it there, in her idiom, in two sentences. Then make the fight in the van about who drives, not about where to. She does not need to be for the gate in 13; she needs to be against being driven. As it stands the heroine reverses in two pages and the reason arrives after the funeral is arranged.

**2. Chapters 5 to 10. Lesca is seen only from behind for the whole middle.**

> "Lesca looked at him with Maylis's eyes and kept looking, and Maylis couldn't turn them away. She felt her own face smile at him."

Chapter 2 promised this meeting: "the guess of a woman who had wanted that man alive for twelve years". When she finally stands in front of Jeannot alive, the book is in the other woman's head, and the next line is about a tank of diesel. The same goes for the soup, her first meal at a table with Coralie in eleven years. For six chapters the more active of the two women has no inside, and she turns from a person into the thing that drives Maylis. Her last three chapters have to rebuild her.

What I would do: give the café at noon in 6 to Lesca. One section, her view, the man on the stool. Keep the soup and the stairs where they are, in Maylis, because "I'd have this one again" only works from the hand under the tea towel.

**3. Chapters 14, 16 and 17. The cost of Maylis's choice is shared out until nobody holds it.**

> "'It's no use to anybody. Have it back.'" (Jeannot, the winch having stuck at nineteen under a man with two arms)
> "'That part is mine.'" (Coralie)
> "'And you're not having all of room twelve.'" (Lesca)

Lesca's guilt is one ring, one handle, one man. Maylis's is a committee. Madame Etcheto refused the chair herself. Coralie would not wake her. The list that sent the staff to the wrong end was Lesca's. The seat among the dead was Lesca's, so the death is also a piece of magic. And the winch stuck for Jeannot, so the gate may not have opened for a woman with one arm and a second broken rib. Each speaker says the right thing ("I'm not telling you this so that you'll carry less of yours"), and Maylis refuses the alibi ("That one's yours. I never tried it"), but the plot has already handed it to her. The victim is also the cheapest available: ninety-four, in the bed she chose.

What I would do: let the handle turn for Jeannot, too late. Then the gate could have been opened and the choice is whole. Keep Coralie's share, because it is the reason she leaves. Cut Lesca's claim on room twelve in 17; the scene in 16 with the old woman has already made it, better.

**4. Chapter 9. Coralie's choice between the two women takes one sentence.**

> "Coralie had the two of them in front of her, in one chair, and it didn't take her long."

Three chapters have built to this. She has said she would have "this one again" and "I'd have you tomorrow." Lesca says "This time I've come to stay." Then Coralie pins the left wrist, and the possession plot is over by the bottom of the page. It is the love story's deciding act and it is given as a scuffle. Lesca has no pursuit of her own from here until the van in 13.

What I would do: give the decision its beat. What does she see in the hand over the mouth that she did not have on the landing? One look, one line, then the wrist. It is the only place where the book hurries Coralie.

**5. Chapters 5, 8 and 17. Coralie's own want is delivered as her exit.**

> "'I held my first hand on it, at nineteen, under a night sister called Ithurbide, who ran thirty beds with a torch and a list. I wanted to be her before I'd heard of Peyrebarthe.'"

This is the best thing we learn about her, and it comes while she is packing. Before it there is an envelope "that had been standing there since March" and one sentence on the landing. In both villages she is otherwise built around the other woman's defect: sixteen years of drawing boxes here, eleven years across a landing there. She is so vivid that the reader does not notice until she goes, and then the going reads as the plot's need.

What I would do: move Ithurbide to the landing in 8, where she first says Bayonne. Show her once, early, running a night the way she would run that floor; the corridor in 3 is the place. Then her leaving is a want the reader has backed against Maylis, and "That's what you'd keep" has something to cut.

**6. Chapter 13. Why Lesca stands up.**

> "*That the mayor remain seated.*
> In the steel room the mayor stood up."

She has crossed for a bed with two pillows and sworn she will not go home. Between the seventh ring and the eighth she gives it all up. The materials are there: the right hand "locked on it, cut palm and all" repeats the stair post, and "Mine was under it too." But the reader must assemble them at the fastest point in the book, and the renunciation is hers, the largest act she performs in the present.

What I would do: one sentence at ring seven that names what she sees in the hand on the brake. It is the same hand that held the post against her. She should know it.

**7. Chapters 1 to 17. The one straight answer is never shown.**

> "'Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you, and that was me.'"

That is the whole past of this marriage. Neither woman has a memory of the other from before 2014, a friend, or a thing she likes that is not her job. Crouch spends his first chapter on why the marriage is worth the fear. The eggs do that work for the present, but the book never proves Maylis can want something and say so, and it had the proof in hand.

What I would do: ten lines, in 1 or in 5 at the pad. The day she said it, and what she said.

**8. Throughout. Everyone speaks in the finished retort.**

> "'You'd have left me a pad.'" (Arlette, 9)
> "'I wouldn't want to hear the beginning.'" (Maïté, 5)
> "'I know that word. I've cooked it.'" (Coralie, 17)
> "'It's four when you're the one counting.'" (Darrigrand, 4)
> "'She isn't leaving the area.'" (Marie-Jo, 16)

Each is good. Together they are one voice with devices laid over it: numbered points, three questions, no contractions, "through the chair". Nobody fumbles, repeats, or says a thing beside the point. The exception proves it: Coralie at the oven, "'The—'", is the most alive she is in the book.

What I would do: take the closing retort away from two speakers. Arlette and Maïté would both be stronger plain. Let Coralie run out of sentence once more, in 14.

**9. Chapter 17. The village's verdict comes from a stranger.**

> "'You had the key,' Madame Darrigrand said."

Her only earlier appearance is "Madame Darrigrand signed it with her baby on her arm." The speech is the Port's judgement on Maylis and it is right. It should come from a mouth the reader knows.

What I would do: give her two lines at the petition in 9, or give the speech to Lafitte, whose house is under the bank, who wrote the petition and who has kept the pad on Maylis since chapter 6.

**10. Chapter 17. The last meeting of the two women is an audit.**

> "'That's yours, and I'm not carrying it for you. Five o'clock is yours as well.'"
> "'I won't tell you which of us did better.'"

Thirty lines allot blame item by item, and the scene only comes alive at "'Is yours in the house?'" / "'Across the landing. Four steps. Yours?'" / "'She took the pillow.'" That exchange is the first time either asks about the other's life. Also, at "'...the river up to my ribs.' 'So you were right.'" two speeches by Maylis are run together with a stray pair of quotation marks.

What I would do: cut the allotting by half. Keep Maylis's confession about the sash, Lesca's "I don't know", the stairs, and the three lines about the pillow.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 11, the phone.** "'Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one.'" and "'Because you'd have told me.'" The best scene in the book, and the reversal that re-reads the landing.
2. **Chapter 6 to 8, soup, stair post, landing.** The spoon tipped back into the bowl, "I'd have this one again", the hand locked on the post, and "'That's her,' she said. 'In this hand.'"
3. **Kévin's fingers.** Chapter 3, then "'Five,' he said" in the yard, then "'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much.'"
4. **Madame Etcheto, entire.** "Staff to wait. Staff not to join in." The duck on her knees. "'Nobody emptied hers.'"
5. **Jeannot on the phone at five.** Nine rings, six points, "'And six. Bugger off.'", and with it "'It's Jeannot. Jean's for the stone.'" He is why the reader cares who lives.
