# Critic's report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `manuscript/17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the care home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." (2) The sum is stated once, early, and never has to be restated.
- **Tension: 7.5.** "03:11. She had given the bank ten minutes. It had held for sixteen." (14) Chapters 12 to 14 hold. Chapters 8 and 9 do not: the date is known and the only question is who has the jaw.
- **Progression: 6.5.** "For two nights after that Maylis pushed up against Inguma with every breath she had, because pushing kept her out of the chair, and the chair was how Lesca got in." (8) Three rounds of the same fight for one mouth (6, 8, 9), each ending with Lesca sent home; and chapter 4 walks back through chapter 3.
- **Comedy: 7.** "Eleven years I've been timed like an egg." (7) Dry, exact, and built to pay off. One register, low volume, and the premise's own farce is mostly reported.
- **Characters: 8.** "I know that word. I've cooked it." (17) Coralie is the best thing in the book and the only person in it who performs a joke.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" (17) The same impression that got the first laugh in chapter 1 fails in the last chapter, and nobody remarks on it.
- **Prose: 7.5.** "Maylis hadn't told it to." (3) Plain and controlled. That construction and its cousins ("Maylis hadn't said it", "She hadn't told them to", "Maylis hadn't let them") do the same job four times.
- **The ending: 7.5.** "Then he stepped over the string in his waders and began to dig." (17) It costs: the funniest person in the book is drowned, the wittiest leaves with a pillow. The last steel-room meeting settles its accounts by statement.
- **Clarity: 6.** "Two. Whichever of us wakes first wakes at home. If the other one is still sitting here, she goes home with her." (5) The reader is asked to hold four rules, two villages, two Coralies, one free hand and a counting schedule, and the comedy pays for it.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes.
**Would I press it on a friend?** On the friend who likes a dry village novel, yes. On the friend who loves The Good Place and has been told this is a comedy, no. That friend smiles for five chapters and stops laughing at chapter 10.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below The Good Place as comedy. Level with it at one thing. Crouch is not a comic writer and is not the test here.**

**Below, because of range.** The Good Place gives four people four different ways of being wrong: selfishness, paralysis, vanity, stupidity. They cannot be mistaken for each other with the names removed. In Peyrebarthe nearly everybody is wrong the same way: they count, number, minute, file or tick. Jeannot numbers his grievances. Lesca moves motions. Sarthou speaks for the file. Marie-Jo counts chairs. Coralie draws boxes and rules charts. Lafitte keeps a pad. Madame Ducasse keeps the minutes. Hontaube has a form. Kévin counts fingers. Madame Etcheto sets counting as homework for a demon. It is the book's theme, the sum, turned into a cast, and it is why the book produces one kind of laugh. Only three jokes come from somewhere else: Coralie's impression of Larrère into the oven (mimicry), Josiane's "It's adultery either way, and that one lives with a nurse" (appetite for scandal), and Kévin's "I'd fly... Then I'd do my mum's gutter" (wanting something). Those three are also the three biggest laughs outside the council room.

**Below, because of how the premise is used.** The Good Place states its rule in a sentence and then the rule generates jokes for free. This book's rule, two women in one body with one hand each side, is as good a comic machine, and it runs at full power for one chapter and a half (6, the first two-thirds of 7). Elsewhere the struggle is narrated as effort ("threw herself at the jaw", "went for the mouth", "heaved at them") and nobody in the room sees it.

**Level, at the joke that turns out to be evidence.** The Good Place's reversals make earlier scenes re-read. So do these, and the mechanism is the comic runner:
- The stone that "reads that she opened him" (2) gets its verb in 16, "*Left*", which is also the word on the card in the envelope.
- "Twelve. One, two." / "That's my door" over a bingo duck (8) is the call that seats Madame Etcheto among the dead (15, 16).
- "I'll be brief", said through a car window in chapter 1 and mocked into an oven twice, is said at a garden gate in 16 and followed by nothing at all.
- "Either" is a joke about eggs in chapter 1 and the word that keeps a gate shut in chapter 12.

That is the moral problem made funny without being made small, and at that single skill the book matches its model. It does it quietly, with five or six runners, and with a narrator who never raises the volume. The Good Place does it loudly and at four times the rate.

**Are the people funny in themselves?** Larrère, Jeannot, Coralie, Madame Etcheto, Josiane, Marie-Jo, Madame Ducasse and the woman at Hontaube: yes, each from the first line. Lesca: yes, whenever she has somebody to chair. Maylis: no. She is the straight woman in her own half of the book, with one made joke ("Put it in forty-two") and one chapter of appetite (7).

**Do the scenes build or stop?** Build: the corridor outside room twelve (3), the chrysanthemums (5), the car window going all the way down (6), the mayor on her office floor (7), Larrère untimed (7), the association chaired from a driver's seat (9), the phone call to Hontaube (12). Stop: bingo night (8), the three-way wrestle at the breakfast table (9).

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The farce of one body is reported, not staged, and nobody is watching. (8, 9) Structural.**
"The jaw pulled against Maylis while the left hand filled a cup at the urn." "The ham was the last prize, and every number of it cost Maylis more than the one before."
Bingo night is the largest comic room in the book: the whole village, the care home, a duck with its feet out of the paper, a man who shouts "Here" one number short. In the middle of it a woman is fighting her own jaw at the bar, and the page gives that fight as cost and effort. No one sees her face. No one gets it wrong. The bingo jokes run beside the struggle and never through it. Then in chapter 9 the one moment when all three sides of the triangle are physically in the same gag, one woman gagging herself with a bandaged hand, the other dragging the hand off, a care assistant pinning a wrist across the bread with her full weight, is over in twelve lines and summarised: "Coralie had the two of them in front of her, in one chair, and it didn't take her long." Coralie, who restrains people for a living and is the wit of the book, says nothing while she does it.
**Do:** rebuild the bar scene around what the hall can see. Put a customer at the urn. Let the cup overflow. Let Josiane or Fernand read the clenched teeth as the Castagnet business, or a tooth, and say so. Keep Madame Etcheto finishing the prayer as the topper; it is the right ending and it would then land on a room that has been laughing. Give the kitchen wrestle a page and give Coralie a line in it.

**2. The whole cast runs on one comic engine. (throughout) Structural.**
"Which do I put?" (Lafitte, 6). "For the minutes. What do I put?" (Madame Ducasse, 16). The same joke from two mouths in two villages. Add "That is for the file" (Sarthou, 14), "I'll number it. One." (Jeannot, 9), "Tick one." (Coralie, 5), "There's a form, and I can post you one" (Hontaube, 12), "I counted them out of the hall at seven" (Marie-Jo, 15).
Each is good. Together they are one note, and by chapter 9 the reader can write the next one. Sarthou and Père Honoré also share a voice: the uncontracted official sentence ("I do not record that I would have stayed"; "I am not sure that they are a prayer").
**Do:** keep the clerks and promote the three who are not clerks. Josiane should have a second and third theory, each wronger; her last line, "I only had the one name wrong", deserves a longer run-up. Kévin should want things out loud in every scene he is in, three questions a sentence, as chapter 7 says he does and chapter 12 forgets. Bixente has a grandmother, a minibus and two lines. Take the formal diction away from either the gendarme or the priest.

**3. Every farce scene begins with arithmetic. (5, 7, 8) Structural.**
"Coralie was on the late shift on Wednesday, which meant no alarm would ring in the house at six. Lesca woke at six every day of her life." (7) "Inguma was away counting for Lesca. When it came, Lesca would need the mouth to send it off again, and Maylis meant her not to have it." (8)
To reach the funniest chapter in the book the reader must work out, from a shift rota, which woman wakes first and therefore who rides whom. To follow bingo night the reader must hold that the demon is out counting signatures, will arrive mid-scene, and can be re-sent only by the mouth. Farce needs the audience to know at a glance who wants what. A reader doing sums is not laughing.
**Do:** reduce the rule to two things that can be held: the one who stays in the chair rides the one who gets up; the demon fetches her when it finishes counting. Let the stayer simply decide to stay; cut the alarm-and-rota logic. Open each ridden scene the way chapter 6 does, which is why chapter 6 works: "Lesca had spoken first, with Maylis's mouth."

**4. Chapter 4 walks back through chapter 3, and the comedy stalls for it. (3, 4, 5)**
"*left* / The t was crossed low. It was her handwriting, and she had never in her life written that word on a card." (4), after "*right* / It was in her own handwriting, but the lamp she had seen from that chair was the left one." (3)
The envelope is opened twice, the warm chair is sat in twice, and "Come back across" is delivered three times: the end of 3, the middle of 4, and read back by Coralie at the top of 5. This comes straight after the corridor scene has made the reader laugh for the first time at full strength. Between Madame Etcheto's "With cards?" and Lesca's "All those in favour" there are nearly two hundred lines with two jokes in them (the washing-up, the doorstep).
**Do:** cut Lesca's envelope and first night in the chair to a paragraph. Start chapter 4 on "Rain was falling on the roof, and Lesca was lying under it on her back." The motion, Darrigrand's step and Arlette's kitchen then carry it.

**5. The hall of the dead has one comic idea and plays it straight. (9, 13) The dark smothers the joke.**
"They were the dead of Peyrebarthe, and Maylis had dug the graves for many of them." (9) "At every place there was a bingo card and a saucer of maize kernels, and at every place one of the village's dead sat with their head down." (13)
Madame Etcheto sets it up as a joke: "After that she plays bingo with the rest of the dead." "With cards?" The afterlife is the village hall on a Saturday, paper tablecloths and all. That is the most Good Place idea in the book. When it arrives it is solemn twice over, and it earns one laugh, Lesca's "You can put that in the minutes as apologies received." Nobody dead is petty, or one short, or cross about the prize. The reader is never told what the dead play for.
**Do:** let it be bingo. One of the dead calls a line early. Something is on the edge of the stage. Jean Castagnet with a card in each hand is the right kind of detail; there should be three more like it, and the hall will be more frightening for being recognisable.

**6. The book's most important joke is explained before it is told. (12)**
"She could open it now. Then the river would be on the meadows by dark, and it would be there on her word alone, against a vote and an order." Three paragraphs later: "The folder with Kévin's name in it was in the car on the bank, and that was the reason she would give herself afterwards." Then: "'Either,' Maylis said."
Everywhere else the word comes out as a reflex, faster than thought. Here, at the one place it kills somebody, it comes after a hundred and twenty words of reasons. The reader has finished understanding her before she speaks, so the word confirms where it should shock. The reasons are also given again in 17: "I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me."
**Do:** Jeannot asks, "Shut, or open?" The river goes by. "Either." "Then it's shut." He is down the steps. Then the reasons, in two sentences, while she lifts the handle off.

**7. A quip closes the scene where Coralie leaves the bed. (8) The joke weakens the dark.**
"She had worked the verges out at the speed of the village tractor. Inguma had done them on foot."
It is a good line and it is the punchline to chapter 7. It arrives one section late, directly after "Maylis couldn't promise her that it wouldn't happen again, and she wouldn't ask her back to lie beside that." Coralie has sat thirteen hours beside an open-eyed body with a doctor's number under her thumb, has taken her pillow out, and the section ends on a joke about tractor speed.
**Do:** move it to the floor of the mayor's office in chapter 7, at half past two, where Maylis is waiting for a weight that does not come. There it is the laugh the scene is missing. End the section in 8 on the pillow going down in the spare room.

**8. A runner carries off the fact the ending turns on. (14)**
"'I put that handle in number six.' 'I wrote to them too.' 'I know you did. They took note.'"
Jeannot has just reported that the winch stuck at nineteen under a man with two arms. That means the gate could not have been raised that night by anyone, which changes what Maylis's choice at the dip was worth. It is delivered as banter about registered letters, in a doorway, with the water rising, and nobody stops on it. It comes back only in 17, in a subordinate clause, and Maylis waves it away: "That one's yours. I never tried it."
**Do:** keep the exchange and give the fact one beat of its own first. She looks at the key in his palm. Then the letters.

**9. Runners announce themselves and callbacks are labelled. (5, 6, 9, 10, 14, 16)**
"I'll number that." (6) "I'll number it. One." (9) "Then I'll number it for you, since you'll want it in a letter." (10) And: "That's Either again, with more words." (1) "That's Either again, in pencil." (5)
In chapter 1 Jeannot simply starts, "One. A duck comes down at night onto standing water", and it is funny because he does not know he does it. From chapter 6 he tells us he is about to. Coralie's second "Either again" is the first one with a prop. Separately, the narrator points at callbacks the reader already has: "Maylis had seen her do that at the care home. She had had it done to her." (14, at a dead woman's wrist) and "She had said it once before, word for word, with another woman's mouth." (16)
**Do:** cut the three announcements; let him count. Cut "That's Either again, in pencil" and let the two ticked boxes stand. Cut both labelling sentences. The two fingers on the wrist in room twelve need nothing after them.

**10. Coralie hears the best line in the farce plot and never mentions it again. (6)**
"'Him,' Lesca said. 'I've had that man on my mind every day for twelve years.'" "'Twelve,' Coralie said, across the two bowls. 'And it's his wife who gets the chrysanthemums. I've had sixteen and a shopping pad.'"
The village's misreading, that the keyholder is after one Castagnet or the other, reaches the one person it should hurt, gets one reply, and is dropped. Jeannot carries it into his refusal at the hatch ("You were told the way to my kitchen as well"), so the plot uses it. Coralie does not, in eleven more chapters, though she now knows there is a mayor in the body and has every reason to ask the mayor what she meant.
**Do:** one line on the landing in chapter 8, where she is already sorting which of the two she is talking to: she asks the one who answers what Jean Castagnet is to her. Lesca does not answer. It plants chapter 11 from the other village.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie and the oven door, all three times.** "'In my tray,' she said, and shut the door on him." (1) "'Three against three. It stays shut,' she said, and shut the door on him." (9) "'The—'" (17). The third must stay unexplained.
2. **The corridor outside room twelve.** From "It's in the care plan" to "With cards?" (3), including "She said it had been told twice and given two lots of stars, and it got through them early and came at one" and "Staff to wait. Staff not to join in."
3. **Larrère untimed, and the mayor on her floor.** "I have sentences in that register with no ends on them." "I'm told I wave." And Madame Ducasse bending down to set the next letter beside the hand that signs it: "Council is at half past six." (7)
4. **The word at the winch and the answer to it.** "'Either,' Maylis said. 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. 'That's how the vote left it.'" (12) Move what comes before it. Leave those two lines.
5. **Kévin's hand.** "'Five,' he said." in the headlights at four in the morning (14), and "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much." (17)

## 5. Chapter by chapter

- **00.** A woman, a bank, a phone. No comedy and none wanted. "It rang once." **8**
- **01.** A mayor who tells the postman the town hall opens at two; eggs for Either; the oven. The book shows what it can do. **8**
- **02.** The stone that needs a verb and the timer that cuts off the widow. Heavy with the other village's history. **7**
- **03.** The corridor scene, then a mouth that speaks without its owner. The best chapter in the first third. **9**
- **04.** The same envelope and chair from the other side. Lands only in its last line, "and Coralie Mounet in it." **7**
- **05.** A meeting with nobody in the chair, and chrysanthemums carried past a café window. Farce starts. **8**
- **06.** The window goes all the way down; soup tipped back into the bowl; a hand on the stair post. Sustained. **9**
- **07.** The funniest chapter. A verge-cutter enjoys being mayor and a man talks for eleven minutes. **9**
- **08.** Bingo night misses. The landing after it does not. **7**
- **09.** A vote chaired from a parked car; a handover note that lists Coralie as a distance; a card with a name on it. **8**
- **10.** The night itself, told flat, then six points and "Bugger off." The switch at its cleanest. **8**
- **11.** No laughs. A call log read out like a chart. Needed, and slow to start. **7**
- **12.** Too long by its first third, but Hontaube's form and the word at the winch are in it. Ends on a ringing phone. **9**
- **13.** Two women fight over a steering wheel by the ring; one declines her seat among the dead for the minutes. **9**
- **14.** The evacuation. "Then they'll want the bins out." Forty-one. **9**
- **15.** A confession that arrives at the back of the square a moment late, and a man who claps. Ends on chairs. **8**
- **16.** "I'll be brief," and then nothing; a form with two boxes. Quiet after 15. **7**
- **17.** One pillow, one letter that does not thank her, one grave. As an ending: **7.5**
