## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below the bar on prose, by one point.** Sentence for sentence it is more exact than Crouch. It is slower than Crouch and less dense with jokes than The Good Place, and those are the two things the bar asks for.

1. **Crouch changes gear and this prose has one gear.** Narration sentences average 12.1 words, and every chapter sits between 10.6 and 13.8. The committee meeting (2) runs at 13.7 and the flood (14) at 11.4. Two sentences in a hundred reach thirty words. Crouch at a climax drops to one-line paragraphs and fragments. Chapter 13 does exactly that ("One ring." "Two." "Three.") and is the best-paced chapter in the book, which shows the writer can do it and mostly does not.
2. **Crouch has the premise working inside his narrator's panic by the end of chapter one.** Here the two women do not speak until chapter 5, about 11,000 words in, a quarter of the book. The first two chapters are written as a briefing.
3. **The Good Place makes its rules the joke.** Chapter 5 does this and is level with it: "'All those in favour.' 'There are two of us.' 'Then it's carried, or it's a tie, and in a tie the chair decides.' 'There's nobody in the chair.'" After chapter 5 the rules stop being jokes and come back as narrator's notes (8, 9, 13).

Above both comparisons at one thing: objects carry the marriage. The shopping pad, the pillow, the window gap and the kitchen timer do work that Crouch gives to interior monologue.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. Chapters 1 and 2 are a briefing. (Structural.)**
"To her the gate was a simple thing. The river was on one side of the bank, and the meadows were on the other side, and lower. Put the gate up, and the river went where the grass was. And the sea was only twenty minutes away, so twice a day the tide pushed the river up." (1) And: "The mayor was Guy Larrère, who was also president of the landowners' association, and the association owned the sluice gate on the flood meadows below the village. Maylis kept the key to its winch." (1)
After a first page that is frightening, the narrator stops to hand out facts in reading-primer sentences. Chapter 2 then repeats the opening in the other village and gives the whole 2014 flood as a set speech. The premise only bites in the last line of chapter 2's third scene.
Do: cut the hydraulics paragraph from chapter 1 and let the reader learn the gate when it is first worked (the association scene in 3, the test in 6). Let the postman scene carry Larrère's two offices, which it nearly does already. Move the end of chapter 3 ("'Come back across,' her mouth said. Maylis hadn't said it.") as early as the timeline allows. That line is where the book starts.

**2. The middle runs on rules the narrator explains at the moment they are used. (Structural.)**
"Maylis shut the teeth. Inguma was away counting for Lesca. When it came, Lesca would need the mouth to send it off again, and Maylis meant her not to have it." (8) "The ham was the last prize, and every number of it cost Maylis more than the one before." (8) "The paper tablecloths lifted along the first row, one table after another, on the way to the bar." (8)
The bingo scene turns on a rule the reader has never seen in action: that the visitor must say the words again when Inguma arrives. So the narrator states it, and the scene's fear becomes arithmetic. The third sentence quoted has no subject for what is on its way to the bar. Chapter 13 has the same note: "She had held Inguma down for eight rings of a phone, and that counted."
Do: show Lesca re-send Inguma once, plainly and aloud, at the kitchen table in chapter 6, where she only says "Next time I'll bring it more." Then cut the note in 8. Name Inguma as the thing lifting the tablecloths.

**3. The book's central failure is explained before it happens, and twice after. (Structural.)**
Before: "Or she could open it at three, when the water was coming through and nobody could say she had chosen. At three it would be the river's doing. At five it was hers. The folder with Kévin's name in it was in the car on the bank, and that was the reason she would give herself afterwards." (12) Then she says "Either." After: "She was waiting for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be her." (12) "'I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me.'" (17) "'Five o'clock is yours as well.'" (17)
"The reason she would give herself afterwards" is the only place in the book where the narrator knows the future. The last steel-room scene then turns the ending into an audit read aloud: "'I won't tell you which of us did better.'" "'That's yours, and I'm not carrying it for you.'" The book judges itself so the reader need not.
Do: at five o'clock keep her weight on the handle, Jeannot's question, "Either", and "Then it's shut." Cut the three paragraphs of reasoning. Keep the confession to Kévin in 17 as the one statement. Halve the steel-room scene: keep the sash and the square, "She took the pillow", Inguma going out over the sill, "Neither of them sat down."

**4. The caption after the beat, book-wide.**
I marked 28. The dead ones restate what an object has just shown: "In sixteen years Maylis had never got that laugh out of her. Lesca had got it in ten minutes, with Maylis's mouth." (6) "Maylis wanted Coralie alive on Saturday morning, and thirty metres from the patch was the only way she had found to write that down." (9) "He had two good arms and a van, and all Hontaube had given her was a form." (12) "Lesca meant to drive this van to the blind. Maylis meant to keep it at the gate, for Coralie." (13) "She wouldn't hire the woman who had rung him once and then opened the gate." (10) "She had shaken those eight hands along the front row and never asked whose they were." (11)
The same habit also produces the best single lines, because those turn the scene and do not repeat it: "Maylis had sat down and said nothing for sixteen years, in the wrong kitchen." (7) "Now there was a morning in it." (6) "It was louder from here." (16)
Do: keep a caption only when it adds a turn. Cut the six quoted first and the others like them, about fifteen in all.

**5. Refrains and re-introductions are used past the point of echo.**
"care home" 53 times, "forty-two" 17, "twelve years" 17, "forty houses" 13, "two fingers" 13, "ninety" 12, "nine minutes to three" 11 plus "02:51" four times, "in daylight" 10, "every year" or "every December" 18. "Guy Larrère" in full returns in fifteen of the eighteen files, "Jeannot Castagnet" 12 times. Roles are re-attached long after they are known: "Sarthou, the gendarme" (9) two chapters after "Adjudant Sarthou" (7); "Bixente, who drove the minibus" (12) after chapter 3; Madame Dufau "who doesn't know what year it is" in 11 and again in 15. Chapter 7 uses "at home" nine times as the label for the other village.
A refrain works three times: when set, when turned, when paid. At thirteen it is the narrator not trusting the reader.
Do: halve every count above. After a character's second appearance, use one name.

**6. One sentence shape, one emphasis, a small stock of verbs.**
Narration joins clauses with ", and" 814 times, 23 per thousand words. No semicolon, no parenthesis, no exclamation mark and two colons in 43,819 words. Emphasis is nearly always a report of what did not happen: "and Maylis didn't ask" (1), "and she didn't knock on it" (1), "Lesca didn't answer." (5, 16), "He said nothing." (16), "and Arlette didn't ask" (16), "She didn't correct her." (17). "didn't" 71, "nobody" 63, "nothing" 57, "never" 43. People and things are "put" (116) and "stood" (108); chapter 15 has "stood" 16 times in 2,249 words, four of them in one paragraph ("Coralie stood in the second row … Marie-Jo Labat stood where she could count the chairs … Lesca's mother stood by the church wall, where she stood every year").
Each of these is a strength at low dose. Together they make page 150 sound like page 15.
Do: keep the negative closer for the five that carry the book. Give chapter 14 the broken syntax of chapter 13. Give one comic scene a long sentence that runs away with itself.

**7. Every villager speaks in the same fronted-number sentence.**
"'Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you'" (Coralie, 1). "'Forty-one times I've paid for a registered letter'" (Jeannot, 1). "'Eleven years I've been timed like an egg'" (Larrère, 7). "'Thirty years I asked that man what he wanted for his dinner'" (Arlette, 9). "'Twelve years I've told them so'" (Madame Etcheto, 16). "'Eleven Decembers I've held my face still for you'" (Maïté, 16). "'Eleven years of my signatures'" (Lesca, 8).
Seven speakers, seventeen uses. Jeannot's numbered points ("One. … Two. …") are also Lesca's ("There are four points"). The voices are otherwise distinct, so the shared construction shows.
Do: leave it to Coralie and Larrère, who use it best. Give Arlette, Maïté and Madame Etcheto their own way into a grievance.

**8. Scenes open on a quoted line and a back-fill, and close on a button.**
"'What time does the director of the care home get in?' / It was ten past six on Monday the thirtieth of November, with rain on the kitchen window. Coralie had just come down in her tunic, and Lesca had spoken first, with Maylis's mouth." (6) Twenty of ninety scenes open this way, sixteen of them in chapters 5 to 9: four of six scenes in 6, five of six in 9. Each then stalls for two sentences of pluperfect.
Twenty-four scenes end on a sentence of five words or fewer. Five of eight in chapter 12, four of six in chapter 9. "The window went up." ends a scene in 1, 6, 9 and 12.
Do: in chapters 6 and 9, open half the scenes on place or action. Let the window close a scene twice: the first time, and the time it goes down.

**9. Fear is named, and the clock keeps running after the deadline has gone.**
"She was scared." appears in the same three words in 00, 1, 12 and 14. In 00 and 14 it is part of a word-for-word echo and earns its place. In 12 it follows five sentences that have already done the work: "It had not changed. She was awake. Nobody had given her this night. It was her own, and it was happening. She was scared."
Chapter 14 shows the time fifteen times. Up to "03:11. She had given the bank ten minutes. It had held for sixteen." the clock is the scene. After the bank breaks there is no deadline, and "03:14." "03:19." "03:31." "03:52." are habit.
Do: cut "She was scared." in 1 and 12. Drop the standalone times after 03:11 and keep 04:16.

**10. Seams and inconsistencies.**
- 17, steel room: "'…with the river up to my ribs.' 'So you were right. Ten minutes against twenty-five.'" Two quoted runs in one paragraph with nothing between. A beat was cut and the join shows.
- 17: "Coralie came in at nine" after "forty hours" in her tunic. Forty hours from Friday afternoon is Sunday morning, but three scenes have already run since "first light on Sunday": the Port, the towing, the bus and the café. Morning or evening has to be chosen.
- "police" in 4 ("typed, by the police", "a police officer"), 7 ("the wildlife police") and 17 ("police tape"); "gendarme" everywhere else.
- Tags: "said X" sixteen times against "X said" 170, mixed inside one scene (6, the café: "said Josiane", "said Lafitte", "Jeannot said", "said Jeannot Castagnet").
- The article that marks a contested body slips. Maylis's own right hand is "her right hand", then "the hand", then "the right hand" within chapter 6.
- Narration contracts throughout, except "did not" (00) and "had not" twice and "could not" once at the end of 12. If this is a change of register for the two nights at the gate, chapter 14 should keep it. It does not.
- "She wanted to say: don't go." (9) The only colon in narration that introduces thought.
- "the prefecture at Pau" (9, 11, 12) and "the prefect's office in Pau" (6).
- "sandbags" (12) for bags the same scene has filled with grit.

Word-level pattern check: clean. No stock vocabulary, no decorative dashes (eight, all for broken speech), few triads. The formula risks are structural and are faults 6 and 8.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The verbatim echoes across 00, 10, 12 and 14.** "She put her hand flat on the bank beside the water, and the clay moved under her palm." "Low on its left-hand door was a round reflector, screwed on crooked, and it shone red." The same sentences for two women is the book's best device.
2. **Coralie's lines and her objects.** "'I don't cook Either. There's no recipe for it.'" The oven-door impression in 1 and 9, and the third time in 17, when it breaks off at "'The—'". "The car door shutting on a pillow."
3. **The flood memory in chapter 10.** "The reflector went on shining in her headlights at the end of the track for four more minutes. Then the water rose up the door and covered it, and the red light went out."
4. **The hall of the dead and its answer.** "'The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies … You can put that in the minutes as apologies received.'" (13) Then the living hall: "The last chair was empty. / 'Forty-one,' Coralie said." (14)
5. **The confession travelling in chapter 15.** "The loudspeaker on the bank road said 'drowned him' after the square had finished hearing it." "She watched her sentence travel back through the square and down the road at walking pace, row by row." And Tastet turning the sheet over "to see whether there was more on the back".
