# Round 22 — Reader

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. About 43,800 words. Nothing else opened but the brief.

Lens: an ordinary reader with no special knowledge of sluice gates, French villages, Basque folklore or lucid dreaming.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes — 7.5.** The marriage is the stake I felt, more than the forty houses. "I'd have this one again." (ch. 6)
- **Tension — 7.5.** Chapters 12 to 14 are read without looking up; before that it is dread and curiosity, not fear. "She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it broke." (ch. 14)
- **Progression — 7.** Every chapter changes the position, but the sum the climax runs on arrives as a note between scenes. "One woman could turn the winch, or she could reach that hatch. She couldn't do both." (ch. 8)
- **Comedy — 8.** I laughed out loud five or six times. "Eleven years I've been timed like an egg." (ch. 7)
- **Characters — 8.** I would know Coralie's voice with the name covered. "I don't cook Either. There's no recipe for it." (ch. 1)
- **The switch between dark and comic — 8.** The joke stays in the flood and does not shrink it. "'Here,' he said. 'I know you are.'" (ch. 14)
- **Prose — 7.5.** Plain, and I never had to re-read a sentence for its grammar, only for its rules. "Under the water the torch found the sole of a wader." (ch. 10)
- **The ending — 7.** It costs enough, then spends two chapters and thirteen scenes counting the cost. "That's yours, and I'm not carrying it for you." (ch. 17)
- **Clarity — 6.** At the hinge of the book I was reconstructing a rule from ten chapters back. "She had held Inguma down for eight rings of a phone, and that counted." (ch. 13)

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. From the first line of 12 to the last of 14 I did not stop.

**Would I press it on a friend?** Yes, on the friend who loves The Good Place. To the friend who loves Crouch I would say: you will have to re-read two pages in chapter 4 and two in chapter 13, and the last two chapters are slow.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Just below the bar. About 7.4 against an 8.** Level or above on people and on being funny in the dark. Below on the one thing a Crouch reader takes for granted: never having to stop and work out where you are.

- **Below Crouch on ease of rules.** In Dark Matter the box is explained once, in one scene, and after that every scene tests it. Here the rules of Inguma come from an old woman (ch. 3), a boy in a tractor bucket (ch. 3), Lesca's four points (ch. 5), a mother at a stove (ch. 4), an aside in the steel room (ch. 9) and a line in an office (ch. 11). Lesca's four points are as good as Michael at his whiteboard. The trouble is they are not the last word, and the climax uses the parts that came from elsewhere.
- **Level with Crouch on fear for a marriage, and for three chapters above.** Crouch makes me afraid a stranger with my face will be believed by my wife. This book does that and adds the worse thing: the wife prefers her. "In sixteen years Maylis had never got that laugh out of her. Lesca had got it in ten minutes, with Maylis's mouth." The hand on the stair post in chapter 6 frightened me more than anything in the flood. That fear peaks in chapter 8 and is gone by chapter 10, when Maylis starts drafting a handover note. From there I was afraid of a river.
- **Level with The Good Place on the ethical problem as comedy.** It is a trolley problem and the book never says so. Each person is wrong in their own way: Lesca counts and the count always comes out for her, Maylis says Either, Larrère speaks through a gap, Jeannot numbers his grievances, Coralie will not ask the one question. The reversals do re-read what came before: 02:51 is the minute of the call, Coralie has known for twelve years, the kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice. They are smaller than the comparison's because each re-reads a thread, not the whole premise.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The rules arrive in six instalments, and the climax turns on the least rehearsed one. (ch. 3, 5, 9, 11, 13 — structural)**

> "It fetches from there, and it gets up once to do it. Keep it down for that once, and they call my name out there. One question, before it takes us. [...] So who holds my hand down then?" (ch. 9)

By chapter 9 there are three ways for Lesca to stay (give it more to count, keep the mouth to re-send it, sit on it) and two ways to be stopped (a hand on the mouth, a mother's short job). I could not tell which one Friday would run on. "Who holds my hand down" refers to Sunday's kitchen and to a method she then drops for sitting on it. When chapter 13 says eight rings "counted", I had to go back to an old woman's speech in chapter 3 to find the clause. I should have been afraid at that moment and I was doing bookkeeping.

What I would do: make Lesca's agenda in chapter 5 the complete and only statement. Add the holding-down clause there, in her words, as part of point four. In chapter 9 replace the question with a plain sentence of what she will do on Friday and who could stop her. In chapter 13, one sentence before she sits: what sitting buys, what it costs.

**2. Both reversals at the climax go by faster than I could follow. (ch. 13–14 — structural)**

> "*That the mayor remain seated.* / In the steel room the mayor stood up."

This is Lesca's choice, the one the book has built to since her motion in chapter 4, and I had to infer why she makes it. My best guess: with two of them on one steering wheel the van reaches nobody. The text gives me a reflector "no nearer than it had been twelve years ago" and leaves the rest to me.

Then Maylis. In chapter 13 she pulls the handbrake to keep the van at the gate, "for Coralie", and breaks her own arm against her rib to do it. Two pages later, alone, she drives past the handle to the blind. The reason is there ("Maylis thought she could do better than that") but it is one sentence after a three-breath pause, and it is not confirmed until her confession in chapter 17. In the moment I did not believe the turn. I believed it three chapters late.

What I would do: give Lesca two plain sentences of what she sees before she stands. Give Maylis, at the handle, the thing she says in chapter 17: that she wants to be the one who gets both. It is the truest thing about her and the reader needs it at the handle, not at the inquest.

**3. Two chapters of aftermath, and the last meeting in the steel room is an audit. (ch. 16–17 — structural)**

> "'That's yours, and I'm not carrying it for you. Five o'clock is yours as well. You had your weight on that handle in daylight, with him on the bank behind you, above ground.'"

Madame Darrigrand has said exactly this to Maylis's face four scenes earlier, and said it better, from her own doorstep with silt in the pram. Chapter 15 ends on "Somebody has to stack them", which is an ending. After it come thirteen more scenes. Chapter 16 is seven of them, each closing one account: Tastet, Etcheto, Maïté, the bull, the landing, the lieutenant, the vote, good night. I read 16 at half attention. It is the only chapter where I did not need the next one.

What I would do: in the last steel-room scene keep "I wanted your sash", "I don't know", and the exchange from "Is yours in the house?" to "She took the pillow." Cut the allotting of blame between them. In 16, cut the traffic lieutenant on the square and reduce the bull to three sentences.

**4. I could not say what Larrère did wrong on the night of the flood. (ch. 16)**

> "'You stood in my kitchen that night and rang her from my phone, with his van gone down to the meadows. You said it made no difference where he was.'"

The scene is staged as an unmasking: rockets, the man lit red then white, "I'll be brief" and then nothing. But his order was to keep the gate shut, and a shut gate keeps Jean Castagnet dry. So beyond being in another man's kitchen, what is he guilty of? In Maylis's village it is plain, because he says it down the phone: "He stays dry as long as you do what you're told." In Lesca's village I had to guess.

What I would do: one more clause from Maïté that says what his silence did. He knew where Jean was, and he never told the woman he sent to the gate.

**5. The first half of chapter 7 lost me. (ch. 7)**

> "'He did that to you, and you've come in here and done it to me. Friday, when the town hall's open. You'll have your complaint back.'"

Three scenes of leverage in two pages: an undated resignation, a complaint with one name in it, a threat about nets. I did not know what a complaint naming her would do to her, how Jeannot knows what "he did" to her that morning, or that the poster in the porch was her threat until he reacted to it. I got there on a second pass. This is the place I would have put the book down if chapter 6 had not just been the best chapter in it.

What I would do: one sentence on what the complaint costs her. Let her say the threat in plain words.

**6. The sum the whole climax runs on is delivered in a linking paragraph. (ch. 8)**

> "The walk took twenty-five minutes, and when the bank started to go it would have ten. One woman could turn the winch, or she could reach that hatch. She couldn't do both."

Twenty-five against ten is the book's trolley. It reaches me as a note between the pillow scene and bingo night, in the same breath as two nights of pushing and Lesca getting back in. I did not register it as the thing to remember. I only felt it in chapter 10, when Lesca's own night gave me the same numbers.

What I would do: play the walk as a short scene. Lesca with the watch, Maylis in the right hand, the hatch under their boots.

**7. The flood crossing over is the premise's weakest joint, and it gets six lines. (ch. 5)**

> "'The flood is the biggest thing I have, and it's been coming across to you ever since, one day of my file at a time.'"

A cracked rib crossing as a memory I accept. Nineteen days of weather crossing because she remembers them is a much larger claim, and Maylis's good question ("Why would it come now?") gets one speech. I took it because Lesca says "I'd rather it was the winch. I want somebody to blame", which is a good line. But it also means Lesca brought the river that drowned room twelve, and nobody in chapters 16 or 17 says so. I waited for it.

What I would do: either let one of them say it in the last steel-room scene, in a line, or give Maylis a second test of it early (a detail of the weather only Lesca's file could know).

**8. Two lessons back to back before anything has happened. (ch. 3)**

> "'It's a thing you can learn, waking up inside your dreams. The phone goes off on the hour and you check. Five, and you're awake. In a dream the hand comes out wrong.'"

Kévin teaches dreaming, then Madame Etcheto teaches Inguma, then Maylis practises. Both are charming and both pay (the five fingers in the flood pay more than anything in the book). But for a whole chapter I am being equipped. It is the slowest chapter before 16.

What I would do: cut Kévin to the fingers and "That's the door." Keep the reading-twice test for the moment she uses it.

**9. Jeannot refuses three times in one chapter. (ch. 12)**

> "'I told you not to.' / 'I walked.' / 'I can see how you came.'"

He refuses on the platform, at the van window and at the hatch. The first is the scene. The third plants the dip, the bolt and the missed calls. The second gives me the phone staying in the van and the warning about the track, and otherwise repeats the first. At 4,500 words this is the longest chapter, and the stretch between "Either" and the roof of the van bending is where I felt it.

What I would do: reduce the van-window exchange to the two practical lines.

**10. Small things an outsider trips on.**

- "The steel pawl clicked over at each turn" (ch. 3). I do not know what a pawl is, and it is used eight or nine times and matters to the plot. Call it the catch the first time.
- "Adjudant Sarthou" (ch. 7). The rank is never glossed.
- "the bench that thanked the State" (ch. 16). First and only mention.
- ch. 17, the last steel-room scene: "'Forty-one,' Maylis said. 'The one who wasn't in the hall was room twelve. I stood in her door with the river up to my ribs.' 'So you were right." The close-quote and open-quote in the middle of one speaker's speech reads as a second speaker. I thought Lesca had answered.
- ch. 17, the café: "Had the key and went for a walk, did you?" I took it as said to Maylis and had to re-read to see it is the village's new greeting for a latecomer, said to someone else with her standing there. It is a very good idea. One more word would make it land first time.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 6, all of it.** The window going all the way down, the spoon tipped back into the bowl, the cut palm on the stair post.
2. **The oven door, three times.** Chapter 1, chapter 9, and chapter 17 where she gets as far as "The—" and cannot. With it the shopping pad, every box on it, and "Eggs is what Either gets in this house."
3. **Chapter 10.** The reflector going out, the sole of a wader, then nine rings and "And six. Bugger off."
4. **The bedroom in chapter 11.** "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." and "Because you'd have told me."
5. **The yard and the corridor in chapter 14, to "Forty-one."** Daguerre's "Here", the man asking if it is Thursday, Kévin stopping in the water to count five fingers, the hatbox on the wardrobe.

**In a month I will remember:** a car window going down into the door; a woman doing the mayor's voice into an oven; a right hand tipping soup back into the bowl; a red reflector going out; "Bugger off"; an old woman finishing somebody's prayer over a duck; a boy in floodwater counting to five and wanting six; "I drowned him" arriving late from a second loudspeaker.
