# Round 22 — Thriller

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the care home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." Countable, human, and doubled by a bed. But when the bank does go, the count is one, and it is not the one the reader loves.
- **Tension: 6.** "Maylis read it twice, because room twelve was the last room in the building, at the end under the bank." The book points at its victim five hours before the water. From there the reader is less afraid than the heroine.
- **Progression: 6.** "'A vote's a vote,' Josiane said." Friday is foretold to the minute in chapter 5, and between chapters 5 and 10 nothing either woman does moves the gate, the man or the bank.
- **Comedy: 8.** "'A woman takes flowers to the wife because she's after the husband or because she's after the wife. It's adultery either way, and that one lives with a nurse.'"
- **Characters: 8.** "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'"
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "Then he held his left hand out in front of him in his own headlights, with the fingers spread, and touched each finger with the forefinger of his right hand." A joke from chapter 3 comes back as the most frightening half-minute in the flood.
- **Prose: 7.** "It wasn't loud. It sounded like a truck tipping out a load of gravel, a long way off, and the sound didn't stop." Fast and clear enough for the genre. No sentence slowed me.
- **The ending: 6.5.** "'I know what you picked. I was the other one.'" That line costs. Most of what surrounds it hands the bill round.
- **Clarity: 7.** "'Whichever of us wakes first wakes at home. If the other one is still sitting here, she goes home with her.'" I held the rules, but I was holding them, in chapters 7 to 9, when I should have been afraid.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. From the end of chapter 3 to the end of chapter 6 and from the platform in chapter 12 to "Forty-one" I could not have stopped.

**Press it on a friend?** On a friend who loves The Good Place, yes. On a friend who loves Dark Matter, no: I would tell them it is a comedy with a flood in it.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch as a thriller. Level with him on premise and on planting.**

- **Below: the other self gives up.** In Dark Matter the second Jason has to be beaten. Here the double has the body, the van and the wheel, and lets go inside eight rings on a private thought: "the reflector was red, and no nearer than it had been twelve years ago." The best engine in the book, set running by "and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it", stops because its driver takes her foot off.
- **Below: the wife is never under the water.** Crouch makes the reader afraid for a marriage by putting the wife in the room with the danger. Coralie is thirty metres from the patch for twelve chapters, and when the bank goes she is next seen in a doorway reading a list. The marriage ends with a job in a town the book twice calls "twenty minutes" away.
- **Below: the middle changes nothing.** A Crouch chapter leaves the hero in a different place from where he began it. Chapters 6 to 9 leave Maylis where chapter 5 put her: gate shut, man sworn to his blind, Larrère behind a window.
- **Level: the wrinkle in the premise.** "'You got my rib,' Maylis said. 'And I get your river.'" The woman who has come to stop the flood is the one sending it. That is as good as anything Crouch has had. The book then drops it (fault 6).
- **Level or above: the plants.** The finger count, the dip in the track, the kitchen clock behind the voice, the phone left in the van because of nine rings at dawn. Every one is laid fairly and paid.
- **Against The Good Place on reversals: below.** Its reversals make you re-read what came before. This book has two that could ("I get your river"; "Then it wasn't Inguma that came for her. It was you"), and plays the first in chapter 5 as an agenda item and the second in chapter 16 as an explanation after the fact.

**Where a Crouch reader puts it down:** page three of chapter 1 (the registered letter); the council in chapter 7 at Larrère's eleventh minute; the run from the vote in chapter 9 through chapters 10 and 11; and chapter 16.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. Chapters 12 and 14. The victim is announced, and Coralie is never in danger.**
"*Etcheto back in 12. Walked there on my arm. Said her words again.*" Then: "Maylis read it twice, because room twelve was the last room in the building, at the end under the bank."
At the dip Maylis thinks she is turning her back on Coralie. The reader knows she is turning her back on a woman of ninety-four the book has shown three times. Eight minutes after the bank breaks Coralie is in the lit doorway asking "'Castagnet?'", dry above the hip. The fear the book has built since "thirty metres from the patch" is never spent.
*Do:* keep the text message, and have Coralie obey the last order she is given: "Start at the bank end." When Maylis reaches the yard, Nadine has the list and Coralie is in the old wing, behind the fire door, in the dark. Maylis has a wheelchair in her hands and cannot go. Run the evacuation with that door at the end of the corridor. Coralie comes back up the ramp; twelve still would not open. The death is the same. The reader has spent twenty minutes believing the choice at the dip killed the wife.

**2. Chapters 13 and 14. The double yields unasked, and the fight is over nothing.**
"Lesca meant to drive this van to the blind. Maylis meant to keep it at the gate, for Coralie." Four pages on, alone: "She drove past."
In the steel room Maylis says she will "raise my gate by myself", with him under it. In the van she pulls the handbrake to stop the drive to the blind. Then Lesca leaves, says "Go to the blind first", and Maylis goes to the blind first. The whole struggle moves the van nowhere it would not have gone, and Maylis changes sides twice in a thousand words. The book knows how this reads, because it has to deny it in the last chapter: "'I didn't go because you said it.'"
*Do:* fix Maylis's side before the fight. In the steel room she already says both, and Lesca tells her the sum from experience: there is no both. Then the fight is about who drives, not where. And Lesca must be put out, not walk out. Maylis has never once given Inguma its work without saying "Either". Let her say the words herself in that van and give it something short. That pays chapter 3 and gives the heroine the win.

**3. Chapters 14 and 17. The bill is split four ways and the other branch is closed.**
"'It's stuck,' he said. 'Your winch. I counted the clicks, the way you do, and at nineteen it stopped.'" Then, in the last chapter: "'That part is mine.'" "'And you're not having all of room twelve.'" "'I was in no danger, and I'll say so to anyone.'"
Lesca's choice cost a man and twelve years with nobody to share it. Maylis's costs an old woman, and within a day Coralie has taken a share, Lesca has taken a share, the list has taken a share, Jeannot has put her defence in writing, and the winch has shown that the gate would not have come up at three whoever stood at it. The choice at the dip turns out to have had one live branch.
*Do:* the book says, through Madame Darrigrand and through Lesca, that the real sin was five o'clock. Then make the ending land there and nowhere else. Cut Lesca's claim on room twelve from chapter 17 (it is already hers in 16, said to a living woman). Cut "It was your mayor's list" (fault 1's fix removes it). Keep Jeannot's letter, and keep "That one's yours. I never tried it", which is her refusing the alibi.

**4. Chapters 6 to 9, and the head of 12. One contest, four times, and every door shuts the same way.**
"Maylis threw herself at the jaw, and got it." (6) "Maylis shut the teeth on the rest with the last of what she had." (8) "Maylis brought the right hand up and clapped it over her own mouth first." (9)
Jaw, spoon, stair post, teeth, wrist, mouth. Each round ends when Inguma arrives or Coralie rules. The threat to the marriage peaks on the landing in chapter 8, where Coralie herself settles it, and has nowhere to go after. Outside the body, every attempt fails by the same man through the same window: the association vote, the letter to Pau, then on the day itself the café, Hontaube ("'Then it should have gone last Monday'") and Larrère, three refusals in a row at the head of the longest chapter. Meanwhile the one scene the climax depends on is a summary: "One woman could turn the winch, or she could reach that hatch. She couldn't do both."
*Do:* keep the stairs and the landing. Cut the jaw fight from the bingo bar or the kitchen-table opening of 9; they are the same beat a day apart. Cut the café from chapter 12. Spend the room on the walk to the hatch with a watch, played as a scene, so the reader has the kilometre and the dip in their legs before chapter 14.

**5. Chapters 10 and 11. Two chapters that look backwards on the eve.**
"It rang once. Nobody answered, and she didn't wait for the second ring." (10) "'Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one.'" (11)
The opening page gave the bank, the three breaths and the single ring. Chapter 2 gave "Did you know he was out?" The reader has had the secret since then. Chapter 10 replays the night at length; chapter 11 has Coralie reveal the same call. Both scenes are good. Together they hold the river still for two chapters between Tuesday's vote and Friday.
*Do:* cut chapter 10's replay to what is new: Larrère's order with a kitchen clock behind it, Coralie's car in the yard, the reflector, the four minutes it shone, and 02:51 as Inguma's minute. Open the chapter on the nine rings. In 11, keep Coralie and the phone; drop Arlette's visit to one exchange.

**6. Chapter 5. The best twist is dropped.**
"'The flood is the biggest thing I have, and it's been coming across to you ever since, one day of my file at a time.'"
If the river comes through the chair, the first thing any reader asks is whether keeping out of the chair stops it. Maylis pushes Inguma off for two nights in chapter 8 and nobody looks at the gauge. Nobody proposes it, nobody rules it out, and in chapter 17 nobody says to Lesca: it was your flood that drowned her. The book's one reversal that changes who the antagonist is gets a single scene.
*Do:* one line in chapter 8 after the two nights of pushing: the gauge has kept to the file anyway. And one charge in the last meeting in the chair. It is the thing Maylis has on Lesca, and she never uses it.

**7. Chapters 3, 5 and 13. The rules are recited, down to the clause the climax needs.**
"'If somebody holds it down, so that it cannot get up and fetch, that counts as not fetching.'" Then four numbered points in chapter 5. Then: "She had held Inguma down for eight rings of a phone, and that counted."
A folk tale does not carry a sub-clause for a case that has never arisen. The reader sees it being planted and, at the climax, watches a rule being applied. Nobody is ever wrong about how this works: "So Lesca had a guess", and the guess is right in every part on the first night. Crouch frightens by letting his hero be wrong.
*Do:* take the clause out of Madame Etcheto's mouth and out of point four. Lesca already tells her mother "'I'm going to sit on it.'" Let that be her own idea, and let neither she nor the reader know what it costs until a voice calls her name off a card.

**8. Chapters 16 and 17. The only human antagonist cannot hurt anyone, and is not hurt.**
"'There is no other sheet,' he said." And at the garden gate: "He said nothing."
Larrère's weapon is a personnel file. The one threat with teeth, Kévin's name on a blank sheet, the threat that sat on the passenger seat at five o'clock, is withdrawn in six words. His exposure in Maïté's kitchen confirms what the reader assembled by chapter 10 from a back lane and a ticking clock. Maylis reads "Castagnet, house" on her screen twice in chapter 14 and has no thought about it.
*Do:* give her the thought, on the call: she knows whose kitchen he is standing in while he tells her the man stays dry. And let Sarthou's file carry the sheet forward against Kévin, so the threat she named as her excuse outlives the night.

**9. Chapters 16 and 17. Thirteen scenes after the water.**
"Lesca didn't leave boxes empty. She ticked *No*, and signed under it."
Seven sections in 16, six in 17, each settling one account: sash, medal case, verb, gutter, form, timer, van, suitcase, pad, pillow. Each pays something planted. In a row they read as a ledger, and no clock runs under any of them.
*Do:* in 16 keep four: Madame Etcheto at the minibus, Maïté's kitchen and the garden gate as one scene, the timer turned back to nothing, "Good night, Maylis". The third box and the Tastet opening go. End 16 on "Surname first".

**10. Chapter 1. Three pages of registered post after the best first page.**
"'I've got a registered letter for the mayor,' the postman said into the gap."
Everything in the square is used later. But the reader has just watched something the size of a sheep come off a wardrobe and is given the landowners' association. The chapter holds its hook back to the last half-page: "That morning, when the thing came, a picture had come into her head with it."
*Do:* cut nothing. Move the stone. Give her the picture when she wakes, before the square: her own name cut in granite, in the space where the mayor parks. Then she rakes leaves beside that parking space with his car in it, and the comedy has something under it.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The opening page and everything that answers it.** "For three breaths she held it"; "It rang once"; and, in chapter 10, nine rings, "'Who's dead?'" and a curse in six numbered points.
2. **The end of chapter 4.** A full stop changed to a comma: "*That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*"
3. **The landing in chapter 8.** Two fingers on the wrist, three of the four steps, "'That's her. In this hand'", and the pillow taken back.
4. **Five o'clock on the platform in chapter 12.** The pawl in its first tooth, "'Which is it going to be? Shut, or open?'", "'Either'", "'Then it's shut.'" This is the book's real choice. If anything, give it more room.
5. **The last third of chapter 14.** Kévin's count at four o'clock, the open doors of nine, ten and eleven by torch, the pulse held "for the full half minute", and "'Forty-one.'" With it, his line in 17: "'I wanted six, Maylis.'"
