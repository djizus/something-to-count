Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. I would have put it down twice on the way: in the long town-hall middle of chapter 3, and in chapter 6 between the resignation letter and the council.

## 2. Against the comparisons, for characters

**Above Crouch. Just below The Good Place. Overall a half point under the bar.**

**Above Crouch on the wife.** Daniela in Dark Matter is what Jason wants back. Coralie refuses that job in so many words: "'There's a key, and a winch, and Larrère, and a van. And then there's me, as a distance.'" (8) She has a want that is nothing to do with either woman ("I posted it today, and I posted it for me", 7), she is wrong in her own way in both villages (twelve years of not asking, "Because you'd have told me", 10; "I'm not waking a woman of 94 for orange", 11), and she owns it without being asked ("That part is mine", 16).

**Above Crouch on the double.** Jason2 takes the bed and the book hates him for it from outside. Lesca writes the same intention as a council motion, "*and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it*" (3), and the book stays inside her while she tries. She is not softened. In the last meeting she says "'Your hand on the post stopped me. I wouldn't have stopped.'" (16), and to the old woman who died in her place, "'Nothing. I didn't want to sit down.'" (15)

**Below The Good Place on the one who cannot choose.** Chidi's indecision is a pleasure to watch because he talks it. Maylis's is one word, and the narrator does the rest of the talking for her. For eight chapters she is the less interesting of the two women in her own book. This is the gap between 7.5 and 8.

The lens questions, answered:

- **Distinct?** Yes, by speech and by hand. Lesca chairs, numbers her points and corrects a capital ("'It takes a small m.'", 5). Maylis says Either and crosses a t. The split body is characterisation and not a trick: the right hand that tips the soup spoon is the same hand that locks on the stair post.
- **Wanted-for?** Lesca wants out loud. Maylis's wants are announced by the narrator. See fault 1.
- **Softened or excused?** Lesca, no. Maylis, yes, by circumstance. See fault 5.
- **Described from outside as a woman?** No. Neither is described physically at all, and the one joke about it is right: "They were the same height, and neither of them liked it." (4) "The village's girl" and "little Lesca" are the village's diminutives, used knowingly, for a woman of forty-three.
- **Who would be missed?** Jeannot first. Then Madame Etcheto, Kévin, Marie-Jo. Not Larrère in Maylis's village (fault 7). Arlette is missed because the book drops her (fault 9).
- **Which relationship carries it, and does it pay?** Maylis and Coralie carry it. It pays at three-quarters. The leaving is exact ("She heard her on every stair coming down, all eleven", 16), but the book has shown every reason to go and almost none to have stayed (fault 2). The relationship that pays in full is the small one, Maylis and Jeannot, from "'Put it in forty-two.'" (1) to the second shovel.
- **Does the reader care who lives?** Yes, and the book uses it without mercy. Chapter 9 makes Jeannot loved in one phone call. Chapter 13 saves him, shows he was never in danger ("fetching a man out of a dry hole", 16), and charges the bill to room twelve.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. Maylis is the lesser woman in her own book until chapter 9 (structural)

**Chapters 1 to 8.** "She wanted Coralie to stay awake and keep her fingers on that wrist, but Coralie had to be up before six, and Maylis didn't ask." (1) "Maylis wanted the duck. But Coralie would be the one cooking it" (1). "Maylis did worry about it, and she had written to say so. But the man behind the glass paid her wages, and she didn't knock on it." (1) "She wanted to tell her not to go. But Coralie had wanted that floor since she was nineteen" (8).

What is wrong: she is built from one absence and one sentence shape, *she wanted X, but Y, and she didn't*. The reader is told what she lacks and never shown what she is. Her trade is asserted and kept off the page: "Maylis dug the graves in Peyrebarthe. She knew what a name on a stone meant." (1) She does not dig one until the last page. Her one piece of wit, "'Put it in forty-two'", has to be used four times because there is no second. So when Coralie says "'I'd have this one again'" (5), the reader agrees with her, and the book needs the reader to mind.

What I would do: put a grave in chapter 1 or 2. The priest says "'It's the eighth this year'" (16), so there are seven to choose from. One of the care home's dead, with Coralie at the graveside and Madame Etcheto in her chair. Let Maylis be exact, quick and unasked at the one thing she does without weighing it: the string, the pegs, the depth, the turf lifted so it can go back. It gives her a self before Lesca arrives to outshine her, it lets Coralie be seen watching her, and it loads the final image. Then cut half of the *she wanted, but* sentences, keeping the shopping pad and the wrist.

### 2. The book never shows what Coralie has in Maylis, so her leaving underpays (structural)

**Chapters 1, 5, 7, 16.** "'Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you, and that was me.'" (1) "'I'd have this one again.'" (5) "'I'd have the one who answers. I'd have you tomorrow.'" (7)

What is wrong: every Coralie scene at home is evidence for the prosecution. The other Coralie is given the mirror, and it is one of the best lines in the book: "'Do you know what I'd give for a woman at this table who sat down when she was told? I'd carry my pillow back across that landing tonight.'" (6) Maylis's own Coralie never says the equivalent. What she gives on the landing is loyalty ("it's the other one's bed"), not love. The nearest thing to a reason is the laughing, and Lesca gets a bigger laugh in ten minutes.

What I would do: one concrete thing that only the quiet one does, seen once early and named once late. The material is already there and unused: she carried the shopping up from the Port twice without saying why, and she is at the corridor door at a quarter to ten to drive Coralie home. In chapter 7, between "'That's her. In this hand.'" and Bayonne, give Coralie one sentence of what that hand has done for sixteen years. Then "the car door shutting on a pillow" costs what it should.

### 3. The hinge of Lesca's arc is illegible, and her five words are never reckoned (structural)

**Chapter 12.** "The hand locked on the brake was the hand that had held the stair post against her, and at the end of the headlights the reflector was red, and no nearer than it had been twelve years ago. / Rain was loud on the roof. She had crossed for that sound, and for a bed with two pillows on it. / *That the mayor remain seated.* / In the steel room the mayor let go of the chair."

What is wrong: three chapters earlier this woman said "'I won't go.'" Here she goes, and the reason is three images and a callback. The real reason is two paragraphs above and thrown away: "Lesca waited for the other word, the one this mouth put after everything. It didn't come." She yields because the other one has, for the first time, not said Either. That is the best idea in the pair and the reader has to assemble it.

Worse, "'Go to the blind first'" is Lesca's twelve-year penance handed to another woman as an instruction, and it is the instruction that drowns Madame Etcheto. Nobody says so. In chapter 16 Maylis takes it all ("'I know what I drove for'") and Lesca owns the river and the stairs but not the five words.

What I would do: move the letting go to directly after the missing Either, and say in one plain sentence what Lesca sees. In chapter 16 give Lesca one more line: that she sent Maylis down the track to do what she had not done herself, and that it was her night she was mending and not Maylis's.

### 4. The two women never once speak as the same person (structural)

**Chapters 4, 8, 12, 16.** "'A meeting needs somebody in the chair. Since neither of us is in it, I'll chair from here.'" (4)

What is wrong: four meetings, and all four are business: rules, terms, a refusal, a settlement. The procedure is funny in chapter 4 and it is a wall by chapter 8. They share a father, a mother, a night behind a tent in 2010 and a week of shouting in one February, and neither asks the other a single question about any of it. The book even builds the mirrored pair and does not let them see it: Maylis lays her bandaged palm beside her father's watch and "He marked a number and didn't look up" (8); Lesca walks past the same man and "she didn't stop" (12). The last meeting is twenty-five lines.

What I would do: no new scene. In chapter 8, after "'One night'", one question from each that only the other could answer. In chapter 16, let one of them say she passed their father's table. Take the room from the procedure in chapters 6 and 8.

### 5. Maylis's silence is excused at every point where it should be hers

**Chapters 6, 8, 11.** "The letter would only cost her. The other sheet would cost Kévin." (6) "Behind the car the boy was still on his ladder. Maylis didn't say it a third time." (8)

What is wrong: a cracked rib, a winch that sticks at nineteen, three ignored letters, a mayor with a folder, a form from Hontaube, an orange warning, and a blank sheet with a boy's name on it. By the time she stands on the platform she has been cornered so thoroughly that the Either reads as reasonable. Chapter 16 knows this and has her throw the excuse away, "'That's not why I kept the gate shut. I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me.'" That is the right line, and it dismisses in one sentence an apparatus that took three chapters to build.

What I would do: in chapter 8 let her say "Open" the third time, with the sheet against the glass. Jeannot's vote sinks it four to three anyway, so the plot does not move. Then the Either on the platform in chapter 11 is the first time she is not cornered, and it is entirely hers.

### 6. The narrator says the motive in chapter 13 that chapter 16 exists to confess

**Chapter 13.** "Lesca had rung once and turned the handle, and Maylis thought she could do better than that. She wanted the man and the gate, both, and she wanted to be the one who got them."

**Chapter 16.** "'At the winch I thought I could do better than you. The man and the gate, both. I drove past the handle to be the one who did.'"

What is wrong: the same sentence twice. The confession is the centre of the last meeting, and the reader has already been handed it in the middle of the flood by a voice that is not hers. It is the worst case of a habit that runs through her chapters: "At home she got this man a few centimetres at a time." (6) "Nobody had given her this night. It was her own, and it was happening." (11)

What I would do: in chapter 13 keep the action only, "for three breaths she kept her foot off the pedal", then "She drove past." Let chapter 16 be the first time it is said. Keep "She wanted it not to be her." That one is the book.

### 7. Larrère is a man in Lesca's village and a mechanism in Maylis's

**Chapter 6, Lesca's village.** "'Eleven years I've been timed like an egg,' he said. 'I have sentences in that register with no ends on them.'"

**Chapter 11, Maylis's village.** "'Is that advice?' / 'No,' Larrère said. 'That's the file.'"

What is wrong: across the lamps he has a grievance, a ledger, a question he has never dared ask, and a hat he takes off. On this side he blackmails, parks in a back lane and says shut. When his own prediction is read back to him off a café pad, "Nothing came through it" (8), and he goes on unchanged. The book holds a real irony and gives him none of the benefit: with the gate shut, the adulterer in the kitchen is the only person in the village keeping the husband dry. He is the one in the cast who is not wrong in his own way. He is only in the way.

What I would do: one moment of fear in chapter 8 after Lafitte reads the pad, and his reason said once in his own voice. He believes the bank holds because it has held all his life: "It leaks. It has leaked before, with the river over the line, and it held." (11) Let him be seen to believe it.

### 8. Coralie at home has one question, asked nine times

**Chapters 1, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9, 11, 13, 16.** "'What do you want, Maylis?'" (1) "'Tick one.'" (4) "'What else do you want?'" (5) "'But you'll have to ask.'" (7) "'I'm asking where I am in this.'" (8) "'I asked where you'd be.'" (9) "*Keep asking me things.*" (11) "'Say which, Maylis. For once.'" (13) "'What do you want, Maylis?'" (16)

What is wrong: at work she is a whole person ("'It has enough seats when nobody's counting.'", 11; "'Don't give me the minutes. Go.'", 13). In the kitchen she is the theme, asking its question. By chapter 9 the reader can say her exit line before she does.

What I would do: cut the question from chapter 9. End that scene on the bandage in her teeth, "'I'll have to ask you everything like that'", and her hand on the rib. Let one kitchen scene be about her night, the care plan, Ithurbide, and nothing Maylis owes her.

### 9. Arlette disappears from Maylis's village

**Chapter 8.** "'You said it twice. I've never heard you say anything twice. I'd have liked to be there.'"

What is wrong: that line promises a mother, and she is not seen again on this side. She lives across the yard. Her ponies were on the meadows. She is absent from the flood night and from every scene of chapter 16, where Kévin, Marie-Jo, Jeannot, the priest and Coralie all get their moment. The other Arlette has two of the best short scenes in the book (10, 14).

What I would do: one beat in chapter 16. She is at the tap when the car door shuts on the pillow, or she is the one who does not cross the yard. Either is in character. Nothing is not.

### 10. The small cast blurs, and the ones worth missing blur with it

**Chapter 16.** "'Late,' Fernand said to him, dealing."

What is wrong: this man has been "the man dealing the cards" for fifteen chapters, and he gets a name at the moment he delivers the village's verdict. Larrère, Lesca, Labat, Lafitte, Lassalle. Daguerre, Dufau, Darrigrand, Ducasse. Castagnet, Castéra. Monsieur Darrigrand sweeps a step in one village (3) and Madame Darrigrand signs a petition in the other (8) with nothing to connect them. Lafitte and his pad deserve to be told apart from the rest and are not.

What I would do: name Fernand in chapter 1 or leave him unnamed to the end. Change one of the D names and one of the L names. Give Madame Darrigrand one word in chapter 8 that ties her to the swept step.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 10, the phone.** "'You never asked.' / 'No.' / 'Why not?' / 'Because you'd have told me.'" And "'I thought I could have you without it, if I kept a landing between us.'" The best scene in the book for character. It makes the second Coralie as guilty as the woman she lives with, and it does it from a call log.
2. **Chapter 9, Jeannot's six points.** From "'Who's dead?'" to "'And six. Bugger off.'" and the line after it: "A man had just sworn at her in six points, and he was alive." This is where the reader starts caring who lives.
3. **Chapter 13, from Kévin's count to "Forty-one".** "'Five,' he said." The pulse held "for the full half minute". The line through the last name. The hatbox on the wardrobe. And its echo in chapter 16: "'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much.'"
4. **Chapter 7, the landing.** Two steps taken, the wrist read, the third step, and the pillow taken back. A woman choosing the worse partner on principle while the better one has her hand on the pillow.
5. **Chapter 16, the village's new greeting and the second shovel.** "'Had the key and went for a walk, did you?'" replacing "'Still in the box, were you?'", and Jeannot's letter that "doesn't thank you", answered with "'That one's yours. At three I never tried it.'" She refuses the excuse and he digs anyway.
