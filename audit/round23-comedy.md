# Round 23 — Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full (42,600 words). Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes — 8.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the care home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man, at his blind, and no one came to tell him." Countable, on the table by chapter 3, and never restated in larger words.
- **Tension — 7.5.** "She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it broke." Chapters 11 to 13 are at the comparison's level. Chapters 1 to 8 run on curiosity, not fear.
- **Progression — 6.5.** "'It's still you,' Coralie said. 'On Monday it fetched you off my stairs.'" The crossing, the wrestling for the jaw and the fetching home are run three times between chapters 5 and 8 before the book moves on.
- **Comedy — 6.5.** "'Eleven years I've been timed like an egg,' he said. 'I have sentences in that register with no ends on them.'" That is the level of chapters 1 to 9. From chapter 10 there are comic lines and no comic scene, and the whole book draws on one source of comedy.
- **Characters — 7.5.** "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'" Coralie is the best thing in the book. The older women around her share her voice.
- **The switch between dark and comic — 8.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" The same impression has made Maylis laugh twice. The third time it cannot be finished, and nothing is said about why.
- **Prose — 7.** "She had learned a great deal about road salt." Plain and easy to follow. The flat closing sentence is the narrator's one comic move and it is used on almost every page.
- **The ending — 7.5.** "'I know what you picked. I was the other one.'" It costs enough. The last meeting of the two women is the weakest scene in the last chapter.
- **Clarity — 7.** "'So the one who stays in the chair drives the one who gets up,' Maylis said." The rules are stated once and plainly in chapter 4. Chapter 3 asks the reader to hold too much before that.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. From chapter 2 I did not consider stopping.

**Would I press it on a friend?** Yes, on the friend who likes dry village comedy with a death in it. No, on the friend who was promised The Good Place's joke rate.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below The Good Place at comedy.** Crouch does not joke, so he sets no bar here.

1. **Range.** The Good Place has six leads with six different ways of being funny, and any line can be given to its speaker blind. This book has one way: paper. The pad, the file, the minutes, the register, the care plan, the visitors' book, the form, the clipboard, the letters numbered to forty-two. A dozen characters get their laugh from a document or a procedure. Coralie's mimicry, Josiane's wrong deductions and Monsieur Daguerre's "Here" are the only other sources, and there is no fool anywhere in a cast of twenty.
2. **Gags that are also plot: level.** "Either", nine minutes to three, the kitchen timer, "No one came to tell him", "that's my door", the late-comer's taunt. Each is planted as a joke and collected as a consequence, which is what The Good Place does best. The difference is force. Its big reversal is a shock. Here the prologue and Maïté's question in chapter 3 ("Did you know he was out?") have told the reader that Lesca knew, so chapter 9 confirms and does not overturn. The reversals that do re-read what came before are smaller: Coralie's twelve years with the call log (chapter 10), and the clock ticking behind Larrère's voice (chapters 9, 13, 15).
3. **The moral problem: funny at its edges, silent at its centre.** A flood decided four to three by a card table, chaired from a car parked at the café door, with the casting vote given out of pique, is as good as anything in the series. "It reads that she opened him" is as good. But the two versions of the woman, who are the book's double act, argue the choice itself once, in a dozen lines. The Good Place would have staged that argument three ways and got a laugh from each. The problem is never made small. On that it is level.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The double act is rationed. (Chapters 4, 8, 12, 16.)**
"'There's no both. I've stood where you'll stand.'" … "'Both,' Maylis said."
The premise promises a woman who cannot choose facing herself as a woman who chose. They stand face to face four times in the book, and only the first meeting (chapter 4: "in a tie the chair decides." / "There's nobody in the chair.") is played as comedy. Everything else between them is wordless wrestling for a jaw or a hand. The central choice gets one exchange, and "Both" is asserted, not argued. So when Coralie says "That was Either, with the river in it", she is naming something the reader was never shown Maylis being warned off.
*Do:* in the chapter 8 meeting, let Maylis table "both" as items and let Lesca rule each one out of order with her own night as the evidence, in the form chapter 4 set up. A few hundred words. Then chapter 13 is a woman carrying out a motion the reader has watched fail.

**2. One source of comedy, and no fool. (Whole book.)**
"'For the minutes.'" (Madame Ducasse) "'That is for the file.'" (Sarthou) "'It's in the care plan.'" (Coralie) "'Is this a request, or a notification?'" (Hontaube) "'I've got nothing on the sheet for this.'" (the young man from Pau)
Every one of these is good. Together they are one joke told by five people, and Larrère, Lesca, Marie-Jo, Lafitte and Jeannot tell it too. Everyone in Peyrebarthe is shrewd, tart and economical. A farce needs somebody who is none of those.
*Do:* add nobody. The fool is already in the cast and has been handed a mop. "In his first week he said the words along with her, through the door, to be friendly" is the funniest backstory in the book, and its owner then speaks twice, both times about seven minutes. Give Bixente a mouth at bingo night and in the corridor in chapter 11: the one person in the village who joins in.

**3. The book's most important "Either" gets the least room. (Chapter 11.)**
"Her weight was on the handle." / "'Either,' Maylis said."
By now she has said "Open" twice at the café, crossed out her handover note and let a phone ring nine times. One question from Jeannot undoes that in one word, and the word is the book's running gag. A reader either laughs from habit or does not believe her. The reasoning that makes it true comes four paragraphs too late: "At three the water would be coming through the bank, and it would be the river's doing. At five it would have been hers."
*Do:* move that sentence in front of the word. It is the same logic as chapter 1 ("if Maylis ticked that box it would be Maylis's doing"), and the reader should recognise the shopping pad before she speaks, not after.

**4. The older women share one voice, and its best move is worn out by the end. (Chapters 3, 14, 15, 16.)**
"'There's a tile gone over the back bedroom.'" (Maïté, 3) "'Somebody has to stack them.'" (Marie-Jo, 14) "'That back gutter's overflowing again,' she said. 'Have a look at it before you go.'" (Maïté, 15) "'Sit down. You're grey.'" (Marie-Jo, 16) "'Then you'll dig it. With that side. I'll strap it first.'" (Coralie, 16)
A grave thing is said, and the answer is a chore. It is the book's signature switch and it is right every time, which is the problem: four of the five fall in the last three chapters, from three different women. Blind, "I'd have liked to be there" (Arlette) and "I'd have this one again" (Coralie) are the same speaker.
*Do:* keep the tile, the chairs, the gutter and the strapping. Cut the move from Marie-Jo in chapter 16; let her say that her son's van came up the hill twice and stop there. Give Arlette a way of being sharp that is not Coralie's.

**5. A comic routine stalls the evacuation. (Chapter 13.)**
"'I put that handle in number six,' he said." / "'I wrote to them too.'" / "'I know you did. They took note.'"
Coralie has just read out "Monsieur Castéra. Carried", and "There was nobody to carry him." The water is rising and room twelve is shut. Then two people stand on a doorstep and do the letters routine, and hand a key back and forth, for twenty lines.
*Do:* cut the three lines and the "Have it back" exchange. He says the gate is up, holds out the key, she hooks it on and says there is a man to carry. The handle already has its place in letter forty-two ("It says the handle stopped at nineteen, and who had been written to about it").

**6. The man in the car falls by post. (Chapter 16.)**
"'It says who had rung her, and from whose kitchen. My cousin got that off the screen of your phone on Saturday, and he's my cousin.'"
Larrère is the farce's obstacle for fifteen chapters. In the other village he gets the scene he has earned (chapter 15: "'I'll be brief,' he said." Then nothing). In Maylis's village, where he blackmailed her and rang from his lover's phone, the reader hears about it in a description of a letter. In the Port he walks back to his car with the folder held up out of the silt.
*Do:* Sarthou has the call on record and is standing a metre from him. Let him read it into his notebook in front of Madame Darrigrand, in his own diction: the time, and the name on the screen. One line. It takes nothing off Maylis, because Madame Darrigrand's "You had the key" still follows.

**7. The last meeting of the two women is an agenda. (Chapter 16.)**
"'It folds in three,' Lesca said. 'They take it off me on Tuesday.'" / "'I've been asked which I turned the handle for,' Lesca said."
Two consecutive speeches are tagged to Lesca, so it reads as if a line of Maylis's has dropped out. Around that, the two trade confessions in turn, one item each, as evenly as minutes. It is the double act's last scene and "It folds in three" is its one living line.
*Do:* fix the tags. Cut the exchange to what only this room can produce: the sash, "I don't know", the pillow. Lesca's account of the stairs repeats what chapter 5 showed.

**8. The hinge of the premise is delivered in a lump. (Chapter 3.)**
"On Thursday Lesca went home in the middle of the working day and opened the top drawer of the chest of drawers."
That sentence opens one paragraph of more than two hundred words which contains the card, the first night in the chair, the other card and the gauge. Chapter 2 gave Maylis three short scenes for the same events. Chapter 3 is 3,490 words in ten sections, and after the verb scene it is all mechanism until Arlette at the stove.
*Do:* break the paragraph into its beats, as chapter 2 does. Cut nothing from it.

**9. Two characters count their points. (Chapters 1 and 4.)**
"'There are four points, and I'll say them once. One. When Inguma sits on two people at the same time…'"
Jeannot's "One. … Two. … Three." is established in chapter 1 and pays off in chapter 9 with "And six. Bugger off." Lesca borrows the device in chapter 4. She has her own, and uses it in the same scene: "Next item", "Last item".
*Do:* make her four rules items. The count stays Jeannot's.

**10. Coralie's question is dropped. (Chapters 5, 7.)**
"'What is he to you?'" / "The mouth stayed shut, and Maylis wasn't the one holding it."
The village's mistake about Maylis and the Castagnets reaches Coralie as real jealousy ("I've had sixteen and a shopping pad"), and she asks twice. Nobody answers her, in either chapter or after. In chapter 13 she asks "The gate, or Castagnet?" without knowing what the other woman did to him.
*Do:* one sentence from Maylis in the chapter 8 kitchen, over the handover note: who the mayor is, and what she did at that gate. Coralie's question down the phone then carries it.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The council in chapter 6, whole.** Maylis pins the wrist, the bell does not come, and Larrère finishes eleven years of sentences. "I'm told I wave." It builds through six turns and ends on the hand ringing him off in the middle of the only question that matters.
2. **The ladder of things Inguma is given to count.** Stars, leaves, grass, gravel loads, roof tiles, verges ("Inguma was doing them on foot"), signatures, maize, Arlette's ponies ("That won't take long. They went up a ramp"), nineteen bags of grit, five fingers. Each job belongs to the person who sets it.
3. **Coralie and the oven door, three times.** Chapters 1, 8 and 16, including the third one that breaks off.
4. **The timer cutting off Maïté's verb, and the verb when it comes.** "'I have a verb,' Maïté said." … "The timer rang." Twelve chapters later: "*Left*. He was left out there."
5. **The taunt that changes.** "'Still in the box, were you?'" in chapter 1 becomes "'Had the key and went for a walk, did you?'" in chapter 16, said before the dealer looks up. It is the hardest thing in the ending and it is a joke.
