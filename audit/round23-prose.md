# Critic report: Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. 42,600 words, 83 scenes, 3,954 sentences.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "And there was Coralie, with forty-two people in their beds." (11) The stakes are a street, a man and a marriage, and all three are in one sentence by the time it matters.
- **Tension: 7.** "She took a step back towards it. She took a second, and a third." (13) Chapters 9 to 13 hold at 9. Chapters 5 to 8 stage the same fight over one jaw three times and drop to 6.
- **Progression: 6.** "At eight Lesca stood Maylis on the platform of the sluice gate" (5) and "That afternoon Lesca stood Maylis on the platform of the sluice gate" (7). The middle repeats its own scene openings, its own votes and its own struggle.
- **Comedy: 7.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station." (6)
- **Characters: 7.5.** "I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it." (16) Coralie has the best lines in the book and all of them sound like her.
- **Switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'" (13) The bingo gag from chapter 7 is carried into the flood and is not played for a laugh there.
- **Prose: 6.5.** "Coralie put the pot down. She pulled the shopping pad across the table and wrote the sentence out on it, pressing hard." (4) Nothing is wrong with either sentence. There are 3,954 sentences in the book and most are built like these two.
- **The ending: 8.** "She heard the passage, and the front door, and the car door shutting on a pillow." (16)
- **Clarity: 6.** "On the other side a woman lifted her handbag off the empty chair and put it on her knees. / She walked over to him and stopped at his shoulder" (12). "She" is Lesca. The grammar says it is the woman with the handbag. The book has two women called Maylis Lesca in one body and cannot afford this.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. From chapter 9 without stopping; chapters 5 to 8 on trust.
**Pressed on a friend?** On the friend who reads short literary novels, yes. On the friend who reads Crouch for speed, no: that reader stops in chapter 6.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, by about a point.** Level or above from chapter 9 to chapter 14.

1. **Below Crouch on speed of reading.** Crouch changes the shape of his sentences when the pace changes: a long run, then a one-line paragraph. This narrator has one shape for 42,600 words. The median sentence is 9 words, 57% are ten words or fewer, and 898 times two main clauses are joined with ", and". A spoon tipped back into a bowl and a bank breaking are written in the same rhythm. When chapter 13 borrows Crouch's verbless fragments to go faster, it reads as a second narrator.
2. **Below Crouch on trust.** Crouch lets an action stand and moves on. This narrator shows the gesture and then says what it meant. "because" appears 49 times and "wanted" 36.
3. **Above Crouch on objects, level with The Good Place on the returning gag.** The shopping pad, the window, the timer, the reflector, the pillow each come back changed and carry a turn. "Either" becomes "Both" and then "Either, with the river in it." The Good Place does exactly this with a catchphrase, and this is as good.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The narrator states the motive at the hinge, and chapter 16 then confesses it as news.** (13, 16)
In 13: "Lesca had rung once and turned the handle, and Maylis thought she could do better than that. She wanted the man and the gate, both, and she wanted to be the one who got them." And at the dip: "She wanted it not to be her."
In 16: "At the winch I thought I could do better than you. The man and the gate, both. I drove past the handle to be the one who did." And: "I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me."
The confessions in 16 are the book's last reversal, the one that should re-read chapter 13. The reader has already been handed both of them, in almost the same words, by the narrator. So 16 repeats where it should reveal.
*Do:* cut those two passages from 13. Keep the acts: the foot off the pedal for three breaths, the three steps back towards the lamp, the turn. Keep the cooler line in 11 ("At five it would have been hers") as the only plant. Then 16 tells the reader what she was doing, and they go back.

**2. The possession scenes are bookkeeping, and the head floats.** (5, 7, 8, 12)
"Lesca let go of the bowl and grabbed that wrist with the left hand, to drag it off. […] Maylis locked her elbow against her side, the bad side, and kept the palm where it was. Lesca gave up on the wrist and went for the fingers." (8)
The reader tracks a left hand, a right hand, a jaw, teeth and a mouth, each with an owner, and the narrator is inside both women in the same scene ("Lesca had the rest ready. Maylis felt her take a breath for it"). Because of that, "Maylis" and "Lesca" open 206 sentences: the pronoun can no longer be used. The fight for the jaw is staged in 5 ("Maylis threw herself at the jaw, and got it"), again in 7, again in 8.
*Do:* one head per scene. Chapter 5 belongs to Lesca, chapter 7 to Maylis. In each fight keep the one move that turns the scene and cut the choreography around it. The bingo scene shows the method that works: the hall reads it from outside as toothache.

**3. One sentence shape.** (throughout)
"She unlocked the box with the key on Maylis's belt, fitted the handle onto the winch and turned it with the left hand. She counted the clicks of the pawl while the chain came up wet." (5)
428 sentences begin "She", 119 begin or contain "It was", 79 begin "Then". The verbs are stage directions: "put" 120 times, "stood" 93, "kept" 63. Almost nothing is subordinated, so nothing is weighted.
*Do:* not fragments. Subordinate. Let a paragraph of action run as one long sentence and keep the short declarative for where it stops. Replace half the "put"s with the verb the hand actually does.

**4. The sentence that explains the beat.** (throughout)
"The letter would only cost her. The other sheet would cost Kévin." (6) "Whatever happened at that gate tonight, Maylis would do it herself." (9) "Coralie was behind that door because Maylis had told her where to start." (13) "She wanted to tell him to stay off the meadows on the eleventh, and she had nothing to tell him with but a dream." (4)
Each follows a gesture that had already said it: the blank sheet held up, the note crossed out line by line, Nadine's "You told her the bank end."
*Do:* test each such sentence: does it tell the reader something the gesture did not? "Maylis had sat down and said nothing for sixteen years, in the wrong kitchen" (6) passes. The four above fail. Cut the ones that fail.

**5. The narrator changes voice in chapter 13.**
"Second gear, through ruts full of water." "Mud to the ankle at every step. The torch in her left hand, her right arm pressed across her side." "No way around it." "Colder than the rain." "Torch up. Right arm across her side."
For twelve chapters every sentence has a subject and a verb. For six pages they do not, and then, inside the care home, they do again. The care-home pages are the better ones.
*Do:* restore the subjects and verbs. The clock stamps ("02:59.") already supply the speed and are the narrator's own device.

**6. Chapter 3 re-runs the week, and summarises what chapter 2 played.**
"On Thursday Lesca went home in the middle of the working day and opened the top drawer of the chest of drawers. The envelope had been in it since June 2014 […] Then she was sitting in the steel room from the tobacco barn, with both lamps lit in front of her. The arms of the chair were warm […]"
This is one paragraph of about 200 words, the only one of its kind in the book, and it reads as a synopsis. The envelope, the lying still, the warm chair and the half-drawn card were a full scene in the chapter before.
*Do:* cut it to what differs on this side: the word *right*, the phone in the drawer, the river gauge. Three sentences.

**7. Fifty-three of eighty-three scenes open on the clock, the calendar or the weather.**
"It was raining on Sunday the sixth of December, and the river gauge read forty under the line." (8) "It rained on Saturday the fifth of December, and the river gauge read fifty-five under the line." (7) "At eight", "At noon", "At seven", "At five past nine" open four scenes running in chapter 5.
The countdown is the thriller's clock and has to be there. As the first clause of every scene it makes them all start alike and start slowly.
*Do:* open on the line or the act, as the best openings do: "'Hand,' Coralie said." (9) Put the gauge reading inside the scene, where somebody reads it.

**8. Things explained twice, and scenes reported in a sentence.**
Explained again: "Larrère, its president" (8); "The truck had come because Lesca had signed the lease that turned their grazing land into a gravel pit" (10); "Madame Dufau, who didn't know what year it was" (10 and 14); "Since the night of the plastic bag she had spoken to Lesca only in council meetings, and then only to the wall" (14); "It was Lesca's rule. She had brought it in for one man" (15).
Reported: "She had tried the café first. The nine who had signed on Tuesday had watched Jeannot's hand go up, and a vote was a vote." (11) The village refusing her on the night is worth a scene and gets twenty-five words.
*Do:* gloss once, on first mention, and cut every repeat after chapter 4. Play the café refusal; it is shorter than the Hontaube phone call and matters more.

**9. Numbers collide.**
"Forty-two's started" and "what to do with forty-two people" are a page apart in chapter 5: one is a letter, the other a care home. Forty-one is the letter the postman brings and the count Coralie ends on. Twelve is a room, a date, a span of years, a commemoration, a bingo call and the seats in a minibus. Nineteen is the days of rain, the turn of the winch, Coralie's age at her first job and the bags of grit.
Some of these are meant, and "Twelve. One, two." is the best single stroke in the book. The reader cannot tell the meant from the accidental.
*Do:* keep twelve for the room and the date, nineteen for the winch and the rain, forty-two and forty-one for the beds. Renumber the letters, the minibus seats and Coralie's age.

**10. Usage and slips.**
- "tea towel" (1, 10) and "dish towel" (5, four times) for the same cloth.
- One piece of ground has six names: "flood meadows", "low meadows", "common pasture", "the common", "common land", "grazing land".
- British throughout ("wheelie bin", "pavement", "tarmac", "bugger off") but "truck" and "trailer".
- The narration contracts everywhere except the opening and three sentences in 11: "The gate had not moved yet." "It had not changed." "She could not lift one of her five fingers."
- The dealer is "the man dealing the cards" in 5 and 8 and is named Fernand for the first time in 16.
- In chapter 7 only, the narrator calls Maylis "the keyholder" ("By then the keyholder had served eleven coffees without a word").
- "Maylis let go of the teeth. She would have said it herself." (7) Said what, and which she.
- "'I had it at work by five, and I told them no.'" (8) No to what.
- Worn by repetition: "each one whole and then in parts" six times; Inguma compared to a sack five times ("like a full sack", "a sack being dragged", "a sack being lowered", "like a sack", "a sack of dry husks"); "with her back turned" four times; "didn't look up" five.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **"Either", from the pad to the river.** "I don't cook Either. There's no recipe for it." (1) through "Both." (13) to "That was Either, with the river in it." (16) Not a word.
2. **Scenes that end on an object.** "She changed the full stop to a comma and went on in the same ink to the end of the line." (3) "the car door shutting on a pillow." (16)
3. **The care home in chapter 13, from the glass doors to "Forty-one."** The four doors ("Nine was open. […] Ten was open. […] Eleven was open. […] Twelve was shut."), the pulse held "for the full half minute", Kévin counting five fingers in his own headlights.
4. **Larrère unrung.** "Eleven years I've been timed like an egg. I have sentences in that register with no ends on them." (6) And the window that goes "all the way down, into the door." (5)
5. **The sentence travelling through the square.** "the loudspeaker on the bank road said 'drowned him' after the square had finished hearing it." (14) Also "He had gone grey. The man on the stone hadn't had the time." (5)
