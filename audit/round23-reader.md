# Round 23 — Reader

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else opened but the brief.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes — 8.** One named man against forty houses and the woman she lives with, and the book gives the sum in metres and minutes: "Ten minutes against twenty-five. You turn the handle first." (8)
- **Tension — 7.5.** Chapters 11 to 13 are a 9; chapters 3, 4 and 6 let go of me. "The winch was six hundred metres behind her, and the red reflector was four hundred metres in front. The bank had six minutes left." (13)
- **Progression — 6.5.** The calendar is a good clock, but the book rewinds three times and I had to find my place each time. "The river over there was following this file, one line a day." (3)
- **Comedy — 8.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (6)
- **Characters — 8.** "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'" (7)
- **The switch between dark and comic — 8.5.** The bingo joke comes back in the flood and is not a joke any more: "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'" (13)
- **Prose — 7.** Easy to read until a sentence is cut one clause too short for a reader who was not there when it was planned: "'Six until nine,' Lesca said to the right hand. 'Fifteen hours for a lake.'" (5)
- **The ending — 7.5.** The last image is right; the two chapters before it are out of order. "Then he stepped over the string in his waders and began to dig." (16)
- **Clarity — 6.** "'I had an answer ready for that,' Lesca said. 'It was the winch, and three letters about it in the minutes. I can't get it out. It seems that in here you say the true one.'" (4) I read that four times and still do not know what the answer was.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes, once I was past chapter 3. At chapter 3 I would have put it down for the night and it is even odds I would have picked it up again.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On one who reads slowly, yes, with the words "get to chapter 5". On the friend who reads Crouch on a plane, not yet.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below the bar, by the width of its clarity.** From chapter 9 to chapter 14 it is level with the comparisons, and chapter 13 is above them. Chapters 3, 4 and 6 are where it loses.

- **Below Crouch on ease.** In Dark Matter or Recursion I never read a sentence twice to find out where I am, whose head I am in, or what the rule is. He says the rule in a plain line of dialogue and says it again when it matters. Here I went back about a dozen times: the "why now" answer in 4, the statutes in 6, the price of the night in 8, the moment Lesca lets go in 12, the old woman's seat in 15, the Tuesday that comes before the Sunday in 15 and 16.
- **Level on the marriage.** Crouch frightens me for Daniela by putting the wrong Jason in her bed. This book does the same on the stairs in 5 and the landing in 7, and then gives the wife the decision, which Crouch does not: "'That's her,' she said. 'In this hand. I've had sixteen years of her saying nothing, and I know it when I've got hold of it.'"
- **Level with The Good Place on rules as comedy, below it on rules as rules.** Lesca's four points in the steel room are that show's trick exactly: the rule is the joke ("'Then it's carried, or it's a tie, and in a tie the chair decides.' / 'There's nobody in the chair.'"). But two more rules arrive afterwards without being announced: that in the chair only the truth can be said, and that a laid place must be filled. The second one carries the meaning of the death, and I had it only from an old woman's bedtime speech in chapter 2.
- **Above both on the cost.** One woman of ninety-four drowns, and I had laughed with her twice and heard her win a duck. Crouch's dead are counted. This one I will remember.

### The reader's four answers

**What I did not understand.**
- What the gate does, and why putting the river on the meadows does not put it on the forty houses next door to them. I assembled it by chapter 4. I was never told.
- What Lesca's "answer ready" was in chapter 4, and what "'Nobody was to blame for a dry one'" means.
- What Maylis learned from the statutes in 6, and why Lesca wrote to the prefect in 7 if his order has to come down through Larrère.
- What "*Paid.*" means at the foot of the handover note in 8.
- Whether Madame Etcheto would have died if Maylis had turned back to the winch, given that a seat at the table of the dead was already waiting to be filled.

**Where I was bored, lost or skimming.** Chapter 3 from the committee to the Darrigrand step. Chapter 6 from the register to Larrère's "written order of the president". Nowhere else.

**Where I could not stop.** Chapter 5 entire. Bingo night to the landing in 7. Chapter 9. From Jeannot on the platform in 11 to Marie-Jo's chairs at the end of 14, without a break.

**What I will remember in a month.** The thing on the wardrobe being sent to count stars. A car window going all the way down. Two women fighting over one jaw at a coffee urn while the hall decides it is toothache. The red reflector shining for four more minutes. A man swearing in six numbered points, alive. "Forty-one." A sentence walking back through a square row by row, and a man clapping because he thinks it is over.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Chapter 3 carries a whole second book and makes the reader wait for the one thing chapter 2 promised.** (Ch 3, structural)

Chapter 2 ends on "'Come back across,' her mouth said. Maylis hadn't said it." Chapter 3 then goes back four nights and runs eleven sections. The bed, which is what I turned the page for, is the seventh. Before it I am given the stone, the committee, the three-minute rule, Marie-Jo's feud ("handed her sash back across the council table in a plastic bag"), the verb debate, the tile, the lake and the dredger, and the landing. Then the entire crossing into the chair, which took Maylis all of chapter 2, is done in one block: "On Thursday Lesca went home in the middle of the working day and opened the top drawer of the chest of drawers." That paragraph holds the envelope, the card, the wardrobe, the steel room, the other card and a borrowed morning at the gate, and I could not tell which sentence was which woman's memory. After the bed come Darrigrand's step, the motion, Arlette and the motion again.

What I would do: cut the chapter by a third and move the bed up to the middle. Marie-Jo's 2018 history goes to chapter 6, where Maylis sits at that table and needs it. Darrigrand's step goes to chapter 10, when the square is being taped, or goes altogether. The Thursday paragraph becomes three short ones or two sentences, since I watched the same thing from the other seat a page ago. Keep the opening minute, the stone with frost in the letters, the timer ringing on Maïté's verb, the roof, the landing, the bed, Arlette, and the last line.

**2. The ground is never drawn, and the whole book stands on it.** (00 and Ch 1, structural)

"She took the winch handle out of its box. / For three breaths she held it, and she did not fit it to the winch." I did not know what the handle would do, so the three breaths weighed nothing. The first account I get of the gate is that it empties a duck pond: "'On Monday you put the gate up with the river low, and my water ran off into it.'" Then it is "a door in a bank" (3), then it lets the river "out over grass" (4). The tide is mentioned in passing. The Port, the bank, the meadows and "the terrace" are never set side by side, and I never learned why three hundred hectares of flooded meadow stay off the houses that stand beside them.

What I would do: two plain sentences in the opening, between the platform and the handle, saying what raising the gate does: the river goes out over the empty meadows, the weight comes off the bank, the houses stay dry. Then her pointing the headlights down the track becomes a question I can read. And one sentence in chapter 1 that lays the place out in the order she drives it: the houses under the bank, the gate further along, the meadows below the gate, the village on the high ground.

**3. The death is accounted for twice, and I cannot tell which account is the true one.** (Ch 14 and 15, structural)

In 13 Madame Etcheto drowns because Maylis walked on from the dip. In 14 "Somebody had sat down to play with the dead, and it wasn't Lesca," and in 15: "'Then it wasn't Inguma that came for her. It was you.' / 'Yes.'" So she also died because Lesca would not take her seat. If the seat had to be filled by morning whatever happened on the track, then the choice at the dip, which the book spent twelve chapters loading, decided nothing. If it did not have to be filled, the scene in 15 is decoration. The book does not say, and it is the question I closed chapter 15 with.

What I would do: one line that fixes the order. The place was laid because Lesca held on for eight rings; who filled it was settled on the track, at the dip. Lesca can say it to the old woman, or the voice can stop calling "Lesca. Maylis." only when the bank goes. Either way both women keep their share, and the dip keeps its weight.

**4. The explanation of why the flood is coming now is the least clear passage in the book, and it is the keystone of the premise.** (Ch 4)

"'I had an answer ready for that,' Lesca said. 'It was the winch, and three letters about it in the minutes. I can't get it out. It seems that in here you say the true one.'" Maylis asked why it rains now. A winch does not make rain. And the rule that the chair forces the truth appears here for the first time, as an aside, after Lesca has just counted the rules out as four. Then: "'Nobody was to blame for a dry one,' Maylis said," which I cannot parse.

What I would do: let Lesca begin the false answer out loud so that I can hear what it is (the neglected bank, Larrère, somebody at fault) and have it die in her mouth. Make the truth rule point five, said as plainly as the other four. Give Maylis's line a subject.

**5. Maylis crosses a world to find out who can order the gate raised, and I could not tell what she found.** (Ch 6)

"Maylis ran the left forefinger down it and stopped under *on the order of the prefect*." Then "The right hand came across and put its own forefinger further along the same line, under *sent down through the mayor*." Then Larrère at once: "'The line above that one,' he said. 'The written order of the president.'" Three clauses in fifteen lines, each shown by a finger. I skimmed. In chapter 7 Lesca writes to the prefect anyway, and in 11 the envelope "said nothing about a gate".

What I would do: one sentence in Maylis's head after the two fingers. The prefect can order it, but his order comes down through the mayor, and at home the mayor is the man in the car, so there is no way around him. And one clause in 7 for why Lesca writes regardless.

**6. Chapter 15 runs to Tuesday night and chapter 16 starts again on Sunday.** (Ch 15 and 16, structural)

I read the council remove Lesca and Coralie say "'Good night, Maylis'". Then, in the last meeting in the steel room: "'It folds in three,' Lesca said. 'They take it off me on Tuesday.'" I had already watched them take it. For half a page I did not know when I was. The same exchange has a second snag: that line is followed directly by "'I've been asked which I turned the handle for,' Lesca said," two speeches running, both tagged to Lesca, so it reads as though a line of Maylis's has dropped out between them.

What I would do: end 15 on the ladder, with Larrère going back down the lane having said nothing. Put the council and the landing after the last steel-room scene, or move the council to Sunday evening. Join or separate the two Lesca speeches.

**7. The turn of Lesca's whole story is carried by a sentence I had to read three times.** (Ch 12)

"The hand locked on the brake was the hand that had held the stair post against her, and at the end of the headlights the reflector was red, and no nearer than it had been twelve years ago." Then the motion in italics, then "In the steel room the mayor let go of the chair." I worked out afterwards that she lets go because while two women fight over one van nobody reaches the man. On the page I had the letting go before I had the reason.

What I would do: one plain sentence before she lets go. The van has stopped, the phone is at eight rings, and every ring she spends holding the chair is one the man at the end of the track does not get.

**8. The counting jobs are reported when they are finished and not when they are set.** (Ch 5 and 7)

"'Fifteen hours for a lake.'" (5) I had the plan from a conversation with Arlette two chapters earlier and had to go back for it. "'It's done my signatures,' Lesca said, low, to the right hand. 'Every one since 2015.'" (7) That job was never set on the page at all. These are the stakes of every crossing, how long she has, and I learn each one as it runs out.

What I would do: half a line at the moment of crossing each time, saying what she gave it. Arlette's ponies in 10 are done right and show how.

**9. I do not know what Maylis is asking for in the handover note.** (Ch 8)

"*In return, one day in your mayor's chair, when I ask.* / *Paid.*" She has already had a day in that chair, in 6. Is "Paid" the day she has had, or the heading of the bill? In the steel room it is "'For the day in your chair I want the timer, and a table of people who won't sit down until I do,'" and in 9 it is "to be paid for it with a day in the mayor's sash". Three wordings, and the bargain is the thing she crosses out at the end of 9.

What I would do: cut "*Paid.*" or make it a sentence. Say once that she wants a second day.

**10. Forty-one and forty-two each mean two things.** (Ch 5, 13, 16)

Forty-one registered letters and "'Put it in forty-two'"; forty-two people in their beds and "'Forty-one,' Coralie said." In 5 the two meet in one scene: "'Forty-two's started,' Jeannot said," and four pages on, "'one was what to do with forty-two people.'" At the last line of 13, for a moment, I thought of a letter. If the echo is meant, the book never uses it. If it is not, it is in the way at the worst possible line.

What I would do: move the letters to another number, or make the echo pay once, out loud.

Smaller, each one word's work: the card dealer is "the man dealing the cards" for fifteen chapters and "Fernand" in the sixteenth; Saint Catherine's Day is given no date.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **"Either."** The shopping pad, the eggs, the gendarme who cannot summon it, and five o'clock on the platform with her weight on the handle: "'Either,' Maylis said. / 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. 'That's how the vote left it.'" Then Coralie's "That was Either, with the river in it."
2. **Bingo night and the hall of the dead.** The jaw at the urn, "Tooth" and "Castagnet", the old woman finishing the words over her duck, and the same waxed floor and paper tablecloths when the wall of the steel room comes away. "'The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies.'"
3. **Lesca's night and the phone call that answers it, in chapter 9.** "The reflector went on shining in her headlights at the end of the track for four more minutes." Then nine rings, and "'And six. Bugger off.'"
4. **Chapter 13 from the dip to "Forty-one".** "She wanted it not to be her." The corridor of chairs, the man who asks if it is Thursday, Kévin counting five in his own headlights, room twelve, the hatbox on the wardrobe.
5. **The sentence at the microphone and what the square does with it.** "'Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him.'" The loudspeaker on the bank road saying it late, the man who claps, the child who asks, and "'Somebody has to stack them.'"
