# Critic report, round 23: Thriller

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. 42,600 words. Nothing else opened but the brief.

Overall against the bar: **7. Good, and short of it.** Chapters 11 to 13 are at the level of the comparison. The first 15% and the middle third are not.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** "Ten minutes against twenty-five. You turn the handle first." (8) One sum, stated early, walked with a watch in 7, and paid to the minute in 13.
- **Tension: 7.** "She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it broke." (13) That is the level. Against it: "She had learned a great deal about road salt." (6)
- **Progression: 6.5.** "Two. I hadn't given the eleventh a thought until you handed it to me." (5) Every attempt to save the man bolts his hatch tighter, which is the right machine. But the one attempt to stop the flood itself is half a sentence: "The river gauge kept to the file anyway" (7).
- **Comedy: 7.5.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station." (6)
- **Characters: 8.** "I'd have this one again." (5)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" (16) The book's running joke fails in her mouth, and that is how the reader learns what the night cost her.
- **Prose: 7.5.** "Then the water rose up the door and covered it, and the red light went out." (9) Plain and fast. It drops into summary at the places listed below.
- **The ending: 8.** "I know what you picked. I was the other one." (16)
- **Clarity: 6.** "In the steel room the mayor let go of the chair." (12) The turn of the whole possession plot, and the reader has to work out why.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. From chapter 8 I could not have stopped.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who loves The Good Place, yes. On a Crouch reader, not yet: I would have to tell them it starts moving 6,500 words in, and a book I have to apologise for is not at the bar.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch, overall.** Three things put it there.

1. **Speed.** In Dark Matter the hero is taken in the first chapter and is standing in the wrong world by the second. Here the first staged crossing and the date of the flood both arrive about 6,500 words in, 15% of the book, after a rewind and a committee meeting. The opening page (00) and the endings of 1 and 2 buy that time; chapter 3 spends it.
2. **The premise is never tested from inside.** A Crouch book answers the reader's first objection on the page, usually by having the hero try it and fail. Here the reader's first objection (the flood comes across the link, so cut the link) is never tried in a scene. See fault 1.
3. **Chapter endings in the last third.** Crouch ends on the turn. Chapters 0, 2, 3, 8, 11 do this as well as he does ("It rang once."; the motion; the card). Chapters 10, 14 and 15 end on a grace note, with the engine off.

**Level with Crouch** on the fear for a marriage. Dark Matter makes you afraid that another you is better with your wife. "I'd have this one again", said to the wrong woman while the right one listens from inside one hand, is that fear, done as well.

**Above Crouch** on what the ending costs. Crouch lets the family get away. This book drowns the old woman who taught the heroine the words, sends the wife to Bayonne with a pillow, and has the saved man say in writing that he was never in danger.

**Level with The Good Place** on the reversal that re-reads what came before: "Castagnet, house" on the screen at 02:53 re-reads every scene of Larrère at a car window, and "That was Either, with the river in it" re-reads the heroine's one brave word.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

### 1. Nobody tries to cut the wire. (Chapters 4, 7, 11. Structural: it needs a scene.)

> "The flood is the biggest thing I have, and it's been coming across to you ever since, one day of my file at a time." (4)

> "For two nights after that Maylis pushed up against Inguma with every breath she had, and kept herself out of the chair. The river gauge kept to the file anyway, and on Friday morning it read seventy under the line." (7)

From chapter 4 the reader knows the river is carried by two women lying under one creature. The first thing a thriller reader asks is why they do not stop. Madame Etcheto has said "It comes to that bed." Arlette will say "Sleep on your side, Maylis. Tonight. With your knees up, so it has nowhere to sit." There is a spare room. Yet in chapter 4 neither woman raises it, Maylis goes back to the chair the next night out of curiosity, and the only test of the idea is the half sentence above. The second test is a hope in a van: "she hoped Inguma wouldn't find her under it" (11). This is the haunted-house question, and the book answers it in passing. Until it is answered on the page, the flood is not inevitable, and a flood that is not inevitable is not frightening for seven chapters.

**Do:** two additions. In chapter 4, let Maylis say it in the steel room, where it has to be true: then stop coming. Lesca cannot; it has sat on her for twelve years and she has never once kept it off. In chapter 7, stage the two nights. Maylis on her side through the rib, or on the kitchen floor, awake until four. Friday morning at the gauge, with the line from Lesca's file already in her head: seventy under, to the centimetre. Door shut. From that page the eleventh is coming whatever anyone does, and the charge Maylis only makes in 16 ("It was your river") is live from the middle of the book, when it can still drive a scene.

### 2. Chapter 3 rewinds, and buries the clock in a summary. (Structural.)

> "Four nights earlier, at nine minutes to three in the morning on Tuesday the twenty-fourth of November, Lesca, the mayor of Peyrebarthe, was awake in her own bed, and she couldn't move."

> "The last line in her own handwriting was for the eleventh of December, at ten at night, and after that the file was typed, by the gendarmes."

Chapter 2 ends on the best hook of the first quarter: her mouth says a sentence she did not say. Chapter 3 answers it 2,300 words later, after going back four nights, a committee debating a verb, a roof tile and a bowl of soup. Then the single fact that drives the book, the date, and the detail that makes it chilling, the handwriting turning into typing, are delivered inside one 190-word paragraph of summary ("On Thursday Lesca went home in the middle of the working day…") that also contains her first sight of the steel room. At 3,490 words this is where a Crouch reader puts the book down.

**Do:** keep the order, cut 800 words from committee, roof and soup (keep "It reads that she opened him", keep Maïté's verb cut off by the bell, keep "It hadn't had it yet"). Break the Thursday paragraph into two staged scenes: the chair, and the file open on the desk, read line by line until the hand changes.

### 3. The turn of the possession plot is written in code. (Chapter 12.)

> "*That the mayor remain seated.*
> In the steel room the mayor let go of the chair."

For eight chapters the threat is that Lesca takes the body and the bed and keeps them: "I've had one bowl of soup at your table. I won't go." (8) She gives that up between the eighth ring and the next line. The reasons offered are two images, the hand on the brake and a reflector "no nearer than it had been twelve years ago". The reader must deduce that she has seen the man drowning a second time while two hands fight over a handbrake. There is a second problem under it: both women want to drive to the blind, so for eight rings the fight has no visible alternative outcome, only a different driver.

**Do:** at ring one, say what is being fought for: if Lesca drives, she is sitting on Inguma and she never leaves. At ring eight, give her one plain sentence of the sum she is so good at: the van is not moving, the bank has ten minutes, and she is spending them. Then the motion, then she lets go. Nothing else in the chapter changes.

### 4. The day in the other village is the one time a woman might die by the rules, and it costs nothing there. (Chapter 6.)

> "If it never came, all of this was hers. … It came with Coralie finding her dead."

> "She had learned a great deal about road salt."

Rule four hangs over Maylis for five overdue hours, and the hours go to a signing gag and an eleven-minute speech by Larrère. Then Inguma arrives. In Lesca's village a mayor has frozen in front of her council and nobody mentions it again; chapter 10 opens on Arlette. At home the price (the spare room) is told at the top of 7. And what Maylis went for, the rule about the gate, is a dead end. What she actually brings back is Kévin with his cap in his hands, and chapter 8 uses it without saying so ("Maylis didn't say it a third time").

**Do:** cut once, mid-council, to the bedroom at home: the chart, the half-hourly pulse, the thumb over the doctor's number. That puts a clock in the scene. In chapter 8, when "Labat, Kévin" goes up against the glass, give her the cap, in five words. In 10, let one councillor's look tell Lesca what her body did on Wednesday.

### 5. A prophecy proved in public changes nothing. (Chapters 8 and 11.)

> "'I wrote that eight days ago, Guy.'" (8)

> "The nine who had signed on Tuesday had watched Jeannot's hand go up, and a vote was a vote." (11)

Lafitte reads the prediction back word for word, at the café door, to the man who has just fulfilled it. In a thriller that is the scene after which the village moves. Here nine people sign, and one sentence disposes of them three days later. Madame Darrigrand, who believed enough to sign, sleeps under the bank on the Friday with her baby and on Sunday says "You had the key." The Port does not behave as if it is in danger, so the reader stops believing it is, until 03:11.

**Do:** let the believers act and be blocked by the same word that blocks Coralie: orange. And put one of the nine on the bank at five on Friday to watch her say "Either". "You had the key" from a woman who saw the handle in her hand is worth three times the same line at second hand.

### 6. Chapter 11 runs too many errands before the clock strikes.

> "'Is this a request, or a notification?'"

> "She knocked two more times, and the stovepipe went on smoking."

3,690 words, nine scenes. The night-of chapter opens on a comic phone call about a form. Jeannot then refuses her at the van window, on the platform, through the hatch at 17:55 and by phone at eleven, after refusing her by phone in 9. The walk to the hatch is the third timing of the same kilometre (7, 11, 13) and it pre-plays the climax: when the hatch lifts at 03:03, the reader has already seen it lift, seen the eye, smelled the stove.

**Do:** Hontaube in four lines, ending on "You'll belong to us." Cut the 17:30 walk and the knock. Let "Don't ring tonight, and don't knock" be his last word, and let the hatch open for the first time in 13. If Larrère's two missed calls are needed, she can be at the patch with her hand on the clay.

### 7. The falling action is 6,800 words, 16% of the book, and it keeps the wrong thing back.

> "'Then it wasn't Inguma that came for her. It was you.'
> 'Yes.'" (15)

After "Forty-one" the reader wants one scene: Maylis and Coralie in the kitchen. It comes 3,800 words later. Chapter 14 earns its place; it is the second climax. Chapter 15 is four closures at 400 words each, and only the back gate has a turn in it. Its first scene explains aloud what "A chair was pulled in" had already done. Its council scene and its landing scene end exactly where the reader expects.

**Do:** fold the Etcheto scene (keep "Nobody emptied hers") and the Maïté scene into the end of 14. Give the council vote six lines, ending on "Lesca, Maylis. Surname first." Go straight from there to the silt. One chapter fewer, and the kitchen arrives while the reader still has the corridor in their legs.

### 8. The room that forces the truth fires a blank the reader has heard twice. (Chapters 10, 15, 16.)

> "'It seems that in here you say the true one.'" (4)

> "'In here it would have to be true. I don't know.'" (16)

Which did she turn the handle for is the book's last open question. It is answered "all she found was a handle, turning" in 10, "I don't know. I had the whole sum" in 15, and again in 16. The steel room was set up in 4 as the place where a false answer cannot be got out of the mouth. When it is finally used for this, it certifies an answer already given twice. There is also a slip in 16: two consecutive speeches are both tagged to Lesca ("'It folds in three,' Lesca said." / "'I've been asked which I turned the handle for,' Lesca said."), which reads as a dropped line.

**Do:** make the room do the work, as it did with the winch. She tries "the houses" and cannot get it out. She tries "her" and cannot get it out. What comes is "I don't know." Same answer, and the gun on the wall has gone off. Then 10 and 15 are the rehearsals and 16 is the proof.

### 9. The card with her name on it is forgotten on the night. (Chapters 8, 12, 13.)

> "LESCA, MAYLIS." (8)

> "Her place stayed laid." (12)

A place laid is filled by morning, by her or by another. The card carries a name two women share. Lesca refuses the seat. The obvious "another" is the other Lesca, Maylis, who then wades rib-deep into an unlit wing; the next is Coralie, who is at door twelve. Chapter 13 never lets the reader fear either. The best plant in the book is not in the reader's mind at the moment it should be.

**Do:** one line at the top of the ramp: a card, a saucer of maize, her name surname first. One more when Nadine says Coralie went back for twelve. Leave "Twelve. One, two." where it is in 14.

### 10. Two set-ups in the run-in neither fail on stage nor pay. (Chapters 7, 8.)

> "She gave him the date the bank would break, and she asked for his order to raise the gate that afternoon." (7)

> "*In return, one day in your mayor's chair, when I ask.*
> *Paid.*" (8)

The letter to the prefect is the plan from chapter 4 ("Whose order?" "The prefect's."). Its outcome is an envelope that "said nothing about a gate" and one remark from Larrère. The handover note is written, read by Coralie, and crossed out a chapter later. Each is a way out that closes off the page.

**Do:** close the prefect on the page: Larrère opens Pau's reply at his car window and reads her the one sentence that sends it back down through the mayor. The note earns its place through Coralie's "And then there's me, as a distance"; cut the bargain lines at its foot, which nothing collects.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **"It rang once."** The last line of 00, and its repayment in 9, where Maylis lets it ring nine times and is sworn at in six points by a living man.
2. **"Either," on the platform at five on Friday, with her weight on the handle.** (11) No interior, no excuse, three lines. It is the real choice of the book, and it is right that the night is only its consequence.
3. **"Castagnet, house."** The kitchen clock heard behind the voice in 9, the name on the screen in 13, the clock on the wall in 15, and "I'll be brief" followed by nothing.
4. **Chapter 13 from the dip to "Forty-one."** Her own van blocking the way back ("'I told you,' he said. 'Not in that van.'"); "She had given the bank ten minutes. It had held for sixteen."; Kévin counting five fingers in his headlights; the pencil line through the last name.
5. **Coralie on the fourth stair and on the landing.** "Are you coming up?" and "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak." With "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." in 10, these are why the reader is afraid for two marriages at once.
