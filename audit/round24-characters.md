# Critic report — lens: Characters

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else opened.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes — 7.5.** "The hatch was twenty-five minutes away on foot. The bank had ten." A named man against forty-two named beds, in two numbers. It takes until the end of ch 3 for the reader to hold both.
- **Tension — 7.** "In her hand, the phone began to ring." Chapter ends are strong from ch 2 on. Inside ch 6 to ch 8 the fights for the body have a known result, because the thing always comes and fetches the visitor home.
- **Progression — 6.** "She had pushed on Sunday night and again on Monday, and the rib had let her have one breath each time." That sentence in ch 8 repeats the action of ch 7. The middle moves the river gauge forward and the two women sideways.
- **Comedy — 8.** "'And six. Bugger off.'" The people are funny in themselves, and each in a different way.
- **Characters — 7.5.** Villagers and Coralie 8.5, the two women as a pair 7. "In sixteen years Maylis had never got that laugh out of her. Lesca had got it in ten minutes, with Maylis's mouth." The pair is set up that well and then given four business meetings.
- **The switch between dark and comic — 8.5.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" The book's running joke breaks off in Coralie's mouth two days after room twelve. Nothing in the comparisons does it more cleanly.
- **Prose — 7.5.** "She watched her sentence travel back through the square and down the road at walking pace, row by row." Plain and exact. Stage business with hands and wrists is repeated until it stops registering.
- **The ending — 8.** "Then he stepped over the string in his waders and began to dig." It costs a death, a job and a marriage, and it thanks nobody.
- **Clarity — 6.** "'So the one who stays in the chair drives the one who gets up,' Maylis said." Five numbered rules in ch 4, a sixth in ch 8, and every villager in two versions. I had to stop and work out who was driving in ch 5, ch 7 and ch 12.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. From ch 9 I could not have stopped. Between ch 6 and ch 8 I read on for Larrère and Coralie, not for the two leads.

**Press it on a friend?** On a friend who loves The Good Place, yes, with a warning about the rules in ch 2 to ch 4. On a friend who reads Crouch for speed, not yet.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Above Crouch on people.** Crouch's wife is what the hero wants back; she is seen through his wanting. Coralie refuses that role out loud: "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." The second Coralie is better still: "Because you'd have told me." No one in Dark Matter or Recursion has a reason that good for a silence.

**Below Crouch on fear for the marriage.** Crouch makes one ordinary kitchen evening and then makes the reader watch another self walk into it. This book builds a better kitchen in ch 1 (the pad, the oven door, the eggs) and writes the threat down in ch 3: "*That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*" After that the threat is played as a comedy of jaws and wrists. I was afraid for the marriage once, at the foot of the stairs in ch 5.

**Level with The Good Place on the supporting cast, below it on the leads.** Larrère behind his few centimetres of window, Jeannot with his numbered grievances, Josiane with her theory of adultery, Sarthou billing the village for his own sandbags: each is wrong in his own way, which is the series' method. But the series puts its leads in a room and makes them change each other by talking. These two meet four times, and three of the four meetings are agenda items. The series' reversals re-read what came before; here ch 10 (Coralie has known the minute for twelve years) does that, and it is the best thing in the book. The reversal the two leads owe each other, in ch 12, does not.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Lesca's surrender happens in one narrated sentence. (ch 12)**
"She had come to take this life from a woman who never said what she wanted, and that woman had just said it. / *That the mayor remain seated.* / In the steel room the mayor let go of the chair."
For eleven chapters she will not be sent home. She has sat down on the thing itself to stay. Then one line from Maylis undoes her, the narrator explains why, and an italic motion that says "remain seated" is set over the act of getting up. Her own reasons for crossing are not answered by what Maylis says: "Lesca wanted to be looked at by the one who didn't know", and "I'm bringing this one up the ladder myself." This is the hinge of the double's arc and I read it twice to find it.
*Do:* make it a choice the reader watches. She is holding the chair with a man at the end of the headlights, "no nearer than it had been twelve years ago", and every ring she spends holding on is his. She has made this count before. Let her see that she is making it again, with the bed on one side and the man on the other, and let go for him. Then "Go to the blind first" is her decision and not a parting gift, and ch 16's "it was my own night I was putting right" has something to confess. Cut the explaining sentence. Cut the italic or move it to where she sits down.

**2. The two women never get to know each other. (ch 4, ch 8, ch 16)**
"'There are four points, and I'll say them once.'" That is their first meeting. Their second is an argument about a tide. Neither asks the other one thing a person would ask her double. The only exchange in which they speak as two people with one life is six lines on the last night: "'Is yours in the house?'" "'Across the landing. Four steps. Yours?'" "'She took the pillow.'" What each envies is confessed only then ("I wanted your sash"), after it can change nothing.
The room has the best device in the book, "Standing in this room, a woman can only say what's true", and it is only ever tested on Lesca ("'I w—'"). It never catches Maylis.
*Do:* rebuild the ch 8 meeting. Keep the river discovery to four lines. Spend the rest on each woman being stopped by the room when she tries to say she does not want the other's life. Maylis cannot get out "I don't want your square". Lesca cannot get out that it is only about Castagnet. That is the Good Place scene this book has the machinery for, it would be funny, and it is what fault 1 needs behind it.

**3. Maylis's worst decision is made for her by a bone. (ch 7, ch 8, ch 9)**
"on the first breath the rib caught and held. She had no second breath to push with, and she had to lie still." And in ch 8: "She had no push to give it."
Her flaw is that she will not choose. The choice that brings the river into her village is whether to let Lesca sit, and the rib takes it away from her. Then on the Thursday, after she has told Lesca "Don't come on Friday" and knows what each night costs, she goes in herself with no reason given: "That night Maylis lay still under Inguma, and then she was in the chair". She is excused the first time and unexplained the second.
*Do:* on the Friday of ch 7 let her be able to hold it off a third night, and lie still anyway, for a reason of her own. She has just heard "I'd have this one again", and the house is worse without the one who answers. On the Thursday of ch 9 give her the reason in a line: she wants the night Lesca will not tell her. Then the forty-one are partly hers before the dip, and "I had the key" weighs more.

**4. For a third of the book the lead is a brake. (ch 5 to ch 8)**
"Maylis had her own teeth shut, and she meant to keep them shut until the hall was empty."
From the kitchen of ch 5 to the kitchen of ch 8 everything Maylis wants is a negative: keep the jaw, keep off the stairs, keep out of the chair. The book ends that run on a literal handbrake. Meanwhile the double gets the laugh, the window, the café and the soup, and Coralie says "I'd have this one again." The reader agrees with her, and for too long.
Maylis is most alive when she wants something by mistake: "'I want that,' Maylis said, before she could stop the mouth." And: "She liked it, and she slowed down to get more of it." There is one chapter of that.
*Do:* in each scene where she is driven, give her one thing she wants and takes. At the bingo, let her open the mouth once on purpose because Lesca is about to say what she would have said. The text is one clause from it: "Maylis let go of the teeth. She would have said it herself."

**5. Coralie's own want arrives at the midpoint as a speech. (ch 4, ch 7)**
The plant: "she reached behind the fruit bowl and took out an envelope that had been standing there since March." The payoff, three chapters later: "I want the geriatric floor at the hospital in Bayonne, on nights."
Until that landing she is the person who asks what Maylis wants. She is the stake for both women and the object of a written motion. She needs a direction of her own before she is the prize, or she is a function of the lead's flaw.
*Do:* put Ithurbide and the torch and the list into ch 2. Coralie is already standing in that corridor at night, running it. Let the reader see what is in the envelope in ch 1, and see her not post it.

**6. The sheet with Kévin's name gives Maylis an alibi the book takes back too late. (ch 6, ch 8, ch 11, ch 16)**
"*Labat, Kévin.* / Behind the car the boy was still on his ladder. Maylis didn't say it a third time." Then, after everything: "That's not why I kept the gate shut. I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me."
During the scenes the reader is invited to read her silence as loyalty to a boy. The retraction is one line to the boy himself, a day after the dead are counted.
*Do:* remove the alibi before five o'clock on the Friday. Kévin is on the platform in ch 11 and already says "I'm in three of his letters." Have him tell her he would do the six months. Then "'Either,' Maylis said", with her weight on the handle and nothing left to hide behind, is entirely hers.

**7. The woman the reader will miss most is given the rulebook to recite. (ch 2, ch 4, ch 15)**
"When it sits on two people at once, it has them both in one seat, and one of the two may go home with the other."
Madame Etcheto is a person when she says "A rib doesn't laugh" and when she keeps both hands on the duck. In that speech she is delivering a rule cut to fit this plot exactly, which her mother happened to tell her. Lesca repeats it as numbered points two chapters later, and "a place that is laid is filled by morning, by her or by another" is said three times in the book.
*Do:* leave her the words, the counting and the bingo of the dead. Those are hers, and they are what kills her. Let the two-in-one-seat rule be found out by the women in the chair, once.

**8. What Lesca did to Marie-Jo is never in a scene with Marie-Jo. (ch 3, ch 6, ch 14)**
"She had been Lesca's deputy mayor until a night in 2018, when she handed her sash back across the council table in a plastic bag."
The reason is in ch 6, in the other village, through Maylis's eyes: cameras on the gates, six months for eels, and the boy is her son. The two facts are never set side by side. The reconciliation ("'Maylis,' she said.") and its last line about "the coat you gave him when he came out of prison" pay off a sum the reader has to do alone. It is the most personal harm Lesca's counting has done to someone still in the room, and the room never has it out.
*Do:* one exchange in ch 3 or ch 6 across that council table, through the chair, about a cap.

**9. Two of the three strongest voices have the same signature. (ch 1, ch 4)**
Jeannot: "'One. A duck comes down at night onto standing water, and onto nothing else. Two. A hand of water does it.'" Lesca: "'One. When Inguma sits on two people at the same time, it puts them in one seat, and that's this chair.'"
When they meet in ch 5 they count at each other and nobody notices. Lesca already has her own music, the agenda: "Next item", "All those in favour", "apologies received".
*Do:* give numbered points to Jeannot alone. Or have him notice, once, that she counts like him.
Two smaller drifts of the same kind. Ch 6 says "The Kévin she knew asked three questions in every sentence", and after ch 2 he does not. The man who deals the cards is "the dealer" for fifteen chapters and "Fernand" in ch 16.

**10. Lesca's eleven years across a landing have nothing on the other side of them. (ch 3)**
"Coralie had carried her pillow across that landing in the February after the flood, and it had stayed in the spare room."
What she had before that February is not on the page. What she takes from the other bed is two fingers and a laugh. Maylis has one memory of her own ("'You,' Maylis said."). Lesca, who shared that night in 2010, has none. So the reader is told what she wants back and never shown what it was.
*Do:* give Lesca one thing her Coralie stopped doing in 2015 that Maylis's Coralie still does. The voice into the oven is the obvious one. Then the laugh over the soup in ch 5 is a woman hearing it for the first time in eleven years, and the broken "The—" in ch 16 hits in both houses.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie at the oven door, three times.** Ch 1, ch 8, and the one in ch 16 that stops. It is the marriage, the comedy and the cost in one gesture.
2. **Larrère's window and Larrère's eleven minutes.** "The window went down. It went all the way down, into the door." And in the other village: "'Eleven years I've been timed like an egg.'" The same man made twice by one prop each. Also his last "I'll be brief" at the garden gate, followed by nothing.
3. **Coralie's two refusals.** The landing in ch 7 ("she's in this wrist hearing every word, and she hasn't asked me into it") and the bedroom in ch 10 ("I wanted you in the house, and I thought I could have you without it, if I kept a landing between us"). Do not shorten, explain or soften either.
4. **The dip.** "She wanted it not to be her." The book refuses to call the choice brave, and then lets a neighbour say so from her doorstep: "fetching a man out of a dry hole." Maylis measures the distance and does not correct her. Leave every word. With it: Kévin's "I wanted six, Maylis."
5. **Jeannot, entire.** The six points down the phone, the hand kept up until the car has left the square, letter 42 ("It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you"), and the last line, where "little Lesca" becomes "Lesca" and he picks up her father's shovel. With him, the other Etcheto's "Nobody emptied hers."
