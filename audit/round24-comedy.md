## 2. Against the comparisons

**Crouch: above.** He does not attempt comedy. Dark Matter plays "another me wants my wife" as horror; this book puts it on headed paper — "*That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*" (3) — and it is both funnier and more frightening.

**The Good Place: below. A 7 against its 8.**

- **One way of being wrong, in a dozen mouths.** The series gives four leads four different faults. Here Larrère, Jeannot, Lesca, Sarthou, Marie-Jo, Madame Ducasse, the woman at Hontaube and the young man from Pau are all funny the same way: they talk like paperwork. Only Coralie, Josiane and Madame Etcheto are funny by another route.
- **The premise's own comedy runs two days.** The series reruns its premise until it is wrung out. This book lets one woman loose in the other's village once each way (5, 6) and then spends five chapters keeping them apart.
- **Level on the central problem.** The series made the trolley problem funny by staging it. This book does it once, with one word: "'Either,' Maylis said. 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said." (11) The laugh and the catastrophe are the same syllable. That moment is as good as the comparison; there is one of it.
- **Level on callbacks.** "'I said that house. I only had the one name wrong.'" (16) re-reads the adultery farce as true. It lands as confirmation, not reversal, because the two bowls and the folder under the bread gave it away in 4.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The best comic engine is switched off at chapter 6.** Lesca loose in a village that takes her for Maylis is the funniest thing in the book ("'It takes a small m,'" 5). She gets one day. At bingo she is gagged by design: "Maylis had her own teeth shut, and she meant to keep them shut until the hall was empty." (7) In 8 she announces "'This time I've come to stay'" and is thrown out thirty lines later, at breakfast. From there to the flood, Maylis drives alone, and when Maylis drives alone the jokes belong to other people and she is their audience ("Maylis laughed" is her commonest comic action). *Do:* let the Sunday in 8 run. Lesca stays through the morning with Coralie's weight on the left wrist, and goes out into the square once more before Inguma takes her. The cost is the two solitary scenes that open 7 (two nights of pushing, the gauge), which can go to a paragraph.

**2. One comic register.** "'So there's the question of the winch. It isn't settled, and you're not to worry about it.'" (1) "'One. A duck comes down at night onto standing water, and onto nothing else. Two.'" (1) "'There are four points, and I'll say them once. One.'" (4) "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (6) "'Is this a request, or a notification?'" (11) Each is good. Together they are one joke: officialese. Jeannot and Lesca even share the device of numbered points, three chapters apart. *Do:* numbers belong to Jeannot only; Lesca speaks in items and motions, never "One. Two." Give Kévin and Arlette a comic fault that is not procedural and is shown, not announced — Kévin's is currently told to us: "The Kévin she knew asked three questions in every sentence." (6) Cut that sentence; he already did it in 2.

**3. The theme is said in plain words after the joke has already said it.** Narration, 11: "At three, if it came, it would be the river's doing." Narration, 13: "If she went on, whatever the river did behind her was the river's." Maylis to Kévin, 16: "'I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me.'" Then Coralie says it once, funny and unbearable: "'That was Either, with the river in it.'" (16) The series never glosses its own punchline. *Do:* keep 13 (it is the choice) and Coralie. Cut the gloss in 11 and the confession to Kévin, who answers it with "'I flew,'" as if he had not heard.

**4. The meeting in the box stops being a meeting.** Chapter 4 has a perfect conceit — a woman chairing herself — and it produces "'There's nobody in the chair,'" and then sixty lines of rules and river: "'Your river gauge reads what my file reads, day for day. On Friday the eleventh, at night, your river will be ninety over that line.'" The same facts are given again in 5: "'This gate is a door in that bank. Raise it, and the river runs out over the meadows.'" *Do:* the box keeps the four rules, each contested as a point of order by a woman who knows forms. The river and the gate are told once, on the platform in 5, where there is a gate to look at.

**5. The truth room is wasted on one woman.** "'Standing in this room, a woman can only say what's true.'" (4) It stops Lesca three times. It never stops Maylis, and thirty lines before the rule is found she says "'Either'" in that room and it comes out whole. A room where only true things can be said, and a woman whose one word is an evasion: the book's best available joke is not made. *Do:* in 4 the word sticks in her throat too, and that is how rule five is discovered. Then "'Both,'" in 8 comes out whole, and the reader knows she means it.

**6. The laugh in the minute's silence is over in four lines.** "The laugh started low down. Her own rib would have stopped it, but this body had nothing to stop it with. The right hand came up and shut over her mouth. The laugh came out through the mayor's nose." (6) Five chapters of a cracked rib stopping every laugh were the setup for this. A kitchen timer set to one minute is ticking. It should be the longest minute in the book and it is the shortest. *Do:* let the minute run its sixty seconds — she tries to think him dead, gets letter seventeen, then the next letter, the hand, the nose, and only then Maïté.

**7. Lesca's village has no comedy after chapter 6, and the rehearsal that could carry it is cut at twelve lines.** "'Can he be asked not to?' 'You can ask him.'" (10) Then "He stopped counting," and the scene is over. Meanwhile 14 has to set up its own payoff in the paragraph that pays it: "Every year, after the wreath, the mayor kissed the widow on the cheek, and every year he took that picture." *Do:* the young man from Pau walks Lesca through the whole ceremony in 10 — the medal for the camera, the kiss for the photographer, where to stand — before Coralie's forty-two stops him. Then 14 needs no explaining and "'I've got nothing on the sheet for this'" pays twice.

**8. Jokes told again by the narrator.** "The hall was watching the keyholder, who had said two words in a deep voice to the floor in front of the bar and bitten off the third." (7) We watched it. "In eighteen years beside that car Maylis had never seen it do that." (5), then Lafitte ("'Right down.'"), then Lesca over the soup: three tellings of one window. Arlette's "every day he said Either, and I never got used to it" (8) turns the word into an inheritance and lets Maylis off. And the word leaks: "'It's adultery either way'" (5), "'He wouldn't for me either'" (11). *Do:* cut the first, the second and Arlette's; reword the strays so the word appears only when it is the joke.

**9. In the two-in-one-body gags the reader has to work out whose intention failed.** "Maylis felt the mouth get ready for the word, and stop. It tried again. 'Ei—' Lesca said." (5) "She had nothing to put in the mouth, and Maylis took it from her." (5) The café scene is in Lesca's head, the soup in Maylis's, the kitchen in both, and "she" floats. A physical gag dies if it needs a second reading. *Do:* one head per scene in 5, and in each tussle name who wants what before the body moves.

**10. Letter banter with the water rising.** "'I put that handle in number six.' 'I wrote to them too.' 'I know you did. They took note.'" (13) A woman is behind a door nobody can open, and two people stop for a callback. *Do:* cut the three lines. Letter 42 in 16 does this work, at the right time.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. Coralie at the oven door, three times: done in 1, done again in 8, and broken off at "'The—'" in 16.
2. Madame Etcheto's whole line: two lots of stars and "Staff not to join in" (2), "'That's my door'" over the duck (7), and the same three words in 15.
3. Larrère with no bell — "'Eleven years I've been timed like an egg… I'm told I wave'" (6) — and "'I'll be brief,'" followed by nothing, at the garden gate (15).
4. The hall of the dead as bingo night: the duck's feet, the man with his arm round his card, "'You can put that in the minutes as apologies received.'" (12)
5. The platform at dusk: "'Either.'" "'Then it's shut.'" (11), and Jeannot's six points down the phone, ending "'And six. Bugger off.'" (9)
