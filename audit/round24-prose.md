# Critic report: Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. About 43,400 words in 85 scenes.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.5.** *"That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it."* (3). The public stake and the private one are fused in one sentence at the end of the third chapter.
- **Tension: 7.** *"She took a step back towards it. She took a second, and a third."* (13). Chapters 11 to 13 hold. Chapters 6 to 8 do not, because the reader cannot yet say what causes the flood.
- **Progression: 6.5.** *"On Sunday night Maylis could have pushed, and she lay still, because keeping Lesca out meant having a better plan, and Maylis didn't have one."* (4). A turn of the plot is handed over in a subordinate clause.
- **Comedy: 7.5.** *"Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station."* (6).
- **Characters: 7.5.** *"Because you'd have told me."* (10). Coralie is the best-written person in the book. Maïté, Arlette and Marie-Jo speak in her cadence.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** *"'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'"* (16). A joke set up in chapter 1 fails on purpose in the last chapter, and the failure carries the grief.
- **Prose: 7.** *"Now neither of them could breathe, because they had one nose between them."* (8). Against it: `, and` joins clauses 930 times, one every 47 words.
- **The ending: 8.** *"Then he stepped over the string in his waders and began to dig."* (16).
- **Clarity: 5.5.** *"'One day of my file for every night I sit in that chair with you.' Lesca could say that. 'So that's what it fetches me home for.'"* (8). This is the sentence that tells the reader why the river rises, and it is a riddle.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a patient one. Not on the reader the bar describes: that reader stops in chapter 4, at the four numbered points, or in chapter 8, at the gauge readings.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Against Crouch: above in the making of a sentence, below in ease. Net, level on prose and below on clarity.**

- Crouch has no device as good as the driven body. *"Lesca told the mouth to open. It was stiff, and it was hers to use."* (3). The definite article does the possession. He would need italics and a paragraph of panic for the same effect.
- Crouch never makes a reader go back to find out what happened. This book does it about a dozen times, and always where a rule is being given (faults 1 and 7). In Dark Matter the box is explained once, in plain speech, by a man who wants to be understood. Here the equivalent explanation is an agenda item, a set of gauge readings and a half-line about a lake.
- Crouch changes pace inside his own voice: short paragraphs, then long. This book has one pace until chapter 13, where it gets speed by dropping verbs (*"Torch up. Right arm across her side."*), and that reads as a second hand.

**Against The Good Place: level in how a joke is built, below in how many voices tell them.**

- The callbacks are built as that series builds them. The oven door, the kitchen timer, Monsieur Daguerre's "Here" and the window that goes down *"all the way down, into the door"* each return changed, and the last return is the one that hurts.
- In the series nobody could be given another's line. Here the numbered points belong to Jeannot and to Lesca. *"The village's girl"* comes from Madame Etcheto, Maïté and Larrère. Four women end their speeches on the same short flat sentence.

**Pattern audit.** The vocabulary is clean: no stock intensifiers, no semicolons, dashes only for speech cut off. The tells are structural: one sentence shape, the "never in N years" certificate, the motive stated after the act, and the contrast in two beats (*"It didn't ring. It buzzed twice against his chest."*, *"It wasn't loud. It sounded like a truck tipping out a load of gravel"*, both 13).

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. The rules are the hardest sentences in the book (structural)

Chapter 8: *"On the Friday morning your file said seventy under, and my river gauge read a metre. It hadn't moved since Wednesday, and it hadn't rained. On Friday night the rib gave, and you came in. By Saturday noon it was raining, and that afternoon you read fifty-five off the gauge yourself."* Then: *"So that's what it fetches me home for."*

The whole ending rests on one fact: the flood comes only because Lesca crosses, so Maylis could stop it by keeping her out. The reader is given it as four gauge readings to subtract, capped by a line that does not parse. The same compression hides the other rules:

- Chapter 5: *"'Six until nine,' Lesca said to the right hand. 'Fifteen hours for a lake.'"* This refers to a plan made two chapters earlier and never shown carried out.
- Chapter 2: *"it had been told twice and given two lots of stars, and it got through them early and came at one."* Two lots should take longer. The joke's sum does not work on a first reading.
- Chapter 15: *"'What did you have last night?' 'Nothing. I didn't want to sit down.'"*
- Chapter 16: *"'That one's yours,' Maylis said. 'At three I never tried it.'"*

**Do:** In chapter 8 give Maylis one plain sentence in the room where only true things can be said, and let it come out whole: the river rises on the nights Lesca sits in the chair, and stops on the nights she is kept out. Cut *"So that's what it fetches me home for."* In chapter 5 have Lesca name what she gave Inguma to count. Fix the other three by adding the missing half-sentence. This alone moves clarity from 5.5 to 7.

### 2. The narrator gives the motive at the act, and the character gives it again in chapter 16 (structural)

Chapter 13: *"Lesca had rung once and turned the handle, and Maylis thought she could do better than that. She wanted the man and the gate, both, and she wanted to be the one who got them."*

Chapter 16: *"At the winch I thought I could do better than you. The man and the gate, both. I drove past the handle to be the one who did."*

These are the same words twice. The second motive gets the same treatment three times:

- Chapter 11: *"At three, if it came, it would be the river's doing."*
- Chapter 13: *"She wanted it not to be her."*
- Chapter 16, to Kévin: *"I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me."*

The last chapter confesses what the reader was told at the time. It could have been a reversal that re-reads chapter 13, which is the thing the bar names in The Good Place.

**Do:**

- In chapter 13, cut the two quoted sentences. Keep *"for three breaths she kept her foot off the pedal"* and *"She drove past."*
- In chapter 16, cut the line to Kévin. The steel room then becomes the first place anyone says why.
- In chapter 12, cut *"Lesca was fighting to keep this body for good, and Maylis was fighting to do her own night in it."* The scene shows it.
- In chapter 12, cut *"She had come to take this life from a woman who never said what she wanted, and that woman had just said it."* The line before it, *"It didn't come"*, is enough.
- In chapter 13, cut *"He was telling her that from the man's own house, at three in the morning, on the man's phone."* The screen and the clock have already said it.

### 3. One sentence shape and one pace

Chapter 7: *"Lesca waded through it, with the water over the ankles of the boots, and looked at the watch on the far side."*

This is the template: a clause, a `with` phrase, `, and`, a clause. The counts are 930 for `, and` and 455 for `with`. A kitchen and a breaking bank move at the same speed. Thirty-nine of the 85 scenes open on the clock or the calendar (*"At eight"*, *"At noon"*, *"At seven"* and *"At five past nine"* open four of the six scenes in chapter 5). Chapters 4 and 5 open on the same phrase: *"with rain on the kitchen window."*

**Do:** Leave the kitchens alone. Break the pairs into single sentences in the four passages that must run: the gate in chapter 5, the bank in chapter 11, the van in chapter 12 and the track in chapter 13. Then cut the verbless fragments after 03:11 in chapter 13 (*"Up to her thighs." "Colder than the rain." "Torch up."*), which will no longer be needed. Open a third of the scenes on something other than the hour.

### 4. The "never in N years" certificate

- *"In eighteen years nobody in that square had waited to hear what she had to say."* (2)
- *"Nobody at that table had ever asked her a question about it."* (3)
- *"At that table she had never said anything except that she abstained"* (3)
- *"In eighteen years beside that car Maylis had never seen it do that."* (5)
- *"In sixteen years Maylis had never got that laugh out of her."* (5)
- *"Maylis had never kept anybody waiting in her life"* (6)
- *"Nobody at that table had heard him finish a sentence since 2015"* (6)
- *"Sixteen years, and that's the first time I've seen that hand hold on to anything"* (8)
- *"I've never heard you say anything twice."* (8)
- *"Until tonight it had never been off her belt."* (13)

Each one certifies its moment as a record. Ten of them mean that every event in the book is the first of its kind, and the reader stops believing the count.

**Do:** Keep three: the window (5), the laugh (5) and the hand (8). Cut the rest. In each case the event stands without the tag.

### 5. Maylis's silence is explained every time it happens

Chapter 1, three times in one chapter:

- *"She wanted Coralie to stay awake and keep her fingers on that wrist, but Coralie had to be up before six, and Maylis didn't ask."*
- *"Maylis wanted the duck. But Coralie would be the one cooking it, after a day's work, and if Maylis ticked that box it would be Maylis's doing."*
- *"Maylis did worry about it, and she had written to say so. But the man behind the glass paid her wages, and she didn't knock on it."*

The formula is want, reason, did not. It returns in chapters 2, 4, 6 and 8. "She wanted" and its variants appear 24 times. After the shopping pad the reader knows the rule, and from then on the narrator is saying what the scene has shown.

**Do:** Keep the duck. Cut the reason from the other two in chapter 1, leaving the want and the silence. From chapter 2 on, cut the want as well wherever the next line of dialogue is *"Either"* or nothing.

### 6. Shared verbal machinery

Chapter 4, Lesca: *"'One. When Inguma sits on two people at the same time...' ... 'Two. When one of us wakes...' ... 'Three. Inguma fetches.'"*

Chapter 1, Jeannot: *"'One. A duck comes down at night onto standing water, and onto nothing else. Two. A hand of water does it.'"*

Two people own one tic, and it is the one Jeannot is known by to his last page. Speeches also end alike across four women:

- Arlette: *"I'd have liked to be there."*
- Maïté: *"Don't do it in the square."*
- Coralie: *"I'd have this one again."*
- Marie-Jo: *"Somebody has to stack them."*

Each is a good line. Together they are one voice.

**Do:** Take the numerals from Lesca. She already has *"The first item"*, *"Next item"* and *"Last item"*, which are hers and a mayor's. Let Arlette end one speech in the middle of a thought. Give *"the village's girl"* to Madame Etcheto alone.

### 7. Numbers that collide

- **Nineteen:** days of rain, turns of the winch, bags of grit.
- **Twelve:** years, a room, a date, a bingo call, minibus seats, the dead man's letters.
- **Nine:** minutes to three, rings, petition names, strings of lights.
- **Forty to forty-two:** forty houses; forty letters (3, 5) and forty-one letters (1, 16); forty-one survivors; forty-two beds; letter No. 42.

Some of these are the book's best strokes: one ring against nine, forty-two beds against *"Forty-one"*, room twelve against *"Twelve. One, two."* The reader cannot tell those from the accidents, so the designed ones lose force. Forty against forty-one letters is defensible (Lesca's knowledge dates from before the forty-first was signed for), but it reads as a slip.

**Do:** Keep the numbers that pay. Change the incidental ones: the bags, the minibus seats, the lights, the petition. Add a clause in chapter 3 that makes the forty deliberate.

### 8. Repeats that are not refrains

- *"The voice came out lower than Maylis's own."* (3 and 5, word for word)
- *"with the sound of a sack being lowered down a step"* (5 and 7, word for word)
- *"the way it goes out of a bench"* (3) and *"the way warmth goes out of a chair"* (13)
- *"with the coffee pot in her hand"* (4 and 5)
- Chapter 2 ends *"Maylis hadn't said it."* Chapter 4 ends *"She hadn't told them to."* Two chapter endings in three use the same construction.
- The dead Castagnet is described twice in the same words (*"in two jackets ... a card in each hand"*, 8 and 12).
- The sack appears nine times as the measure of Inguma.

The designed refrains work: the prologue returning in chapter 13, the two fingers on the wrist, nine minutes to three. These others are the same sentence used twice with nothing gained.

**Do:** Keep the first of each pair and rewrite the second. Give chapter 4 a different last line.

### 9. Usage and consistency

- *"tea towel"* (1, 10) and *"dish towel"* (5, four times).
- *"truck"* eight times in a text that otherwise says torch, bonnet, windscreen, pavement, pram and tip.
- The dug-out land is *"common pasture"*, *"the common"*, *"common land"* and *"grazing land"*. What replaced it is *"the lake"*, *"the gravel lake"*, *"a gravel pit"* and *"the hole"*. A reader takes these for different places.
- The dealer is *"the man dealing the cards"* and *"the dealer"* for fifteen chapters and *"Fernand"* in chapter 16.
- *"The mayor"* in narration is Lesca through chapter 15. In chapter 16 it is Larrère: *"He let the mayor come to within a metre of him."*
- The driven body takes *the*. Chapter 6 slips: *"shut her eyes"*, *"Maylis left her finger where it was"*, *"Maylis took her hand down."*
- *"Embankment"* three times, *"bank"* everywhere else.

**Do:** Pick one noun for each thing and hold it. Name the dealer when he first speaks, or never.

### 10. Stock verbs and two filler words

Chapter 14: *"She kept her face the way she had kept it at all of these ceremonies."*

The counts: *went* 178, *put* 113, *took* 110, *turned* 107, *stood* 93, *looked* 86, *kept* 65, *still* 65, *stopped* 64, *own* 58, *the way* 36. The plain verbs are the style. *Kept* and *own* are habits: *"her own bed"*, *"her own stairs"*, *"in Maylis's own voice"*, *"his own lamp"*.

**Do:** Cut *own* wherever nobody else's is in question. Halve *kept*.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The grammar of the driven body.** *"Lesca kept the eyes on it for longer than a cut needed."* The article carries the premise. The right hand that stays Maylis's carries the comedy and the climax.
2. **The oven door.** It appears three times, and the third time Coralie cannot finish it. Do not add a word to *"The—"*.
3. **Monsieur Daguerre.** *"'Here,' he said. 'I know you are.'"* (13), and the man who asks whether it is Thursday: *"Then they'll want the bins out."*
4. **Kévin's hand.** *"I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much."* (16).
5. **Coralie on the phone log.** *"Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one."* (10), down to *"Because you'd have told me."* This is the best page in the book.
