# Round 24 — Thriller

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full, and the brief. Nothing else.

Overall for this lens: **7**. Good, and one mark under the bar. Chapters 00 to 05 and 11 to 13 are level with Crouch. Chapters 6 to 10 and 15 are not.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "*Watch stopped at 03:11.*" (ch 3). A date, a named man and forty houses are in play by the end of the third chapter. It stays under 8 because the heroine's own losses are partly spent before the night: the key goes to Hontaube in January whatever she does.
- **Tension: 6.5.** "She had worked the verges out at the speed of the village tractor. Inguma was doing them on foot." (ch 6). It is funny, and it is the line where the thing from the wardrobe stops being frightening. Chapters 11 to 13 are a 9. Chapters 6 to 10 are a 5.
- **Progression: 7.** "'Either,' Maylis said. / 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said." (ch 11). Every exit is shut by somebody with a reason, and the gauge readings count down cleanly. The middle repeats its moves.
- **Comedy: 7.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (ch 6).
- **Characters: 7.5.** "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'" (ch 7).
- **The switch between dark and comic: 7.5.** "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'" (ch 13). The bingo joke comes back in a flooded corridor and is no longer a joke. In chapters 5 to 8 the switch runs the other way and the comic disarms the dark.
- **Prose: 7.5.** "It wasn't loud. It sounded like a truck tipping out a load of gravel, a long way off, and the sound didn't stop." (ch 13).
- **The ending: 7.5.** "'Had the key and went for a walk, did you?'" (ch 16). The cost is real and nobody is let off. Chapter 15 takes half a mark.
- **Clarity: 6.** "'One day of my file for every night I sit in that chair with you.' Lesca could say that. 'So that's what it fetches me home for.'" (ch 8). This is the rule the flood depends on. It is stated once, and the sentence after it cannot be parsed.

**Finished unpaid:** yes, but not in one sitting. I would have put it down in chapter 6 and picked it up the next day.

**Press it on a friend:** yes on the friend who loves The Good Place. No on the friend who reads Crouch for the pace.

## 2. Against the comparisons

Below, by one mark.

**Level with Crouch**

- Speed of premise. Dark Matter has its man in the wrong world by the third chapter. Here the prologue puts the gate and the phone on page one, "'Come back across,' her mouth said. Maylis hadn't said it." ends chapter 2, and the first line of chapter 3 opens the second village.
- Fear for the marriage. Crouch puts the other Jason in the house with the wife. This book puts the other woman inside the body and lets her be better company: "In sixteen years Maylis had never got that laugh out of her. Lesca had got it in ten minutes, with Maylis's mouth." (ch 5). The stairs that follow are the most frightening page in the first half.
- The night itself. Chapter 13 runs on clock stamps and distances the reader has already walked, and every number in it was planted.

**Below Crouch**

- Sustained threat. In Dark Matter the threat multiplies after the midpoint. Here it is managed down. By chapter 5 the creature takes instructions from anyone who knows the words. By chapter 7 Coralie can tell the two women apart by a wrist and sends the wrong one downstairs. Nothing supernatural frightens again until the van roof in chapter 11.
- The box. A Crouch reader names Dark Matter by chapter 4: a sealed room built to put a person in two states, a second self who took the other road, and that self coming for the bed and the wife. What is this book's own is the single body with the right hand held back, and the flood that travels with the visitor. That is enough to stand apart. But the steel room is an origin and nothing more. The reader who came for the box gets folklore.

**Below The Good Place**

- The staging of the reversal. The Good Place stops the scene for its reversal, and everything after it is a different show. This book's equal turn, that the woman who came to warn the village is carrying the flood, is leaked in narration in chapter 7 and recited in chapter 8, and nobody's plan changes.

**Above both**

- The cost of the choice. Dark Matter lets the family drive away. Recursion resets. This book runs one choice twice, gets a body each time, and shows the second one's wallpaper.

**Where I was afraid, and of what**

- Ch 1 and 2: the thing itself, and the mouth.
- Ch 3: the file. "That village hadn't been spared its flood. It hadn't had it yet."
- Ch 5: the stairs. A woman about to be taken to bed in her own body.
- Ch 8: the card with the name on it.
- Ch 11 to 13: Coralie under the bank, and then the fire door nobody comes through.

**Where a Crouch reader puts it down**

- Ch 1, between the wardrobe and the eggs.
- Ch 6, at the council table.
- Ch 7, at the bingo.
- Ch 15.

**Does the choice land?** Yes. "She wanted it not to be her." is the right motive, it is ignoble, and it was planted on the first page with a shopping pad.

**Does the ending cost enough?** Yes for the death. Not quite for the job.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. Ch 7 to 12. The heroine has no move against the cause of the flood, and the one contest she is promised is skipped.**

"She had pushed on Sunday night and again on Monday, and the rib had let her have one breath each time." (ch 8). Then, at the end of chapter 11: "It was an hour and nine minutes short of four." And the next chapter opens: "Lesca was in the chair in the steel room, and Maylis was in it with her."

Once the book says the mayor carries the river, the only defence against her coming is a cracked rib, and the rib fails in summary. Friday night is set up with a clock, hold out until four, and that clock is spent in the gap between two chapters. So for four chapters the reader waits for something that cannot be stopped. A thriller shows the exit and then shuts it.

What I would do: the exit is already in the book. On her side with her knees up, the thing has nowhere to sit (Madame Etcheto in ch 2, Arlette in ch 10), and Coralie's chart has a column headed *turned*. Somebody could keep her turned until four. Coralie is taken off the board in one line, "I'm on nights from Friday", and Maylis never sees what that shift costs her. Let her see it. Let her fail to ask for it, which is her fault from page one: "Maylis didn't ask." Then play the hour and nine minutes in the van, on the page.

**2. Ch 7 and 8. The best turn in the book is told twice and changes nothing.**

"Since then the river had made up everything it had missed, and it was back on the file, to the centimetre." (ch 7). Then in chapter 8 Maylis recites the same evidence in one paragraph, and the turn itself gets one line: "'You got my rib,' Maylis said. 'And I get your river.'"

The reader has the answer a chapter before the scene that delivers it. Twelve lines after it, the scene has moved on to another argument and another set. Lesca still comes. Maylis had the answer on Saturday and wrote her a handover note on Tuesday.

What I would do: one scene, where the evidence is. Lafitte's pad has a cross beside "seventy under". Let her do the sum there, in front of Jeannot, on the day the rain comes back, and cut the narrated version at the gauge. Then chapter 8's meeting can start from "Don't come on Friday" and have room for the refusal to weigh what it weighs.

**3. Ch 12. The two women fight for eight rings over something they both want.**

"'I'll drive,' Lesca said. 'I left a man at the end of that track. I'm bringing this one up the ladder myself.'" In chapter 8 the same woman said: "'There's no both.'" and "'You turn the handle first.'" She changed her plan off the page. Maylis then drives down the same track herself.

So the fight is over who holds the wheel, not over what happens. The reader cannot want an outcome. Two smaller faults sit inside it. "Maylis didn't try to pull her off." has the heroine announce she will block the chair and then stand aside. And "she could hold it down for this one" tells the reader the antagonist could have won and chose not to.

What I would do: keep Lesca on her chapter 8 line. In the van she goes for the winch, because her sum always comes out for her, and Maylis's right hand fights for the track. Then "Go to the blind first" is Lesca reversing herself as she leaves, which is what those five words want to be. If she must want the track from the start, show her change her mind in chapter 10, where she has a whole chapter and never mentions it.

**4. Ch 8, 11, 12. Three sentences the plot hangs on cannot be read at speed.**

"'So that's what it fetches me home for.'" (ch 8). "If it didn't get her into that chair before four, nobody crossed tonight, and the bank held." (ch 11), written while the river is already ninety over and coming through the patch. "*That the mayor remain seated.* In the steel room the mayor let go of the chair." (ch 12), where the motion says stay and the action is go.

A reader cannot be afraid of a rule they cannot work out. Why the bank breaks on Friday is only made plain afterwards: "Eight rings had been long enough for her night to come through into the other village".

What I would do: one plain statement at dusk on Friday, at the gauge. The river is here because of Thursday night. The break has not come. The break is tonight's, and it comes only if she does. Cut the "fetches me home for" sentence. At the eighth ring, say in plain words what Lesca does and why.

**5. Ch 15. A chapter of tidying, second from last, that repeats what the reader has and runs past the end of the book.**

"'You stood in my kitchen that night and rang her from my phone, with his van gone down to the meadows.'" The reader had this in chapter 13: "under the time it said Castagnet, house." It is staged again with fireworks.

The question of which she turned the handle for is answered "I don't know" three times: "all she found was a handle, turning" (ch 10), "'I can't find which part of it turned my hand'" (ch 15), "'In here it would have to be true. I don't know.'" (ch 16). The council vote is a scene here and a line in the next chapter: "'They take it off me on Tuesday.'" And the chapter runs to Tuesday night, then chapter 16 goes back to Sunday.

What I would do: keep Madame Etcheto at the minibus and Maïté's verb. Run them as the tail of chapter 14, after the chairs. Give Lesca the clock on the kitchen wall in one paragraph and drop the garden gate. Cut the vote. Ask "which was it" once, in the room where the answer has to be true.

**6. Ch 2 to 12. The creature is given eleven things to count, and becomes a service.**

Stars, leaves, grass, gravel loads, roof tiles, verges, signatures, maize, ponies, grit bags, fingers. "'That's a small roof,' Lesca said. 'Next time I'll bring it more.'" (ch 5).

By the fifth job the fetch can be booked like a taxi. The one time its lateness bites, the cost happens off the page: "By now Coralie had been awake for hours, beside a body with its eyes open, reading a pad that said two." (ch 6).

What I would do: keep the stars, the gravel loads, the verges, the ponies and the fingers. Cut the tiles, the signatures and the grit bags. Each job that stays should cost somebody something on the page.

**7. Ch 6 and 7. The council and the bingo run at their comic length, not their thriller length.**

"By then he had been on his feet for eleven minutes." (ch 6). The council scene is about ninety-five lines and carries three plot beats: the line in the statutes, the question cut off by the bell, and the vote that puts the mayor a metre from the stone. The bingo is about a hundred and ten lines and carries three: Jeannot's promise to be in his blind, Madame Etcheto finishing the words, and "That's my door." These two scenes sit back to back, and between them the count to Friday goes quiet.

What I would do: cut each to its three beats and the two best jokes. In the council that is the sentences with no ends on them and the back lane. In the bingo it is the grass trimmer and "Jean's for the stone."

**8. Ch 6, 8, 11, 16. The job she is blackmailed with is already gone.**

"as she would have for the men from Hontaube, who took over the key in January." (ch 8). The resignation letter threatens three weeks of work. The book concedes it: "it was the smallest thing that had happened to her since Friday." (ch 16). The threat that works is the other sheet, with Kévin's name on it.

What I would do: delete the lines that hand the key to Hontaube in January. Keep "Hontaube opens that file and decides who it keeps", so that a clean file is a job and a dated letter is the end of one.

**9. Ch 1. The square asks for attention it has not yet earned.**

"'One. A duck comes down at night onto standing water, and onto nothing else. Two. A hand of water does it.'" Between the thing on the wardrobe and its second visit, the chapter introduces a registered letter, an association, a sluice gate, a winch, a key, a blind and how ducks land. All of it pays later. None of it has a reason yet, and the reader came for the wardrobe.

What I would do: keep the postman and the car window, which are the fastest character work in the book. Cut Jeannot down to the letters and "Put it in forty-two". Move the hand of water to chapter 5, where Lesca explains what the gate does.

**10. Ch 5, 7, 8, 12. The fight for the mouth and the hands is the same turn four times.**

"The coffee came up to the brim and over it. It ran along the bar and off the end, onto Josiane's shoes." (ch 7). Before it comes the soup spoon (ch 5), after it the kitchen table (ch 8). By the van, where it has to frighten, the reader knows it as a comic number.

What I would do: cut the spoon and the urn. Keep the stairs, the kitchen table, because Coralie picks a side there, and the van.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The prologue**, whole, to "It rang once."
2. **Ch 3, the file.** The three gauge lines, "*Watch stopped at 03:11.*", "It hadn't had it yet.", and the motion that ends the chapter.
3. **Ch 5, the stairs**, from the hand on the post to "'Are you coming up?'"
4. **Ch 10, Coralie and the call log.** "'Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one.'" It rereads eleven years of a landing in one speech.
5. **Ch 13, from the dip to "'Forty-one.'"** The clock stamps, "It had held for sixteen.", Kévin counting five fingers in his own headlights, the pencil line through the last name.
