# Critic report — Characters

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes — 7.5.** The forty houses stop being a number in chapter 10: "'There were forty-two in their beds that night. The others have died since then, in those beds, of the things people die of.'" Before that chapter the Port is a figure Lesca recites.
- **Tension — 7.** Chapters 11 to 13 are as tight as anything in the comparisons: "The winch was six hundred metres behind her, and the red reflector was four hundred metres in front. The bank had six minutes left." Chapters 1, 2 and the first half of 7 run on a river gauge and a rib.
- **Progression — 7.5.** Each chapter moves a rule or a position, and one line re-reads the first half: "'Forget the note. The river only rises when you come.'"
- **Comedy — 7.5.** It comes out of who the people are: "'Opened what? There's a name over it. It reads that she opened him.'"
- **Characters — 7.5.** The best of it is a person refusing to be a prize: "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'" The two leads are narrower than the people around them.
- **The switch between dark and comic — 8.** The oven-door routine, done twice for laughs, dies in her mouth the third time: "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'"
- **Prose — 7.** Plain and easy to follow, but it runs on a small kit of stock phrases. "with the sound of a sack being lowered down a step" appears word for word in chapters 5 and 7, and the sack appears nine times in all.
- **The ending — 8.** It costs her the job, the key, the woman and a death, and her first straight answer is not the one she was asked for: "'She's being buried here on Tuesday. I want to dig the grave myself.'"
- **Clarity — 6.5.** In the shared-body chapters I lost whose head I was in: "She had known for a week that he would be." That "she" is Lesca, four lines after a paragraph in Maylis's head.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes, without effort from chapter 3.
**Would I press it on a friend?** Yes, on the friend who loved The Good Place. Not on the one who reads Crouch for speed; chapter 1 would lose that reader.

## 2. Against the comparisons (Characters)

**Above Crouch. Below The Good Place on the two leads, level with it on the village.**

- **Above Crouch: the partner is a person.** Daniela in Dark Matter is what Jason wants back. Coralie has a want that has nothing to do with either woman ("I posted it today, and I posted it for me"), a professional ethic that decides the landing scene, and a twelve-year secret of her own in the other village. Crouch never wrote a spouse who could say "'Because you'd have told me.'"
- **Level with The Good Place: each villager is wrong in their own way, and the wrongness drives the plot.** Jeannot votes shut because he was told how to vote. Larrère will not put a river on maize "for a flood that hasn't come". Josiane has the adultery right and the name wrong. Madame Etcheto will not sleep in a chair. Coralie will not wake her for orange. Nobody dies of bad luck.
- **Below The Good Place: the leads have one note each until late.** Eleanor is selfish in twenty ways and funny in all of them. Maylis has a word and Lesca has an agenda. Six other characters name those flaws for the reader, and a memory-transfer device spares the two women from ever having to find each other out. Their relationship, the one the premise sells, is the thinnest of the four that matter.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The chair does the two women's talking for them. (Structural; ch. 4, 6, 7, 9)**
"'In this chair, what one of us knows, the other gets.'" From then on neither has to ask the other anything. "Maylis had been given the rest in the chair" (6). "It had come to her in the chair, out of Lesca's file" (7). In chapter 4 Maylis says "'I don't know anything about eleven years'" and Lesca answers "'That's for another night.'" That night never comes. Worst, the one exchange with teeth — "'When it was you, did you know he was out there?' 'No one came to tell him.' 'That's not a no.'" (8) — is thrown away a chapter later, when "the night of the flood came to Maylis anyway" with Lesca not even in the room. The central secret is handed over free. The two women meet four times, each time as a meeting with items, and they never discover one another.
*What I would do:* limit the chair to the body: the rib, the smell, the scar, one image. Facts have to be asked for, in the room where a lie will not come out. Make chapter 9 a scene: Maylis asks again, Lesca cannot say "no", and has to tell the night herself or stand there unable to. Her saying it at the stone in chapter 14 then becomes the second time, in front of people, which is harder, not weaker.

**2. Each woman's flaw is a label, and the village reads it out. (ch. 1, 4, 6, 8, 10, 14)**
Maylis's is announced in the first kitchen scene — "'Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you'" — and then repeated by Lesca ("'you've spent it writing Either on a shopping pad'"), Jeannot ("'You're told.'"), Sarthou, Arlette ("'You never say what you want. Your father was the same'") and the narrator ("She was the one who said Either"). "Either" is on the page twenty times. Lesca gets the same treatment: "'It's four when you're the one counting'", "'You hear everybody, and then you do the sum by yourself'", "her count came out for her, as her counts always did", "'your counting comes out right for you every time'". A reader told the diagnosis six times stops looking for it in what the women do.
*What I would do:* keep Coralie's in chapter 1, Sarthou's joke, the platform in 11 and "Either, with the river in it" in 16. Cut Arlette's, the narrator's and Lesca's. For Lesca keep Darrigrand's on the bank and cut the other three. The actions already carry both flaws: the unticked box, the text she does not send twice, the motion written on headed paper.

**3. Maylis is built from what she lacks, and her eighteen years of work are not in her eyes. (ch. 1–13)**
The narrator files her wants one by one: "She wanted that, and it surprised her how much." "Maylis wanted him to be right." "She was scared." There are seventeen such sentences. What she alone would notice, a woman who has dug every grave and cut every verge in that village, arrives in the last chapter: "It wasn't much, and she took more than that to the tip in a morning." That sentence is a person. For fifteen chapters before it she is a silence with a rib. The result is that the reader prefers Lesca, exactly as Coralie does, and the book ends on the one the reader prefers less.
*What I would do:* give Maylis her trade as a way of seeing from chapter 1. She should size up the care home corridor, the stone, Maïté's wardrobe and Coralie's bag as the person who will one day have to move, mow or bury them. Replace half the "she wanted" sentences with one thing she notices and nobody else would. Her one real joke, "'Put it in forty-two'", proves she is funny when she is not being watched. She needs two more.

**4. The marriage has a number where its past should be. (ch. 1, 5, 7, 8, 16)**
"Sixteen years" is said seven times. The sixteen years contain two incidents: the stacked chairs in 2010 and the eggs. I know what Coralie has put up with. I do not know what she has had. So when she carries the pillow to the car I think she is right to go, and I grieve for her, not for the house. Dark Matter spends its first chapter making a marriage worth a universe. This one is a problem to be solved by the end of chapter 1.
*What I would do:* swap three of the seven "sixteen years" for three specific things Maylis has done without being asked and without saying why, things only a woman who never says what she wants would do. One scene early where her not-asking is a rest for a woman who is asked for things all day. The book already knows this ("Maylis had sat down and said nothing for sixteen years, in the wrong kitchen") and never shows it in the right kitchen.

**5. The women of the village speak in one voice. (ch. 4, 8, 16)**
Put the lines side by side. Maïté: "'Don't do it in the square.'" Arlette: "'I've never heard you say anything twice. I'd have liked to be there.'" Marie-Jo: "'Sit down. You're grey.'" Madame Darrigrand: "'I'd rather you heard it from me than from the café.'" Coralie: "'I know that word. I've cooked it.'" Every one is the same dry, exact, short reproach that ends on a hard noun. The men are told apart by ear at once: Jeannot numbers his points, Larrère says he will be brief, Sarthou never contracts a verb, Kévin asks three questions. Of the women only Josiane and Madame Etcheto have a sound of their own. That is the nearest the book comes to writing "a village woman" and not a person.
*What I would do:* let Arlette talk too much. Let Maïté be wrong about something out loud. Let Marie-Jo be funny on purpose, as she nearly is at the microphone ("'Tooth.'"). Keep the laconic line for Coralie, who has earned it at a bedside.

**6. In the shared-body chapters the head changes without warning. (ch. 5, also 7 and 8)**
Chapter 5 opens in Maylis ("Maylis felt her take a breath for it"), moves into Lesca at the café ("none of that had got her ready for the back of his neck"), and is back in Maylis at the soup ("In sixteen years Maylis had never got that laugh out of her"). These are the scenes where telling the two apart is the whole point, and they are where I had to re-read.
*What I would do:* one head per section, fixed at the first line. The café is Lesca's. The soup and the stair post are Maylis's. Nothing else needs to change.

**7. "I don't know" is said three times, and the room that cannot lie signs it off. (ch. 10, 15, 16)**
The book builds a room where "a woman can only say what's true" and loads it with one question: the houses, or Coralie. Lesca's answer to Coralie is that both sentences sound "no more true, and no less". To Maïté: "'I can't find which part of it turned my hand.'" To Maylis: "'In here it would have to be true. I don't know.'" The answer is human once. Three times, with the truth-room vouching for it, it reads as a character being kept safe from the worst thing about herself.
*What I would do:* say it once, to Maïté, who has the right to it. In the steel room let her try both sentences aloud and let one of them stop in her mouth, as "Because nobody has looked after that bank" did in chapter 4. The book has shown that gun twice and fires a blank with it at the end.

**8. Lesca's renunciation takes one sentence. (ch. 12)**
She has written a motion to "take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it", said "'I won't be sent home'", and sat on the thing that takes her home. Then: "The hand locked on the brake was the hand that had held the stair post against her, and at the end of the headlights the reflector was red, and no nearer than it had been twelve years ago. 'I'm letting go.'" The turn of her whole arc is two images and a comma. I believed it, and I did not feel what it cost.
*What I would do:* one more ring. She has the rain on the roof and the two pillows a hundred metres of road away, and she knows that while she holds on, the brake stays on and nobody reaches the man. Let her count what she is giving up, as she did on paper in chapter 8 ("Nil"), and let it come out as something other than nil.

**9. Jeannot is let off. (ch. 8, 11, 16)**
His hand decided it: "'Nobody tells me what's under my floorboards and then tells me how to vote. Shut.'" He bolted the hatch and left his phone in the van. Then he ends the book with the shovel, and his letter lists everything except that: the dry cellar, the handle, the kitchen the call came from. "'It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you'" is exactly him. A man that exact would count his own vote.
*What I would do:* one more "It says" in the letter: how he voted on Tuesday, and why. He does not apologise. He numbers it.

**10. In Maylis's village Larrère is a window, a folder and a catchphrase. (ch. 6, 11, 13, 16)**
In Lesca's village he is a man: "'Eleven years I've been timed like an egg'", and the garden gate he comes to and is not let through. In Maylis's he blackmails, orders and drives off, and what he wants is only ever a car with its lights off. So his call at 02:53 from the Castagnet kitchen — "'He stays dry as long as you do what you're told'" — lands as a plot fact. It should be the ugliest thing a person does in the book.
*What I would do:* give Maylis one overheard exchange between him and Maïté in chapter 6 or 11, a few lines, in which he wants something and is refused or indulged. Then the kitchen clock behind his voice has a man standing under it.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The owner keeps her right hand.** The spoon tipped back into the bowl, the tap shut at the urn, the post at the bottom of the stairs, the handbrake. It makes two people in one body visible, funny and frightening with no explanation needed.
2. **Coralie on the landing, chapter 7.** Two steps, the wrist, "'That's her. In this hand'", the third step, and then "'You'll go back down those stairs.'" She wants the one who answers and refuses her anyway.
3. **The phone log, chapter 10.** "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." and "'Because you'd have told me.'" It re-reads the landing, the pulse-taking and eleven Decembers in the second row. It is the best scene in the book.
4. **The oven door, three times.** Chapter 1, chapter 8, and the one in chapter 16 that will not come.
5. **The last want, and the man it brings.** "'What do you want, Maylis?'" answered with the grave, then a letter that "doesn't thank you" and a second shovel. Also the dead who do not look up: her father marking a number beside his watch, Jean Castagnet putting a kernel on his second card while the woman who drowned him confesses to his shoulder.
