# Round 25 — Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes — 7.** "'Ten minutes against twenty-five. You turn the handle first.'" (8) The sum is clean and the comedy has something real to be played against.
- **Tension — 7.** "She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it broke." (13) Chapters 11 to 13 hold; chapters 9 and 10 make me wait for them.
- **Progression — 7.** "'Forget the note. The river only rises when you come.'" (8) The task changes at the right place, but it changes in one line of dialogue (fault 1).
- **Comedy — 7.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (6) Good, constant, and all in one register. I laughed aloud about a dozen times in seventeen chapters; The Good Place gets that in one episode.
- **Characters — 7.5.** "'I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it.'" (16) Coralie is the funniest person in the book and the one I would miss. Maylis is funny only as a right hand.
- **The switch between dark and comic — 8.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" (16) The same oven door, the third time, and it cannot be done. This is the thing the book does as well as the comparison.
- **Prose — 6.5.** "Somebody had sat down to play with the dead." (14) The sentence before it was "A chair was pulled in to a table." The narrator keeps adding the sentence that says what the last one did.
- **The ending — 7.5.** "'It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you.'" (16) The right last note, reached through a steel-room scene that recites the moral first.
- **Clarity — 6.** "She had known for a week that he would be." (5) The last woman named is Maylis; "she" is Lesca. In the shared-body chapters I stopped to work out whose "she" it was about once a page, and a stopped reader does not laugh.

Would I have finished it unpaid: yes. Would I press it on a friend: on one who likes dry village comedy, yes; not with the words "as funny as The Good Place".

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below The Good Place as comedy; level with it on the switch; and it charges more for its jokes than the series ever did.**

1. **Range.** The Good Place has four people who are wrong in four voices: Eleanor is crude, Chidi runs on, Tahani drops names, Jason is from another planet. Here twelve people are funny in one way, the flat literal retort that closes a scene. The book is amusing at a steady level and almost never gets out of hand.
2. **The reversal.** The series is remembered for the turn that makes you re-read everything before it. This book has one of the same size: the woman who came to stop the flood is the flood, and the thing on the chest was the only one guarding the village. It is said in two sentences in chapter 8 and nobody reacts to the second.
3. **The comic flaw as the moral flaw.** This is level, and in one respect above. "Either" starts on a shopping pad, is said to a demon, to a gendarme, and then at the winch with the man standing safe on the bank, and an old woman drowns. Chidi's indecision never killed anyone who stayed dead. The book makes its problem funny without making it small until chapter 11, and then it collects.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The book's one great reversal is thrown away in a line of dialogue. (8; structural)**

> 'Every night I cross, I bring a day of my flood with me.' She could say that, all of it, so she went on. 'And Inguma has taken me home every time, to keep my flood out of your village.'

Maylis answers only the first half: "'There's none now unless you bring it. Don't come on Friday.'" The second half is the bigger joke and the bigger horror. For four chapters the comedy has been about tricking the thing with sums: the gravel stones through the kitchen door, the verges it does "on foot", the maize at bingo. This sentence says every one of those tricks held the watchman off his post, and that Madame Etcheto, finishing the words over her duck in the funniest moment of the book, bought the river a day. Nobody on the page sees it. In chapter 11 Etcheto says "I said the rest of them for you, over my duck," and Maylis says "I heard." A reader should feel bingo night turn over under her at that moment, and the book has not given her what she needs to feel it.

What I would do: no new scene. In chapter 8, give the Inguma half its own beat. Maylis looks at what climbs into the chair at the end of that scene and it is not the same animal. One line that names the maize. Then in chapter 11, at the wheelchair, let Maylis's "I heard" carry it, or let the old woman, who is the only authority on the thing, be the one who works out what she did. Ten lines across two chapters. It also pays chapter 12, where Maylis hires the watchman back: "'You'll count the fingers of my right hand. Then you'll take her home.'"

**2. Everybody is witty in the same shape. (Whole book; structural)**

> 'A rib doesn't laugh.' (Etcheto, 2) — 'It's soup. It doesn't need a vote.' (Coralie, 3) — 'Then the village can sweep it.' (Darrigrand, 3) — 'Those are my boots.' (Jeannot, 4) — 'Winter things.' (Maïté, 4) — 'I'm not a visitor. I'm her mother.' (Arlette, 10) — 'Don't give me the minutes. Go.' (Coralie, 13)

Seven mouths, one joke-form: the short, literal comeback that ends the exchange. Each is good. Together they make a village of people who all had the same writer. The proof that the book can do otherwise is chapter 6, when Larrère is let off the timer and runs for eleven minutes: "'Eleven years I've been timed like an egg'", "'I'm told I wave.'" It is the funniest passage in the book because it is a different shape, a man who cannot stop. Sarthou is another shape ("'I shall require it. I shall lay it myself, and the village will receive a bill for my labour'"), Daguerre another ("'Here'"), Jeannot's numbered points another.

What I would do: ration the dry retort. Coralie and Madame Etcheto own it; leave it with them. Go through Maïté, Arlette, Darrigrand and Marie-Jo and cut the closing comeback wherever one of them has it. This is done by deletion, scene by scene.

**3. The last two chapters explain the two best dark jokes after they have landed. (15, 16)**

> 'Twelve. One, two. And a chair was pulled in.'
> 'That's my door.'
> 'Not this door. There are two of me, Madame Etcheto, and the other one has a village, with a room twelve at the end under the bank.'

Chapter 14 opens with the bingo caller calling a room number and a chair being pulled in. That is the best dark joke in the book and I got it in the dark, alone. Chapter 15 then has Lesca walk an old woman through it step by step. Chapter 16 does the same to the climax:

> 'At the winch I thought I could do better than you. The man and the gate, both. I drove past the handle to be the one who did.'

Chapter 13 has already said it in the narrator's voice: "Lesca had rung once and turned the handle, and Maylis thought she could do better than that. She wanted the man and the gate, both, and she wanted to be the one who got them." And "I slowed down to get more" is chapter 6 word for word. The room where a woman can only say what is true was a comic device in chapters 4 and 8 (the sentence that will not come out). In 16 it is a confessional and both women speak in finished paragraphs.

What I would do: in 15, keep "It never came last night … Where was it?", "It was with me", and the bath emptying; cut the explanation of the two villages. In 16, cut both confessions and go from "'It was mine,' Lesca said" to the stairs, the landing and "'Don't come again.' 'I won't.' It came out whole, and they both heard it." That last is the device paying off, and it is buried under the speeches.

**4. The rules of the thing are a joke a fifth of the time and plumbing the rest. (3, and the opening of 7 and 8)**

> So Lesca had a guess. Somewhere there was another Peyrebarthe, where the other lamp had come on.

The Good Place makes every rule of its world a gag at somebody's expense. This book can: the three points put to a vote with nobody in the chair (4), and "She had worked the verges out at the speed of the village tractor. Inguma was doing them on foot" (6). But chapter 3 spends nine paragraphs on a woman deducing a premise the reader has held since "to see whether there were two of you" in chapter 1, and it is the longest stretch without a smile before chapter 9.

What I would do: cut the deduction in 3 to its two contradictions (her ribs, a dead man's forty letters) and go to the stone. Keep the ladder ("It hadn't had it yet"), which is a discovery and has frost on it.

**5. The night of 2014 is told twice at length, and the book opens with no sign that it is a comedy. (00, 1, 9)**

> She took the winch handle out of its box. For three breaths she held it, and she did not fit it to the winch. (00)

> Before she fitted the handle to the winch, she turned the van around on top of the bank (9)

00 shows the handle, the van turned, the track, the one ring. Chapter 9 then says "One man knew she had looked down the track first", and I am the second, and have been for nine chapters. What 00 buys is good: every "No one came to tell him" from chapter 3 on is funny-terrible because I know. What it costs is chapter 9, which becomes a page and a half of straight retelling with one laugh held back for the end. Separately: 00 plus the paralysis is some eight hundred words of dread before "counted one of her, paid her, gave her a sandwich".

What I would do: keep 00. Cut chapter 9's flashback to what 00 did not show: the kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice, Coralie's car in the yard, the reflector going out, the sole of a wader, the priest. And shorten the paralysis in chapter 1 by a third, so the sandwich arrives sooner.

**6. The climax of the monster plot is a woman sitting on it, and the page will not smile. (12, set up in 10)**

> Then she sat down on Inguma.

Chapter 10 knows this is absurd: "'I'm going to sit on it,' Lesca said … The secretary had her hand over her mouth." Chapter 12 plays it as pure strain: steel plate, rivet heads, heels off the floor. Here the dark smothers a joke the book itself set up, and the result is neither frightening nor funny. Maylis is standing a metre away watching her own double ride the thing that has crushed her for a fortnight, and she has no reaction; she just pulls an arm.

What I would do: one look from Maylis before she pulls. One line. The scene after it (handbrake, hand over the eyes, the horn) is slapstick with a death in it and works because nobody pretends it is dignified.

**7. A joke at the care-home doorstep lets the worst moment go slack. (13)**

> 'I put that handle in number six,' he said.
> 'I wrote to them too.'
> 'I know you did. They took note.'

The line before it is the knife: "It would have gone up at three." Then two people trade the letters gag while the water climbs and one door is still shut. The callback is right and the place is wrong.

What I would do: cut the three lines here. "'It's no use to anybody now. Have it back'" follows straight on from the key. If "They took note" must be kept, Jeannot's letter in 16 already says "who had been written to about it."

**8. The narrator says the line before the character does, or after. (5, 13, 14)**

> At noon the whole village was in the café, and that was why Lesca had waited until noon to walk Maylis into it. (5)

Twelve lines later Lesca says it herself and it is the joke: "'I came at noon because you're all here at noon.'" In 13: "She knew whose kitchen. It was written on her screen," and then "He was telling her that from the man's own house, at three in the morning, on the man's phone." In 14: "Somebody had sat down to play with the dead."

What I would do: delete the three narrator sentences. Then search for more of the same; these are the three I marked, not all there are.

**9. People laugh on the page harder than the joke allows. (2)**

> The rib caught the laugh and turned it into a bark, and that finished Coralie. She put her forehead against the wall beside the door and shook

"Staff to wait. Staff not to join in" is funny. Two women helpless against a wall tell me it is funnier than it was. The rib that stops a laugh is a real motif and it pays in chapter 6, when a body with all its ribs laughs through the minute's silence; that payoff does not need this much set-up.

What I would do: stop at Coralie's shoulders. Keep the bark, because Madame Etcheto needs to hear it.

**10. The numbers of the running gag collide with the numbers of the dead. (5, 13, 16)**

> 'Put it in forty-two' (5) — 'one was what to do with forty-two people' (5) — 'Forty-one,' Coralie said (13) — 'I paid to register forty-one of them' (16)

Forty houses, forty letters in chapter 3, forty-one letters, letter forty-two, forty-two beds, forty-one living. The head-count that ends chapter 13 is the hardest line in the book and it shares its number with a joke about registered post.

What I would do: move the letters out of the forties.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **"Either" at the winch (11)**, with Jeannot safe on the bank and her weight on the handle, and everything that leads to it and from it: the pad, the eggs, "'Either,' Maylis said" to the monster, Sarthou's form, "That was Either, with the river in it."
2. **The oven door, three times (1, 8, 16).** Exactly three. The third one stopping at "The—".
3. **The council in chapter 6**: the wrist pinned to the table, Larrère finishing eleven years of sentences, the hand that gets loose and rings the bell on his question, "'The chair votes once, Madame le maire.'"
4. **The dead at bingo (12)**: "'I saw your van,' she said. 'I rang once.' He put a kernel on a card." And the man with his arm round his card. And "'The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies.'"
5. **The motion (3)**: the comma, the withheld line, and "*and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*" It is the moment the book says its comedy has teeth.

Also leave alone: Daguerre's "Here" and Maylis's "I know you are"; Kévin's five fingers; "And six. Bugger off."
