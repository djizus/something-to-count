# Round 25: Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. 42,952 words, 81 scenes. Counts below are mine, taken from those files.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "'The gate, or Castagnet? Say which, Maylis. For once.'" (13). Countable and personal by then. For ten chapters before it the threat to Maylis is a date in a folder.
- **Tension: 6.5.** "She pushed until four again, flat on the kitchen floor with her forearm across her side." (7). Two nights of the book's central contest are told as summary.
- **Progression: 6.5.** "'Every night I cross, I bring a day of my flood with me.'" (8). The rule the plot turns on arrives halfway through, as a sentence.
- **Comedy: 7.5.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (6).
- **Characters: 7.5.** "'I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it.'" (16).
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'" (13). The bingo joke comes back in a flooded corridor and does not break it.
- **Prose: 7.** "But the man behind the glass paid her wages, and she didn't knock on it." (1). The glass is the book's strength. The clause that explains, and the action told as a thing not done, are its two habits, and both are in this sentence.
- **The ending: 7.5.** "'You won't get that down two metres with one arm, Lesca.'" (16). The costs are paid. The scene in the steel room before it itemises what the reader already holds.
- **Clarity: 6.** "'It's the eighth this year. I've kept count too.'" (16). On the last page I cannot say what is being counted, or what "too" answers.

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. Would I press it on a friend? No. I would tell a friend about the oven door and the five fingers, and lend it if asked.

## 2. Against the comparisons

Sentence for sentence, above Crouch. At what his sentences are for, below him. Below The Good Place in the range of its voices.

1. **Crouch never makes the reader stop to compute. This book does, about once a page.** His prose is thin, first person, present tense, and every "I" has one owner. Here the reader must keep track of whose "she" it is, which Coralie, which Larrère is "the mayor", and which twelve. The grammar for the shared body is exact (see section 4), and the narration around it is not.
2. **Crouch gives fear in the body and lets motive stay dark until it turns. This book states motive as it goes.** "Wanted" appears 41 times, "because" 48. Feeling is entered in a ledger: "She was scared", "She liked it", "It scared her to do it". The objects carry more than Crouch's ever do (the pillow, the wrist, the pad), which is where it beats him.
3. **The Good Place gives each of its people a sentence nobody else could say. This book gives each a device and one shared sentence.** The devices are good: Jeannot's numbered points, Larrère's "I'll be brief", Sarthou's uncontracted officialese, Coralie's chart voice. Under them, seven speakers count grievance in years in the same shape, and the two women are narrated in one voice.

Punctuation is clean. No exclamation marks, no semicolons, no ellipses, dashes only for cut-off speech, thirteen distinct words ending in "-ly" in the book. The date arithmetic holds, the letter count holds, and every "two words", "three words", "five more words" and "six words long" is correct.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The narrator states the motives that the ending confesses, so the confession is not news. (13, 11, 6, then 16.)**
At the winch: "Lesca had rung once and turned the handle, and Maylis thought she could do better than that. She wanted the man and the gate, both, and she wanted to be the one who got them." At the dip: "If she went on, whatever the river did behind her was the river's. She wanted it not to be her." Three chapters later she says both, in the room where only the truth can be said: "At the winch I thought I could do better than you. The man and the gate, both. I drove past the handle to be the one who did." And to Kévin: "I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me." The reader has been told twice. The Good Place makes its reversals re-read what came before. Here nothing is re-read, because the narrator filed it at the time.
What I would do: cut the two glosses in 13, the one at the gate in 11 ("If the gate went up now and Lesca never came tonight, the flood on the meadows was Maylis's alone. At three, if it came, it would be the river's doing."), and "She wanted one day as the woman who signed things" in 6. Leave the acts: three breaths with her foot off the pedal, then "She drove past." On first reading it is courage. In 16 it becomes vanity, and the reader goes back. The same cut applies to the stated resolve that ends 9: "Whatever happened at that gate tonight, Maylis would do it herself."

**2. Two women, one narrator's voice. (All chapters.)**
Chapter 3 opens by repeating chapter 1's first sentence, which is right. It then goes on in chapter 1's syntax. Lesca is the woman who acts, and her narration is built from the same negations as Maylis's: "She didn't try her hands", "she had told nobody", "She didn't trust it", "Lesca didn't answer". The dialogue has the same fault. "Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you" (Coralie). "Eleven years I've been timed like an egg" (Larrère). "I've kept this counter thirty years" (Josiane). "For thirty years he stopped four turns short" (Jeannot). "I've told them so for twelve years" (Madame Etcheto). "Years" appears 43 times.
What I would do: keep the negative sentence for Maylis and take it out of Lesca's chapters (3, 10, 12, 14, 15), where the narration should run on what she does and signs. Keep the years tally for Coralie, whose grievance it is, and cut it from four of the other speakers.

**3. The shared-body scenes change heads without notice. (5, 7, 8, 12.)**
In the café in 5, "Maylis felt the mouth get ready for the word" is followed a page later by "She had known for a week that he would be. His forty letters had been in her head since the first night", which is Lesca, and then "In sixteen years Maylis had never got that laugh out of her", which is Maylis again. "She had no answer for him" and "She had come for this room, and now she had it" have no owner until the reader works it out. In 12 the narrator stands outside both: "Maylis wanted her out, because every minute Lesca stayed was the river. Lesca wanted to stay."
What I would do: one head for each scene. In 5, 7 and 8 stay with Maylis in the passenger seat, who can know Lesca only by what the body does ("the eyes wouldn't leave him"). It is funnier and more frightening, and it saves Lesca's inside for 12. Move the frost-in-the-letters lines to a Lesca chapter.

**4. The premise and its rules are stated as findings. (3, 4, 8, 11.)**
"So Lesca had a guess. Somewhere there was another Peyrebarthe, where the other lamp had come on." (3). Later in the same chapter it is delivered in scene, on the ladder: "That village hadn't been spared its flood. It hadn't had it yet." In 4 the meeting earns its three points, because the meeting is a joke. Then: "Standing in this room, a woman can only say what's true." In 8: "And Inguma has taken me home every time, to keep my flood out of your village." In 11 the narrator says it again: "What broke it was the last day of Lesca's flood. That day was tonight's, and it would only come across if Lesca did."
What I would do: cut the guess in 3 and let the ladder carry it. In 4 and 8 keep the sentence that will not come out ("I was going to tell you it was the tide. I can't get it out.") and cut the sentence that names the rule. Maylis's evidence in 8 is enough: "The gauge didn't move, and it didn't rain." Cut the paragraph in 11.

**5. Three scenes in four open on a clock or a calendar. (59 of 81.)**
Chapter 5's six scenes open "It was ten past six on Monday the thirtieth of November", "At eight", "at nine", "At noon", "At seven", "At five past nine". Chapters 4 and 5 both set their first scene with the same clause: "with rain on the kitchen window". The book has one clock that frightens, 02:51, and one calendar date. The rest is a timetable.
What I would do: give the time only where the time is the threat. Open the others as 9 opens ("'Hand,' Coralie said.") and as the association scene in 8 opens ("'I'm heating the whole square'").

**6. The numbers collide. (Throughout.)**
About 730 number words, not counting "one", and 110 figures: one word in fifty. Twelve is the years since the flood, the room, the date, the seats in the minibus and the letters the dead Castagnet sent. Eleven is the date, the years of the landing, the hands in the vote, the chairs Maylis watches, the stairs, the coffees and the hour. Nineteen is the days of rain, the turn of the winch, the bags of grit and Coralie's age. In 5, "'Put it in forty-two'" is forty lines from "what to do with forty-two people". Forty-one is the letters Jeannot registered and the residents who lived. Room twelve against the bingo call is designed and pays in 14. The others are noise around it.
What I would do: keep the numbers that are clocks or keys (02:51, ten against twenty-five, the nineteenth turn, room twelve, letter forty-two). Change or cut the incidental ones: "eleven coffees", "watched her do eleven chairs", "all eleven", "nineteen bags", and the count of beds.

**7. The sum is recited until it wears. (3, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 14, 15.)**
"Forty houses" eleven times, "No one came to tell him" six, ten minutes against twenty-five in five chapters. Lesca's account being a formula is the point, and the book says so in 15. The narrator need not repeat it for her. Chapter 9 retells the prologue beat for beat (the van turned, the phone, the one ring) before reaching the only new thing, the reflector. In 10: "She had the sum. It was forty houses, forty-two beds, a car in the yard of the care home and a van at the end of a track, with ten minutes against twenty-five."
What I would do: start 9's account at the headlights. In 10 keep "She had the sum" and go straight to "all she found was a handle, turning". Cut "Ten minutes against twenty-five" from Lesca's speech in 8, a page after the reader walked it in 7.

**8. Action told as a thing not done, at a density that flattens it. (Throughout, worst in 6, 7, 12 to 15.)**
"Didn't" 68 times, "nobody" 62, "nothing" 51, "never" 44. The closing clause "and she didn't" or its kin, 37 times: "and she didn't knock on it", "and Maylis didn't ask", "and he let it", "and I don't open it", "and she didn't stop". It is the right figure for a woman who says Either. It is used for everyone: "He came no closer" (2), "Coralie didn't touch her" (10), "He didn't look up from his numbers" (12), "Nobody came through it" (13). Around it the sentence has one shape, two short clauses joined by ", and", 922 times in about 4,000 sentences. The one change of voice is in 13, where fragments arrive at 02:57 ("Second gear, through ruts full of water."), stop at the hatch, come back at 03:06 ("No way around it.") and stop at the yard.
What I would do: ration the negative clause to Maylis before the handle in 11, where it means something. In 13 commit to the fragments from the dip to the gap, or use none.

**9. The props are named past the point of need. (1 to 8.)**
"Rib" 62 times, ten of them in chapter 1: "left-handed because of the rib", "a little at a time, because of the rib", "on her back, because she had to". The laugh that catches on the rib is played in 1, 2 and 8. "Hand" 288 times. The trailing ", with" phrase 173 times: "with her keys in her hand", "with the coffee pot in her hand", "with a potato in one hand and a knife in the other", "with his glass", "with her bag on her arm".
What I would do: after chapter 1, cut every "because of the rib". "Left-handed" tells it. Play the rib laugh twice. Halve the ", with" phrases, starting with the ones that hold an object the scene never uses.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The grammar of who owns what.** "Lesca stood Maylis on the platform." The driver gets "the left hand", "the mouth", "the jaw". The owner keeps "her right hand". I checked every use in 5, 6, 7, 8 and 12 and found no slip. With it, the narrator's "Maylis" and "Lesca", and Jeannot's "little Lesca" becoming "Lesca" in the last speech.
2. **The prologue returning word for word in 13.** "She put her hand flat on the clay beside the water, and the clay moved under her palm." "She was scared." And the reflector sentence of 9 returning in 11: "screwed on crooked, and it shone red."
3. **The oven door.** "'In my tray,' she said, and shut the door on him." (1), again in 8, and broken in 16: "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'"
4. **Kévin's fingers.** "'Five,' he said." (13) and "'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much.'" (16).
5. **Room twelve.** Nine, ten and eleven open, each with one object. "...and she held it for the full half minute." "Then she took the pencil on its cord and drew a line through the last name on the list." Not a word of feeling is in it, and none should be added.
