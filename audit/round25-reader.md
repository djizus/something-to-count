## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below on clarity, below on the first quarter, above on what stays, above on the cost of the ending. Net: just under the bar until chapter 11, at or over it from there.**

- **Rules.** Dark Matter asks a reader to hold one rule (the box, and your state of mind picks the door). This book asks for seven: what one knows the other gets; whoever stays seated wakes in the other body; the owner keeps the right hand; the thing takes the visitor home when its count ends; nobody can lie in the steel room; each crossing brings a day of flood; the one who cannot be taken home sits down with the dead. The Good Place carries that many, and delivers them the same way, as procedure ("'in a tie the chair decides.' / 'There's nobody in the chair.'"). That delivery is level with the series. But the series never lets the audience doubt a rule at the moment it decides someone's fate. This book does, on the last night (fault 2).
- **Speed.** Crouch has his man abducted before chapter 1 ends. Here the first body is taken on the last line of chapter 2, which is quick enough. Then chapter 3 rewinds four nights and takes eleven sections. That is the one place a Crouch reader stops.
- **The marriage.** Crouch makes me afraid a wife will not tell the two men apart. Here the wife tells them apart by pulse, prefers the wrong one, says so, and still refuses her: "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'" (7) That is better than the comparison.
- **Reversals that re-read.** Three, all clean: "Castagnet, house" on the phone screen (13); "'Nine minutes to three,' she said. 'I've known that for twelve years.'" (10); "fetching a man out of a dry hole" (16). Level with the series.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. Chapter 3 keeps me from the bed, then sags once I am back in it.**
"'It's four when you're the one counting,' he said."
Chapter 2 ends with a stranger using Maylis's mouth. Chapter 3 goes back four nights to another village, and the crossing I am waiting for is the seventh section of eleven. That part I will sit through, because the stone, the timer and the frost on the ladder each pay. The damage is after the crossing: the doorstep, the motion begun, the mother's kitchen, the motion finished. Four sections of falling action after the strongest scene so far, and the motion's last line has to do the work of restarting me.
*Do:* cut the Darrigrand step from this chapter (his "four" can be one line of Arlette's in 14, where it already half is). Move Arlette's kitchen before the crossing, so Lesca has the words before she needs them. Write the motion once, straight after "So it could be done. She could cross." End on its full text. Take the gauge sheets from three quoted lines to one.

**2. On the last night the book does not show its own rule, and the page supports the opposite reading.**
"What broke it was the last day of Lesca's flood. That day was tonight's, and it would only come across if Lesca did." (11)
Against that: at five the river is "at the height of her knees", which is exactly where the opening has it at ten to three in 2014; at ten it is "over the line and still rising"; and the phone that brings the news of ninety over starts ringing before Lesca has crossed. So I believed the bank was going whatever Maylis did in the van, which makes the vigil pointless and the *Either* at five the whole failure. Then chapter 16 has both women agree it was Lesca's river. I cannot tell which the book means, and it is the question the ending rests on.
*Do:* one gauge reading at 01:12 that has not moved since eleven. One more after the eighth ring that has. Then "You're the water" is something I saw.

**3. Chapter 12: I do not know what Maylis is fighting for.**
"Maylis wanted her out, because every minute Lesca stayed was the river. Lesca wanted to stay."
That sentence is the only statement of the stakes, and what follows is a fight over a steering wheel, a gear lever, the eyes and a handbrake. It reads as a fight about where the van goes. Lesca wants the blind. Three minutes later Maylis drives to the blind. I had to go back to see that the fight was about who was in the body, not about the direction. It also asks eight rings of a phone to cover starting a van, driving the bank and turning onto the track.
*Do:* bring "'You're the water.'" forward to the second or third ring. Let the right hand's first act be to point at the gauge or the patch. Drop the counted rings as the clock and use the dashboard minute.

**4. The night of the flood is told four times.**
"Before she fitted the handle to the winch, she turned the van around on top of the bank, so that its headlights pointed down the track across the meadows." (9)
The opening shows me the look down the track and the single ring. So from chapter 3 I know what "No one came to tell him" hides, and Maïté's question is never a question for me. That is a fair trade for the hook. But chapter 9 then replays the whole night, and chapter 14 summarises it again across two sheets of a speech, before the one sentence that matters.
*Do:* in 9, cut everything the opening gave and keep what is new: Coralie's car in the yard, the reflector, the four minutes until "the red light went out", the wader, the priest. In 14, give sheets one and two a sentence each.

**5. The last two chapters tell me again what I watched.**
"'It took me to the hall where the dead play. They had a place for me at their table, with my name on the card, and they called me to it. I wouldn't sit down.'" (15)
That is chapter 12, repeated to a woman in a wheelchair. In 16: "'I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me'" repeats "She wanted it not to be her" (13). "'I drove past the handle to be the one who did'" repeats "she wanted to be the one who got them" (13). "I don't know" which she turned it for is said in 10, in 15 and in 16.
*Do:* in 15, start Lesca's answer at "Then they called twelve." In the last steel room, keep only what is new: the stairs, "'She took the pillow'", "'Don't come again.'" / "'I won't.'" and that it comes out whole. Let Maylis say the river line to Kévin or to Lesca, not both.

**6. The hinge of the book is its least clear sentence.**
"If the gate went up now and Lesca never came tonight, the flood on the meadows was Maylis's alone. At three, if it came, it would be the river's doing." (11)
This is why she takes the handle off in daylight with the man standing on the bank behind her. Everything after follows from it, and I read it twice. Chapter 13 says the same thing in six words.
*Do:* say it here as plainly as there. She could flood three hundred hectares on her own word, or wait and let the river be the one who did it.

**7. "Sit on it" is a rule invented the morning it is needed.**
"'I'm going to sit on it,' Lesca said. 'It can't take me home while I'm sitting on its back.'" (10)
Nothing has told her that. And I had an obvious question the book does not answer: she got a whole day by giving it a lake of gravel to count, so why not give it something bigger?
*Do:* one line. A count always ends, and she means not to be sent home at all. Let her doubt it will work.

**8. I cannot picture the gate.**
"'On Monday you put the gate up with the river low, and my water ran out through it.'" (1)
Raising the gate drains his blind in chapter 1 and drowns it in chapter 11. Both are right, but nobody tells me that water goes through an open gate toward whichever side is lower. Nor do I see why a flooded meadow does not reach the Port, or the breach the blind, when both lie on the land side of one bank.
*Do:* two plain sentences in chapter 1, in Maylis's order of the bank, where "The meadows were a basin" already stands.

**9. Bingo night: I do not know what the shut teeth are for, and a day goes uncounted.**
"Maylis had her own teeth shut, and she meant to keep them shut until the hall was empty." (7)
I learn only at the end of the scene that she is stopping the words that would buy Lesca another night. Until then it is a woman with her jaw clamped for no stated reason, and the scene is long. Also: Lesca is in the body from six that morning until the evening with no count given, though in chapter 4 an uncounted crossing lasted twenty minutes.
*Do:* a clause at the first clamp saying what she is keeping in. A line at six that morning in which Lesca gives the thing something to count.

**10. Two loose ends I was left holding.**
"She had been Lesca's deputy mayor until a night in 2018, when she handed her sash back across the council table in a plastic bag." (3)
I have to join Labat to Labat and the cameras to the prison three chapters later, and it is never confirmed. And "'It's the eighth this year. I've kept count too.'" (16): eighth what, and who else is counting?
*Do:* in 6, when Kévin takes off his cap, one clause naming the night his mother gave back the sash. Cut or finish the priest's line.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. ***Either***, from the shopping pad to "'That was Either, with the river in it.'", including "'Either,' Maylis said" on the platform with her weight on the handle. It is the joke and the catastrophe, and it is the same word.
2. **Coralie at the oven door**, all three times, and the third one breaking off at "'The—'".
3. **Chapter 13 from "She drove past" to "'Forty-one.'"** Every minute stamp, Daguerre's "'Here'", Kévin's "'Five'", the list on its cord, the pulse taken in room twelve.
4. **The confession and what the square does with it** in 14: the sentence going back row by row, the man who claps, the child who asks, and "'Somebody has to stack them.'"
5. **Jeannot's numbered grievances**, from "'Put it in forty-two'" through "'And six. Bugger off.'" to letter No. 42, "it doesn't thank you", and the second shovel.
