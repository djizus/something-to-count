# Critic report: Thriller lens

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** *"That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it."* The private stake is as sharp as Crouch's. The public one stays a formula ("forty houses, and the care home, with forty-two people in their beds") until chapter 11, and when the water comes it reaches "the height of a table".
- **Tension: 6.5.** *"Three. The right hand let go of the wheel and knocked the lever out of gear."* Chapters 0 to 5 and 11 to 13 hold. Chapters 6 to 10 run on votes, forms and a kitchen timer.
- **Progression: 6.** *"'Forget the note. The river only rises when you come.'"* The book's one true reversal is announced, with nothing on the page between the note that invites Lesca and the sentence that cancels it.
- **Comedy: 7.** *"'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'"*
- **Characters: 8.** *"'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'"*
- **The switch between dark and comic: 7.5.** *"'It's Saturday.' 'Then they'll want the bins out,' he said."* In the flooded corridor the joke raises the fear. In the hall of the dead ("'The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies'") the joke lets Lesca walk out of her own death for nothing.
- **Prose: 7.** *"The river went through the opening under her feet with a noise she had never heard it make."* Plain and exact. The same plainness is used for a council vote and a breaking bank, so the action never gets louder than the minutes.
- **The ending: 7.** *"'I know what you picked. I was the other one.'"* The cost to Maylis is real. It is then settled across three chapters in which every prop comes back to be paid off.
- **Clarity: 6.** *"'Every night I cross, I bring a day of my flood with me.'"* That is the rule the last third runs on, and the river gauge in chapter 7 contradicts it (fault 2).

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. I would have stopped once, in chapter 6, and come back.

**Press it on a friend?** Not on the friend who reads Crouch for speed. On the one who liked The Good Place, yes.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below.** About a 7 against an 8.

- **The premise is in play as fast as Crouch's, and then stops escalating.** Dark Matter has Jason out of his life by the end of its first chapter; here the double speaks at the end of chapter 2 ("'Come back across,' her mouth said. Maylis hadn't said it.") and states her aim at the end of chapter 3. That is level. But Crouch multiplies the threat in the back half: one rival becomes dozens, all after the same wife. Here the threat narrows. Coralie knows the visitor within twenty minutes of the first crossing ("'Who is that?'"), turns her down on the landing in chapter 7 and pins her hand to the table in chapter 8. After that the reader is never again afraid that Lesca will get the bed.
- **The reversal is the right size and is not staged.** The Good Place turns on a character working it out in front of everyone, in a scene, and every earlier episode re-reads. "You're the water" does re-read the book: the rescuer is the flood, and the thing on the wardrobe has been protecting the village. But Maylis has the evidence on a Saturday, writes Lesca an invitation on the Tuesday and announces the discovery on the Wednesday night, and a room where nobody can lie confirms it for her.
- **The choice is harder than any Crouch sets, and less legible when it is made.** Crouch's choices are clean and felt in one image. This one is better thought: one woman pulled the lever and has had a man on her chest for twelve years; the other would not pull it and has a woman drowned in her bed. But it is made in subtraction ("six hundred metres behind her… four hundred metres in front… six minutes left"), and the reader cannot tell at the dip whether she is choosing or still trying for both.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Chapter 6: nine days from the flood, the heroine takes a day off.**
*"Maylis wanted to see that. She wanted one day as the woman who signed things."*
Her crossing has no errand. The one fact she asks for could be had at home, and the book says so: "The statutes were older than the steel room, so they said the same thing at home." What follows is eleven minutes of Larrère untimed ("'Eleven years I've been timed like an egg'"), a vote on who speaks at a ceremony, and a wait for Inguma that ends because it turns up. This is where a Crouch reader puts the book down. It is the third of five meetings with a chair, a timer or a count (chapters 3, 4, 6, 8, 15).
*Do:* send her across for something she can only get there and needs by Friday: the 2014 file, the association's book with the order "with nothing written under it", the hour the bank went. Let her come back with one fact that changes Friday. Cut Larrère's speech to the two sentences that matter, the order by phone and "did you—". Keep the thirteen hours and the pillow crossing the landing; that price is right.

**2. Chapters 7 and 8: the turn of the book happens between two scenes, and its rule does not match the gauge.**
*"You can have the right."* (Tuesday's note) and, the next scene, *"'Forget the note. The river only rises when you come.'"*
On Saturday in chapter 7 she already sees it: "Yesterday morning that iron had said a metre. She had nobody to say it to, and no mouth to say it with." She has her mouth back on Sunday morning. On Tuesday she offers Lesca both arms for Friday night. On Wednesday she has worked it out, and nothing has happened in between. Then the rule: "a day of my flood" per crossing. After two dry nights the river should run two days late. It does not: "'Fifty-five under… My file has the same.'" So a crossing brings the river up to the file's date, not one day on, and the whole of Friday's plan ("it would only come across if Lesca did") rests on which it is.
*Do:* make the discovery a scene with a trigger, in daylight, before any note. The café's pad already holds it: one cross, one tick. Let Maylis see that the cross sits on the two nights she kept Lesca out. Then either drop the note or have her tear it up at that table. State the rule once in the words the gauge supports.

**3. Chapter 13: the decisive step cannot be read as a choice when she takes it.**
*"Maylis turned her back on the lamp and went on."* Then, at the hatch: *"'Come up. I'm opening the gate.'"* Then, three chapters later: *"'It wasn't Either at the dip… I had to pick one. I picked.'"*
At the dip the book gives the motive ("She wanted it not to be her") and then has her act as if she still means to do both, in sequence, on foot. The reader is asked to hold ten minutes, twenty-five, four, three-two-three, six hundred metres, four hundred, two hundred, sixteen. The numbers do the work the fear should do. "It would have gone up at three" two pages later is not clear either: by whose arms?
*Do:* at the dip, one sentence that closes the gate for good in her mind, in plain words, before she turns: going on means the Port. At the hatch, have her say what is now true, that the bank is going, not that she is opening anything. Cut the sums to the two the reader already owns, ten against twenty-five.

**4. Chapter 13: the one person the reader is afraid for walks back up the ramp after four wheelchairs.**
*"She was back at the doorstep with his empty chair when Coralie came up through the fire door."*
The book has spent twelve chapters putting Coralie thirty metres from the patch. Maylis sends her to the bank end herself, the lights go out, and the beat at the fire door is the best choice in the chapter ("Between her and the door sat a row of people who couldn't walk, and one of them was in her hand"). Then it is released within a page, unaided, and the evacuation that follows is logistics with the outcome known ("'one I can't'").
*Do:* keep the fire door dark and silent until the corridor is empty. Maylis pushes every chair with that door at her back. Coralie is found at 04:10, at the door of twelve, with her shoulder still against it. Same deaths, same count, and the fear runs under the whole evacuation.

**5. Chapters 0, 9 and 14: the night of 2014 is told three times, and the first telling spends the second.**
*"One man knew she had looked down the track first."*
The reader was the second; the opening showed the headlights turned down the track and "It rang once." So for eight chapters "No one came to tell him" is an evasion the reader has already seen through, and chapter 9's page-long account, handed to Maylis by an empty chair on the eve of the flood, reveals only what is new: the kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice, Coralie's car in the yard, the reflector going out after four minutes.
*Do:* keep the opening whole. Cut chapter 9's account to those three things and the sole of the wader, at half the length, and get faster to "She had never had a time of her own."

**6. Chapters 7 to 12: the invader stops being dangerous at the midpoint, and her death costs her nothing.**
*"'You'll go back down those stairs.'"* and *"'She has a ceremony at eleven, and she will not be taking this seat.'"*
After the landing, Lesca's only move is to turn up. Her promised price for staying, set in chapter 2 and printed on a card at the end of chapter 8, is waived with a line for the minutes; she stands, walks, and is taken home anyway.
*Do:* let her nearly win once more after chapter 7, on Coralie's side of it, not Maylis's: Coralie has said "I'd have you tomorrow," so give Thursday a moment where she means it. And in the hall, make standing up cost: the chair she refuses is the chair that is pulled in, and she hears whose number fills it before the lights go.

**7. Chapters 3 to 10: the flood has no body.**
*"'Behind the bank there are forty houses, and the care home.'"*
For ten chapters the river is a figure read off iron: a metre and a half under, seventy under, fifty-five, forty. The first water that does anything is "no wider than a bootlace", in chapter 8. The Port has three faces, and two of them (Madame Darrigrand, Bordenave's house) arrive in chapter 8. When it comes, "'Bordenave is at his upstairs window. He says that he is dry up there and that he is staying.'" The deflation is the point of chapter 10 ("'The others have died since then, in those beds'"), but the reader has to have been afraid first.
*Do:* put the Darrigrand doorstep and the baby in the Port by chapter 2, on the night Maylis fetches Coralie. Give the patch one physical scene before chapter 8 in which the clay does something to a hand or a boot.

**8. Chapters 9 to 11: on the day of the flood the clock runs backwards.**
*"'I said I'd never set foot in here,' she said."*
Chapter 9 ends at ten past five on Friday morning. Chapter 10 spends a morning in the village whose flood is twelve years old, on a visit from a mother and a roll of tape. Chapter 11 then restarts Friday at eleven. Chapters 9 and 10 both end on a statement of intent, not a changed situation ("Whatever happened at that gate tonight, Maylis would do it herself").
*Do:* chapter 10 is the call log. Open on Coralie with the speech in her hand. Give Arlette two lines inside that scene, give the eight strips of tape a paragraph, and end on Lesca going anyway.

**9. Chapters 1 to 11: the man with the power is never frightening until 02:53 on the last night.**
*"'No,' Larrère said. 'That's the file.'"*
His hold is a folder, and Kévin cancels it before it is used: "'I'd do them, Maylis. Don't keep a gate shut for me.'" He is a gap in a window for eleven chapters. Then: "'He's in his blind, yes. He stays dry as long as you do what you're told.'" That man should be in the book sooner.
*Do:* let him say that sentence, or its like, on the Friday morning in chapter 11, so that the reader knows by daylight that he is holding Jeannot's hole over her. Have him take one physical thing from her before Friday, not a signature.

**10. Chapters 15 and 16: three chapters of settlement on one open question, and the accounts are stated twice.**
*"'I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me.'"* (chapter 16) against *"She wanted it not to be her."* (chapter 13).
Also: "'At the winch I thought I could do better than you'" against "Maylis thought she could do better than that"; and Maïté's "'You stood in my kitchen that night and rang her from my phone'" against a screen that already said "Castagnet, house". After "Forty-one" the only question left is whether Coralie stays, and it is answered in the fourth scene of the last chapter. Everything else is a prop returning: the timer, the oven door, the suitcase, the pad, letter forty-two, the finger count, the stacked chairs.
*Do:* cut every sentence in 15 and 16 in which a character explains a motive the reader watched. In the last steel-room scene keep "Is yours in the house?", "Don't come again", "I won't". Run the bus, the café and Marie-Jo as one short scene. Leave the grave as it is.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 3, the last line.** *"That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it."* It turns a rescue into a theft in one clause.
2. **Chapter 5, the stairs.** The cut palm on the post, Inguma coming down past Coralie's ankle, and *"'Are you coming up?'"*
3. **Chapter 10, the call log.** *"'Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one.'"* and *"'Because you'd have told me.'"*
4. **Chapter 11, five o'clock on the platform.** Daylight, the handle on, the man standing on the bank behind her, and *"'Either,' Maylis said."* with Jeannot's *"'Then it's shut.'"* And Madame Darrigrand naming it in chapter 16.
5. **Chapter 13, the old wing.** *"Nine was open… Ten was open… Eleven was open… Twelve was shut."* The pulse held for the full half minute, the line through the last name, and *"'Forty-one,' Coralie said."*
