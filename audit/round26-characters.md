# Critic report: Characters (round 26)

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.5.** "'The gate, or Castagnet? Say which, Maylis. For once.'" (13). By then the forty-two beds, the man in the cellar and the woman who asks the question are one stake. Before chapter 9 what Maylis stands to lose is a job and a boy's six months.
- **Tension: 7.** "It was an hour and nine minutes short of four." (11). Chapters 9 and 11 to 13 are held tight. Chapters 7 and 8 play the same tussle for the jaw four times.
- **Progression: 6.5.** "On Monday night she tried again. The rib let her have one breath, and then the arms of the chair were warm." (8). Three nights of pushing, two votes that shut the same gate, two breakfasts with a mouth being fought over.
- **Comedy: 7.5.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (6).
- **Characters: 7.** "'There's a key, and a winch, and Larrère, and a van. And then there's me, as a distance.'" (8). Coralie is an 8 and so is Jeannot. Maylis is a 6 until chapter 6. Lesca is a 7.5.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Five,' he said." (13). A boy checks whether he is dreaming, thigh-deep in a flood, with a wheelchair in his hands.
- **Prose: 7.** "The reflector went on shining in her headlights at the end of the track for four more minutes." (9). Plain and exact. The cost of the plainness is in fault 7.
- **The ending: 7.5.** "'You won't get that down two metres with one arm, Lesca.'" (16). He drops "little". It costs Maylis Coralie, and then the village cushions it.
- **Clarity: 6.5.** "'If one of us stays sitting in this chair when the other wakes up, she wakes up in the other one's body, and she drives it.'" (4). The rule is clear once stated, and it is stated a chapter after the reader needed it.

Finished unpaid: yes, from chapter 9 without stopping. Pressed on a friend: not yet. I would lend it and say "wait for chapter 5".

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below the bar on characters: 7 against 8.** Three things put it there.

**Below Crouch at making the reader afraid for a marriage.** Dark Matter opens on a kitchen the reader wants back before anything is taken. This book opens on a marriage that is already hungry: "Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you, and that was me." There is one warm evening (the oven door, the strapped rib) and then the intruder arrives and is better at the marriage than the wife: "In sixteen years Maylis had never got that laugh out of her. Lesca had got it in ten minutes." By chapter 7 the reader agrees with Coralie's "I'd have the one who answers." A reader who agrees with that is not afraid for the marriage. They are waiting to see whether it deserves to survive, which is cooler.

**Level with The Good Place on people each wrong in their own way.** Lesca pulls the lever and then edits the story for twelve years. Maylis will not pull it, so that her hand is clean. Coralie never asks, so that she can keep the woman without the answer. Larrère gives orders by phone so that nothing is signed. Jeannot votes shut because of who asked him. Maïté keeps a packed suitcase with no dust on it and does not carry it out. Nobody's wrong is a copy of anybody else's, and each one is what causes the next. This is the book's best thing.

**Above Crouch on the double.** Jason2 is a thief with a speech. Lesca is a thief who brings a rescue and a flood in the same hand, writes down her own motive ("and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it"), and whose one act of generosity, "Go to the blind first", is what drowns room twelve. That is a better other self than Dark Matter has.

**Below both on the lead as company.** Eleanor and Jason are people the reader likes to be with on page one. Maylis's first ten spoken lines are refusals: "It's the rib." "Nothing. Go back to sleep." "Whichever's less trouble." "I've got lights to hang." "I did a rib." It is the design, and it means the book's lead is its least vivid person for five chapters.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The reader is given no reason to want Maylis to keep Coralie, so the ending reads as justice and not as loss. (Chapters 1 to 7. Structural.)**
"'I don't know who I've had at this table. The café says you've got a mayor in you. I asked five things and I got five answers. … I'd have this one again.'" (5)
The book shows what Coralie lacks, in every scene. It shows what she gets from Maylis twice: the laugh at the oven door in chapter 1 and the bandage in chapter 9 ("Duck."). Between them Lesca takes the kitchen, and nothing she does there is worse than what Maylis does. The other village already holds the answer: when that Coralie says "It's soup. It doesn't need a vote", Lesca does not laugh. Maylis is the woman who laughs at Coralie. Lesca is the woman who makes Coralie laugh and never laughs back. Put that test into chapter 5. Let Coralie do the oven door, or any voice, for the woman who gave Larrère three minutes, and get an answer where she expected a laugh, and notice. One beat. Then "I'd have this one again" is a choice with a cost in it, and the reader has something to fear losing.

**2. The village lets Maylis off. (Chapter 16. Cuts.)**
"'One,' she said, in the voice she called the numbers in. 'On its own. Sit down on it.'"
Forty houses went under and a woman drowned in her bed. The Port's anger is one speech from one doorstep (Madame Darrigrand, and it is the best thing in the first scene) and one gag at the café. Around them Sarthou declines to record his opinion, Kévin tows the van for nothing, Marie-Jo sets out a chair, Coralie takes room twelve as partly hers, and Jeannot brings four pages and a shovel. Lesca saved forty-two and gets a square walking away from her, a medal asked back, her mother's back and eleven hands. Maylis lost one and is handed kindness five times in a chapter. Jeannot's shovel is the ending, and it is worth less as the fifth hand than it would be as the first. Cut Marie-Jo's chair. Cut Kévin's "Nothing. You looked the other way for four winters", and keep "I wanted six". Let Bordenave's pencil move: he signed the petition, his house is under that bank, and his pad has her page. Her mother lives across the yard and is not in the chapter at all. Give her one line or the reader asks where she is.

**3. Larrère's second sheet gives Maylis an alibi for the silence that is supposed to be her nature. (Chapters 6, 8, 11.)**
"*Labat, Kévin.* Behind the car the boy was still on his ladder. Maylis didn't say it a third time." (8)
She has just said "Open" twice in public, which is more than she has said in eighteen years, and what stops her is a threat to a boy. That is decency under blackmail. It is not Either. The book knows it, because it spends two later scenes removing the excuse (Kévin's "Don't keep a gate shut for me" in 11, her "I didn't" in 16). The beat also changes nothing: Jeannot's hand shuts the gate four to three whatever she says. Cut the sheet from chapter 8 and keep it in chapter 6, where it buys the signature. Then "Either" on the platform in 11 stands with nothing to hide behind. The same device keeps this village's Larrère a car window from first page to last. The other Larrère gets eleven untimed minutes and a garden gate and becomes a man. This one needs a single sentence that is not an order, and the place is the 02:53 call from the Castagnet kitchen.

**4. Lesca's renunciation happens in one sentence that the reader has to decode. (Chapter 12.)**
"The hand locked on the brake was the hand that had held the stair post against her, and at the end of the headlights the reflector was red, and no nearer than it had been twelve years ago."
Then: "I'm letting go." Four chapters earlier she said "I won't be sent home", and she has just sat on the thing that takes her home. This is the co-lead's moral choice and the turn the whole crossing was built for, and it takes eight rings and a sentence with two images in it. I worked out why she lets go on the second reading. Say it plainly, in her idiom, in one or two sentences before "I'm letting go": with that hand on the brake she gets neither the man nor the bed, and the only thing she can still give is the van.

**5. The rule that nobody can lie in the steel room does Lesca's honesty for her. (Chapters 4 and 8.)**
"'I was going to tell you it was the tide,' she said. 'I can't get it out.'" (8)
Three times a false sentence stops in her mouth. So the discovery that she is the flood is extracted from her, not admitted, and the four meetings between the two women lose what a scene between two people needs, which is the chance that one of them is lying. The square in chapter 14 is stronger than any of them because no rule is helping her. Keep the rule in chapter 4, where it is funny. Keep "I w—" in chapter 8, because "I won't" coming out whole in chapter 16 depends on it. Cut the tide. Let her say "Every night I cross, I bring a day of my flood with me" because Maylis has cornered her with two dry nights, not because the room gagged her.

**6. The two women never look at each other. (Chapters 4, 8, 12, 16.)**
"They were the same height, and neither of them liked it." (4)
That is the only sentence in the book about what it is to face yourself. Their relationship is carried by hands: the spoon, the stair post, the handbrake. Those are the best scenes in the book, and they leave the meetings in the room as agenda items. Lesca is looking at herself before the gate, a woman with no scar and no stone, and she has a reaction to the word Either and to nothing else. Maylis is looking at what she would be if she had ever decided anything. The only exchange in which they are two people who know each other is the last one: "'Is yours in the house?' 'Across the landing. Four steps. Yours?' 'She took the pillow.'" Give chapter 8 four lines of that kind, before the hall opens behind Lesca.

**7. The narrator tells us what Maylis wants, in the same sentence, about two dozen times. (Throughout: twenty-seven lines.)**
"She wanted that, and it surprised her how much." (2) "Maylis wanted him to be right." (7) "She wanted to tell her not to go." (8) "Maylis wanted to let go of the chair and run for it." (13)
For a woman who cannot say it, somebody has to. When the want contradicts the next thing she does, the sentence earns its place, and "She wanted it not to be her" is the best line in the book. Used at this rate the sentences become captions under a diagram, and they are where Maylis's inner life goes to be filed. Keep the ones followed by the opposite act (the wrist in 1, the duck in 1, Bayonne in 8, the dip in 13). Cut the ones the scene already shows: the rib stopping her laugh in 1, "Maylis wanted to see that. She wanted one day" in 6, Jeannot being right in 7, the fire door in 13.

**8. Lesca enters the book as a deduction. (Chapter 3, first section.)**
"So Lesca had a guess. Somewhere there was another Peyrebarthe, where the other lamp had come on."
Her first two pages are inference: the rib that is not hers, forty letters against twelve, the lamps, the guess, the distrust of the guess. She is working the premise out on the reader's behalf before she has done anything. She becomes a person a page later, at the stone, when she "read his name through to the last letter before she went in." Open on the frost and the stone. Let the rib and the forty letters arrive as intrusions during the committee, and cut the guess to two sentences on the ladder, where the rain already does the work.

**9. Josiane takes the bingo scene from the two women. (Chapter 7.)**
"'It's him she wants,' Josiane told the second row. 'You can go three weeks without bread. Without water it's three days. She's just told him which he is.'"
The scene's subject is Maylis choosing to starve the river by holding her own mouth shut, and "You're the water" is the first thing she has ever said to Lesca's face. It is used at once as the feed for a Josiane line. Josiane speaks eight times in the scene and it is one joke. Her theory pays beautifully in 16 ("I only had the one name wrong"), and it would pay as well from three lines here as from eight. Cut her to the coffee, the tooth and the wife. Leave a silence after "You're the water" before anyone misreads it.

**10. "Stay" costs her nothing. (Chapter 16.)**
"'Stay.' 'That's what you'd keep,' Coralie said."
It took Maylis eleven chapters to ask Coralie back across a landing, on a doorstep, with the bank over their heads, and the asking was the peak of her arc. Here she says the larger thing in one word, instantly, with no beat before it, and Coralie waves it off in a line. Two paragraphs later the book's question is asked "once more, with no box" as if it had not just been answered. Either take "Stay" out, so that the grave is the first thing she says she wants, or make it cost what the doorstep cost: one sentence of her not saying it first.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The owner keeps her right hand.** It tips the spoon, locks on the stair post, covers the eyes, pulls the handbrake, rings the timer to stop Larrère's question. It is how a woman with no voice gets one, and every scene built on it works.
2. **The other Coralie and the phone, chapter 10.** "'Why not?' 'Because you'd have told me.'" And: "I wanted you in the house, and I thought I could have you without it, if I kept a landing between us." Nothing in it should move.
3. **Jeannot from the six points to the shovel.** "And six. Bugger off." "It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you." "You won't get that down two metres with one arm, Lesca."
4. **Kévin's fingers.** "Five" in the yard, and then "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much."
5. **The dip, and the five words that sent her there.** "Go to the blind first." "She wanted it not to be her." The gift that is the fatal advice, and the one want the narrator had to say for her.
