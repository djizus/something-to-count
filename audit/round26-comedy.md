# Critic report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** The bill is paid in a person the reader liked: "'Forty-one,' Coralie said."
- **Tension: 7.5.** The last third is counted in rings and minutes and holds: "On the sixth ring the rib caught and held." The first two thirds lose it in the passages between scenes.
- **Progression: 7.** The days advance, but the nights repeat: "On Monday night she tried again. The rib let her have one breath, and then the arms of the chair were warm."
- **Comedy: 7.5.** Steady, dry, built from running jokes that change meaning each time: "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station." Half a mark under the comparison, for the reasons in section 2.
- **Characters: 8.** Coralie is the best thing in the book: "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them."
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.5.** The book's strongest point. The jokes are killed on the page, one by one: "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'"
- **Prose: 7.** Concrete and easy to read at its best ("It hit the floor like a full sack dropped off a trailer"), and it explains itself at its worst: "Maylis wanted her out, because every minute Lesca stayed was the river. Lesca wanted to stay."
- **The ending: 8.** It costs a death, a job and a marriage, and still ends on a joke that is in character: "It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you."
- **Clarity: 6.5.** Who is driving, which village, which night and what the thing was given to count all have to be carried at once: "Putting Lesca out at breakfast had taken her right hand and both of Coralie's, and it had lasted until nine minutes to three."

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. Would I press it on a friend? Yes, on the friend who liked The Good Place, with a warning about the bookkeeping between chapters 6 and 9.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Against The Good Place: half a mark below on comedy, above on the switch.**

- **Level: the flaw as the engine.** Maylis's "Either" is Chidi's indecision. The book runs it from a shopping pad ("Eggs is what Either gets in this house") to the platform ("'Either,' Maylis said. 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said") to the reckoning ("I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it"). Nine uses, and no two mean the same thing.
- **Above: the moral problem is funny and nobody gets a reset.** The Good Place staged the trolley problem as a simulation that could be run again. Here one woman pulled the lever knowing who was on the track, the other refused to pull it and ran for the one man, and the man was never in danger: "fetching a man out of a dry hole". Jeannot stays wrong in his own way to the last page: "I was in no danger, and I'll say so to anyone." The funniest old woman in the book finishes the prayer that drowns her. None of it is taken back.
- **Below: the leads, and late invention.** In The Good Place the four leads are funnier than anyone around them. Here the woman we follow is the straight man, and the book's own Good Place device, the room where "a woman can only say what's true", catches only Lesca. And after chapter 11 almost every smile is a callback. The Good Place was still inventing in its last episodes.

**Against Crouch: above, where the two meet.**

- Dark Matter's double takes the husband's place and the wife needs weeks. Here the plan is a council motion ("and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it") and the wife has it in ten seconds, by a pulse: "Who is that?" Then she says "I'd have this one again", which is funnier and crueller than anything in Crouch.
- Crouch's rules are in play within pages. This book is still stating rules in chapter 8, and states them as a ledger. That is the cost side.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Between the set pieces the book keeps a ledger, and the smile stops every time. (Chapters 5 to 9; structural.)**
"It was raining on Sunday the sixth of December, and the river gauge read forty under the line." "'Fifty-five under,' she said out loud, to the right hand. 'My file has the same.'" "At dusk the river gauge read a metre twenty under the line."
Counting is the book's best joke and its horror: Inguma's stars, Jeannot's points, the nineteenth turn, five fingers, forty-one. The hydrological figures bury it. I was asked to hold a metre and a half, a metre twenty, a metre, seventy, fifty-five, forty and ninety, with a date on each, and so the one figure that is a joke (seventy predicted, a metre read, Bordenave's cross) does not stand out. The repeated nights are logged the same way: pushed until four, pushed until four again, "tried again". This is where clarity drops and where the comic voice goes flat.
Do: keep three readings (the stall at a metre against seventy, the line on the eighth, ninety on the night). Cut the rest. Give the failed nights one sentence between them. On the timed walk keep "Twenty-five" and cut "Fifteen".

**2. The truth room catches one woman only. (Chapters 4, 8, 12; structural.)**
"I had that answer ready. It gives me somebody to blame, and it won't come out." "The two of them stood and listened to it not come."
This is the book's best comic device and it bites Lesca four times and Maylis never. Maylis is the one who lives on evasion ("It's the rib", "I was slow waking up", "Either"). She is never once stopped mid-sentence in that room. The people are meant to be wrong each in her own way, and the device tests only one way.
Do: in chapter 8, let Maylis begin to say that she will get both, and fail, and find that only "I want both" comes out whole. The text already has "Then I want both"; it does not show that the room refused her the other. Then "Both", said to Coralie down a phone where anything can be said, is a lie the reader has already seen fail.

**3. One gag, a dozen bouts, and the private ones are reruns. (Chapters 5, 7, 8.)**
"Lesca filled the spoon. Maylis brought her right hand across in its dish towel and tipped the spoon, and the soup went back into the bowl. Lesca filled it again. Maylis tipped it again." "She got the little finger up, and then the one next to it, and a sound came out through the gap."
One hand against the other is funny when a room misreads it: the timer in council, the jaw at the bar. In a kitchen with nobody to misread it, it is wrestling. The breakfast fight comes two pages after the landing and four after the bar, and it is the same fight on a smaller stage.
Do: one tip of the spoon. Cut the breakfast fight to its start and its end. Keep "they had one nose between them", "Not on a Sunday. I do this all week" and "Sixteen years, and that's the first time I've seen that hand hold on to anything". Cut the finger-prising between them.

**4. Maylis has a dry eye and the book takes it away when she is alone. (Chapters 7, 11, 13.)**
She can do it: "It sounded like something she was reading off a label." "It wasn't much, and she took more than that to the tip in a morning." But alone on the track and in the van she thinks in sums: "Three minutes to get there. Two to get him up the ladder. Three back." "Then the screen said 01:12, and she hadn't seen it change."
She digs the graves and cuts the verges and prices everything in trailers and mornings. That is her comedy, and it is absent from the long solitary stretches, so the woman we follow is the least funny person in the village for a third of the book.
Do: no jokes. Two or three of her measurements, in her own units, in the walk of chapter 7 and the vigil of chapter 11.

**5. Lesca reasons her way to what the reader already has. (Chapter 3.)**
"So Lesca had a guess. Somewhere there was another Peyrebarthe, where the other lamp had come on."
Coralie gave us this in chapter 1: "They shut you in a box once, to see whether there were two of you." Chapter 2 repeats it. Chapter 3 restarts the book in a new head and spends its first page deducing it a third time, and chapter 4 states it a fourth as three points. The first funny thing in chapter 3 is the kitchen timer, and it is a page later than it needs to be.
Do: cut from "None of it fitted" to "nothing to check it against". Keep the winch that isn't hers, the forty letters against twelve, and "he had been dead since the flood". Go to the frost and the stone.

**6. Chapter 16 is a roll-call. (Chapter 16.)**
Larrère and the date, Sarthou and the file, Madame Darrigrand and the petition, Kévin and the eels, Maïté and the suitcase, Josiane, the dealer, Marie-Jo and the chair, the oven, the pad, the envelope, the bandage, the pillow, the room, the priest, letter forty-two, the shovel. Sixteen things returned to their shelves. Each is well made. By the tenth I was nodding, not smiling, and one of them does not land at all: "It's the eighth this year. I've kept count too." The eighth what?
Do: cut the priest's count. Cut one stop from the first section (Sarthou's file or the pram, not both). The ones that earn the smile are Josiane, the dealer, the oven and Jeannot; clear room around them.

**7. The book laughs for the reader. (Chapters 7, 10.)**
"The second row laughed." "The second row laughed again." "The hall clapped him." "The secretary had her hand over her mouth."
Maylis's laugh catching on the rib is plot and stays. The second row is a laugh track, and it comes after Josiane's two best lines, which did not need it. "I'm going to sit on it" is funny until the secretary tells me so.
Do: cut the four.

**8. Jeannot's numbered points are spent before the one that matters. (Chapters 5, 7.)**
"One. Your mayor's thirty centimetres out. Two. If she can be out on Friday the fourth, she can be out on Friday the eleventh." Forty lines later in the same chapter: "One. It's Jeannot. Two. Jean's for the stone."
Seven numbered speeches in nine chapters, two of them in chapter 7. The shape should climb: three in chapter 1, "One. Those are my boots", three at the vote, six down the phone. The extra ones flatten the climb to "And six. Bugger off."
Do: take the numbers off the speech at the pad in chapter 7 and off the one in the café in chapter 5. Keep "Jean's for the stone".

**9. A punchline explained after it has landed. (Chapter 13.)**
"She knew whose kitchen. It was written on her screen." Then: "He was telling her that from the man's own house, at three in the morning, on the man's phone."
The darkest joke on Larrère is that he orders the gate shut to keep a man dry while standing in that man's kitchen with that man's wife. The screen says "Castagnet, house" and the clock ticks. The reader has it. The last sentence says it again and takes the sting out.
Do: cut that sentence. The same for "Maylis wanted her out, because every minute Lesca stayed was the river. Lesca wanted to stay" in chapter 12, in the middle of the fight for the wheel.

**10. One callback lets the fear out. (Chapter 13.)**
"'I put that handle in number six,' he said. 'I wrote to them too.' 'I know you did. They took note.'"
It is a good line. It is said on a doorstep with the water rising, straight after "There was nobody to carry him", and a man who has to be carried waits while two people do a routine about letters. It is the only place in the flood where a joke costs the scene anything.
Do: Jeannot gives back the key and goes in for Hourcade. "They took note" moves to the grave, where the letter already says "who had been written to about it".

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **"Either", every use of it, in this order.** "Eggs is what Either gets in this house", the gendarme, the platform, "Both", "Either, with the river in it". The platform most of all: the plot turns on the running joke, and it is credible.
2. **The oven door, three times.** "'In my tray,' she said, and shut the door on him." The third time she cannot finish it. Do not let her finish it.
3. **Bingo night and what it costs.** The hall misreading "You're the water", Madame Etcheto saying the rest of the words for her, "'That's till morning,' Madame Etcheto told Coralie, and settled her hands on the duck", and then room twelve. The kindest act in the book is the one that kills her, and nobody says so.
4. **The timer.** It cuts off Maïté's verb in chapter 3, it lets Larrère run for eleven minutes in chapter 6 ("Eleven years I've been timed like an egg"), the hand rings it on his question, Lesca turns it back to nothing on herself, and the verb turns out to be "Left".
5. **Jeannot, ungrateful to the end.** "And six. Bugger off." "It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you." If he thanks her, the ending is smaller.
