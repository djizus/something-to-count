# Round 26, Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else opened but the brief.

Counts below are mine, taken over the whole manuscript (about 43,000 words, about 4,000 sentences, 1,652 paragraphs, 83 sections).

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** Every item in the sum is a person the reader has met, and the last item shares her bed. "'Then which do you want?' Behind Coralie somebody was calling a name. 'The gate, or Castagnet? Say which, Maylis. For once.'" (13)
- **Tension: 7.** Chapters 9 to 13 are at 9. Chapters 3 and 6 are at 5. "On the sixth ring the rib caught and held. She had no breath to push with, and the phone went on ringing in a hand she couldn't close." (11)
- **Progression: 6.** The push-or-chair cycle is reported three times in summary before it changes anything. "On Monday night she tried again. The rib let her have one breath, and then the arms of the chair were warm." (08)
- **Comedy: 8.** The moral fault is the joke, and the joke is never smaller than the fault. "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (06)
- **Characters: 8.** Coralie is the best person in the book and is never described from outside. "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'" (07)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** The comic device of chapter 2 is the darkest beat of chapter 13, with no change of register. "'Five,' he said. He looked at the hand a moment longer. Then he put it under the woman's knees and lifted her in." (13)
- **Prose: 7.** Concrete, clean of filler, with images taken only from the village. It is held at 7 by one sentence shape, one paragraph size, and a narrator who repeats a plant at its payoff. Best: "He had gone grey. The man on the stone hadn't had the time." (05) Worst: "Lesca had no answer for him. Since 2014 her answer had been that no one came to tell him. / Lesca had nothing to put in the mouth, and Maylis took it from her." (05)
- **The ending: 8.** It costs one named death, the job, and the pillow, and nobody is forgiven. "'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much.'" (16)
- **Clarity: 6.** One verb covers two different acts, and the reader has to work out which one brings the river. "'Every night I cross, I bring a day of my flood with me.'" (08) By then "cross" has meant sitting in the chair, driving the other body, and carrying a pillow over a landing.

**Finished unpaid:** yes. I would have put it down for the night at chapter 3 and again in the first third of chapter 6. From chapter 9 I could not have stopped.

**Pressed on a friend:** yes, on the friend who liked The Good Place. Not on the friend who reads Crouch for speed, until chapter 3 is fixed.

## 2. Against the comparisons (prose)

**Level with Crouch, below The Good Place.**

Above Crouch:

- **The image stock.** Every comparison comes from inside Peyrebarthe: "like a full sack dropped off a trailer" (01), "like a bath emptying" (10), "timed like an egg" (06), "like a truck tipping out a load of gravel, a long way off" (13). Crouch's similes could belong to any book. These could belong only to this one.
- **Speech that needs no tag.** Jeannot numbers his grievances, Sarthou never contracts a verb, Larrère announces brevity through a gap in a window, Coralie reads people like charts. That is the Good Place rule that each person is wrong in their own grammar, and the book meets it.

Below Crouch:

- **Variable weight.** Crouch sets a one-line paragraph against a block of thought, and the line hits because of the block. Here the mean sentence is 10.8 words, ten sentences in the whole book run past 35 words, and 14 paragraphs of 1,652 run past 100 words. A one-line paragraph cannot hit when 47 per cent of paragraphs are under 20 words.
- **Speed of information.** Crouch gives the rule once, in the scene that needs it. This book gives the premise in summary (03), again on a ladder (03), and again as three points of order (04).

Below The Good Place:

- **Trust.** The series never tells the viewer that a callback is a callback. This narrator does, at nearly every payoff in chapters 12 to 14 (fault 2).

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

### 1. The motive at the dip is stated four times (13, 16). Structural.

The central choice is interpreted for the reader while it is being made, and then confessed twice more.

- 13: "Lesca had rung once and turned the handle, and Maylis thought she could do better than that. She wanted the man and the gate, both, and she wanted to be the one who got them."
- 13: "If she turned that handle, a man drowned, and her hand had done it. If she went on, whatever the river did behind her was the river's. / She wanted it not to be her."
- 16: "'I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me.'"
- 16: "'At the winch I thought I could do better than you,' she said. 'I drove past the handle to be the one who did.'"

Two motives, each given once by the narrator and once by Maylis. The spoken ones are acts: saying it aloud is the thing both women have failed to do for sixteen chapters, and it answers Lesca's confession in the square. The narrator's versions spend them in advance.

**Do:** in 13, cut "and Maylis thought she could do better than that. She wanted the man and the gate, both, and she wanted to be the one who got them." Cut "She wanted it not to be her." Keep the two "If" sentences, which are her reasoning and can pass for scruple. Then chapter 16 names that scruple as cowardice and vanity, in her own mouth, and the reader goes back over the dip with a new reading. That is the reversal the comparison is known for, and the book already has it written. It is buried under two sentences.

### 2. The narrator repeats the plant at the payoff (worst in 12, 13, 14). Structural, because it is a policy.

- 12: "Lesca had heard those three words once before, across a bar, and she hadn't believed them."
- 12: "Lesca had looked down that track once before, and that time she had reached for a phone. This time she kept the foot down."
- 12: "The hand locked on the brake was the hand that had held the stair post against her"
- 12: "She had told Maylis whose chair that was on Wednesday night, with her back to it."
- 13: "Her right hand was still locked on the handbrake, which she had pulled up to stop another woman driving her. Nobody was driving her now."
- 13: "At five on Friday morning she had let a phone ring nine times, and a man had answered it alive and sworn at her in six points."
- 13: "Coralie had asked her to say which, for once." This repeats a line spoken thirty lines earlier.
- 13: "Room twelve was the last room at that end, and at ten Coralie had walked Madame Etcheto back to it."
- 13: "She had stood in this corridor once before, with Coralie, outside the last door on the left."
- 14: "and she had said it to his widow across a council table, when his widow had asked her a different question."
- 14: "Since the night of the plastic bag she had spoken to Lesca only in council meetings, and then only to the wall."

Each of these stands between the reader and a thing the reader was about to remember unaided. The pleasure of a payoff is the remembering.

**Do:** adopt one rule. Nothing planted in the previous two chapters is recapped. A plant older than that is recapped only when the reader could not follow without it, as with "03:11, the minute on the stopped watch in Lesca's file" (13), which is ten chapters old and stays. Under that rule ten of the eleven sentences above are cut whole. Keep "This time she kept the foot down" and cut the sentence before it.

### 3. One sentence shape and one paragraph size (whole book). Structural.

The default sentence is two clauses joined by a comma and "and": 917 of them, one in four sentences. "She had repaired that bank every spring for six years, and she knew it well." (00) "The rest of the hand followed, and the thing got down and left the way it had come." (01) The median paragraph is 21 words. A joke, a rule of the premise, a line of backstory and a death all arrive in the same unit at the same speed.

The deadpan serves the comic scenes and should stay there. It fails in the stretches that carry information, where six paragraphs in a row are each a fact with a full stop: "She knew something else too. Jeannot Castagnet had sent the town hall forty registered letters, and the last one was about low water at his blind. She had no memory of counting them. / The Castagnet she knew had sent the town hall twelve. He was Jeannot at the café and Jean in the town hall's files, and he had been dead since the flood. / None of it fitted, and the hands in the cuffs of that raincoat were her own." (03)

The book proves the remedy itself. The only long paragraphs are Coralie's in chapter 10 ("The morning after... Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one.") and Jeannot's on the phone in chapter 9. They are the two best pages before the flood.

**Do:** no new writing. Remove paragraph breaks. Wherever three or more consecutive narration paragraphs have the same subject and no speech between them, run them together, starting with chapters 3, 6 and 8. Subordinate one "and" sentence in three ("Because she had repaired that bank every spring for six years, she knew..."). Then the one-line paragraphs left standing, "Twelve was shut." and "The last chair was empty.", stand alone because nothing else does.

### 4. Chapter 3 changes voice, from scene to file (03). Structural.

"Had" runs at 23 per thousand words in chapter 3 against 13 for the book. Twelve sections in 3,400 words, none long enough to be a scene. The narrator stops to file a note on each person as they enter:

- "In her first month as mayor, Lesca had brought in a rule that any member could speak for three minutes on any item. The rule applied to every member and had been needed for one."
- "She had been Lesca's deputy mayor until a night in 2018, when she handed her sash back across the council table in a plastic bag."
- "From the roof she could see the lake, where a gravel company had dug out the village's common pasture. Lesca had signed the lease."
- "So Lesca had a guess. Somewhere there was another Peyrebarthe, where the other lamp had come on."

The last is the premise, handed over as a summary by a narrator who then says "She didn't trust it." A Crouch reader has been given a monster on a wardrobe and a mouth that speaks by itself, and is now reading minutes.

**Do:** cut the two paragraphs from "So Lesca had a guess" to "nothing to check it against". The ladder ("It hadn't had it yet.") and the chair in chapter 4 do the same work in scene. Cut the Darrigrand doorstep section, whose content returns in 14 ("about the four houses"). Let the timer explain itself by ringing in the middle of "hydraulic", which it already does. The chapter loses about 400 words and the last line arrives while the reader still has speed.

### 5. In the possession chapters the accounting of names and limbs is louder than the scene (05, 07, 08, 12).

Chapter 5 uses "Maylis" or "Lesca" once every 40 words. Chapter 12 uses "hand" once every 64 words. "The left hand" and "the right hand" appear 93 times in the book. The point of view changes owner inside a single beat:

"Lesca meant to say Either. Maylis had given that word to that gap for eighteen years, and the window had already started back up to meet it. One word would let Lesca pass for her until noon, when the café was full. / Maylis felt the mouth get ready for the word, and stop." (05)

"Lesca had an answer for her. She pulled at the jaw, Maylis held it, and all Josiane got was a noise through the nose." (07)

The convention itself is sound and must stay: "the" for what the visitor drives, "her" for what the owner keeps. The fault is that the narrator also reports the visitor's intentions from inside her head, so every sentence needs a name to say whose head it is.

**Do:** give each scene one owner. In a scene seen from Maylis, Lesca exists only as what Maylis feels in the muscles: "Maylis felt her take a breath for it" (05) is the model, and it needs no second name. Cut "Lesca meant to say", "Lesca had an answer for her", "Lesca had the rest ready". The spoon scene and the coffee urn already work this way and are the funniest things in those chapters.

### 6. The narrator's afterword on a beat that has landed (01, 05, 06, 07, 11, 16).

- "In sixteen years Maylis had never got that laugh out of her. Lesca had got it in ten minutes, with Maylis's mouth." (05) The laugh, "with her head back", said it.
- "The letter would only cost her. The other sheet would cost Kévin." (06) He has just held the sheet up.
- "She had nobody to say it to and no mouth to say it with, and the one who had her mouth was the flood." (07)
- "Maylis hadn't meant to say it on a doorstep, with the bank over their heads. But she might not get another chance, and that frightened her more than asking did." (11) This sits between "Don't tell me now" and "Come back across the landing", the line the book has waited eleven chapters for.
- "She had been afraid of that date since the first of December. Now it was written in, and it was the smallest thing that had happened to her since Friday." (16)

**Do:** cut all five. Keep the afterwords that add a fact the scene could not show: "He had asked her twice now about a kitchen, and not once about a river." (06) "It would have gone up at three." (13) "Every December, in the council room behind her, Lesca had said forty houses and a care home, in less than a minute." (10)

### 7. Fifty-four of 83 sections open on a clock, a weekday or a date.

Chapter 5 opens its six sections: "at nine", "At eight", "at nine", "At noon", "At seven", "At five past nine". Dates are written out in full every time: "It was ten past six on Monday the thirtieth of November, with rain on the kitchen window." (05) "At eight on Friday morning, the fourth of December" (07) "By noon on Saturday the fifth of December" (07) "It was raining on Sunday the sixth of December" (08).

A countdown needs the reader to know how far the eleventh is. It does not need the reader to hold a diary. The sections that open on speech are the ones that start at speed: "'Hand,' Coralie said." (09) "'I'm heating the whole square,' Josiane said" (08) "'I said I'd never set foot in here,'" (10).

**Do:** one full date per chapter, placed where the count to the eleventh moves. Open every other section on the act or the line. Keep the rain and the frost, which tell the reader which village this is in three words.

### 8. The trailing "with" phrase (172 of them; over 6 per thousand words in 09, 12, 15, 16).

People are posed, then given a prop after a comma. Four in the first seven paragraphs of chapter 16:

"The door of room twelve was shut, with a strip of the gendarmes' tape across it." "Larrère's car was at the crossroads, stuck in silt up to the bottom of its doors, with the driver's door open." "Sarthou stood in the road outside the last house, in uniform, with his cap on." "Madame Darrigrand stood on the pavement beside a pram, with the baby inside her coat."

**Do:** in each run keep the one that carries the scene (the baby inside the coat, because the pram is full of silt) and give the others a verb or cut the prop. "Sarthou stood in the road outside the last house in his uniform and cap."

### 9. Reporting what people do not do (strongest in 12, at 4 per thousand words).

"Maylis didn't answer that." "She closed her hand and didn't give any of them." "Lesca didn't move." "They didn't open." "It didn't come." "he didn't look up from his numbers." "He didn't look up from his numbers, and she didn't stop." All in chapter 12. Across the book: "didn't" 66 times, "nobody" 61, "nothing" 51, and about thirty beats of the type "Lesca didn't answer" or "He said nothing".

The negative is this book's signature, because its subject is a woman who does not act. That is the reason to ration it. "Maylis didn't ask." (01) "She didn't say it a third time." (08) "She didn't correct her." (16) are decisions and carry the theme. "Lesca didn't answer." is a beat-filler that uses the same grammar and wears it out.

**Do:** keep the negative where the thing not done was a choice with a cost. Elsewhere give the positive act ("Lesca looked at the door") or cut the line. In chapter 12 keep "It didn't come", which is the absence of "Either" and the best negative in the book, and cut four of the other six.

### 10. Words carrying too many meanings, and slips of usage.

Overloaded:

- **gate** (118 uses): the sluice gate, the gate onto the platform, garden gates, field gates, the cemetery gate. In 11: "Maylis got out and unlocked the gate to the platform with the key on her belt" and, a paragraph later, "The gate had not moved yet." Call the platform's one a barrier, or let the padlock stand for it.
- **the mayor**: Larrère (01, 16), Lesca (06, 10, 14), and Lesca inside Maylis ("Your mayor's thirty centimetres out", 07; "Her mayor said seventy under", 08). In 16, straight after three Lesca chapters: "He let the mayor come to within a metre of him." Write "Larrère".
- **cross**: the chair, the driving, and the landing. Give the chair its own verb in 08, since that line states the rule of the book.
- **hand** as a measure: "a hand of water" is Jeannot's unit and should be his alone. The narrator also uses "a hand's width" (05, 09, 11), "the width of a hand" (02) and "lift the gate a hand" (08) in a book where "hand" appears 288 times.

Slips:

- "truck" (03, 10, 13) in a text that otherwise says wheelie bin, tarmac, bonnet, windscreen, pyjamas and tap.
- "cardboard folder" (04, 06, 08, 11) becomes "a buff folder" (16).
- "embankment" (00, 01, 03) and "bank" everywhere else, for the same thing in 00 and 01.
- Times in speech are written out ("Two fifty-one", "Twenty past three", "Eleven minutes past three") except "'At 02:57 she left it.'" (16).
- 15: "Marie-Jo turned the white kitchen timer to three and stood it beside the sash. / For eleven years she had watched it tick from the head of the table." "She" reads as Marie-Jo.
- 06: "She took hold of the edge of the desk with the left hand and shut her eyes." The convention gives "the eyes".
- 05: two consecutive paragraphs open "Lesca had no answer for him" and "Lesca had nothing to put in the mouth". It reads as two drafts of one beat. Keep the second.

- 08: "'I've worked out what I'd be giving up, and I've written it down. Nil.'" I did not know what had been written down or what "Nil" counted.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The old wing, 13**, from "Nine was open." to "There was a hatbox on it, and nothing else." and then "'Forty-one,' Coralie said." Four doors in four paragraphs, a pulse held "for the full half minute", a pencil line. No adjective, no comment. It is the best prose in the book and better than anything in the comparison.
2. **The fingers**: "'Five, and you're awake.'" (02), "'Five,' he said." (13), "'I wanted six, Maylis.'" (16). Three lines, eleven chapters apart, never explained.
3. **The four speaking voices**: Jeannot's "'And six. Bugger off.'", Sarthou's "'I do not record that I would have stayed.'", Larrère's "'I'll be brief'" through to the night he cannot finish it (15), and Coralie at the oven door, including the time she bends to it and cannot (16).
4. **The square, 14**, from "'I knew that he was,'" to "'Somebody has to stack them.'": the second loudspeaker saying "drowned him" late, the man who claps beyond it, the deputy turning the sheet over to see whether there is more on the back.
5. **The bedroom, 10**: "'Because you'd have told me.'" and "'The phone doesn't say how long it rang. It says not answered.'" The only place the prose lets a person talk for a whole paragraph, and the proof for fault 3.
