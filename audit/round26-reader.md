# Round 26 — Reader

Read cold, 00 to 16, in order, nothing else opened. Overall against the bar: 7.4. Chapters 9 to 16 meet it. Chapters 1 to 8 do not, and the reason is the amount of bookkeeping they ask of a reader before they pay.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.5.** "Ten minutes against twenty-five. You turn the handle first." One sum, stated once, and I carried it to the end without being reminded. It arrives in chapter 8; before that the stake is a date.
- **Tension: 7.** "On the sixth ring the rib caught and held." From chapter 9 I was afraid for a named person on every page. Before chapter 9 I was curious, which is a weaker thing, and in chapter 6 I was neither.
- **Progression: 7.** "The river had stood still for the two nights she kept Lesca out. It was raining now because Lesca was back." Every chapter changes something, but 7 and 8 change it by repeating the same night six times.
- **Comedy: 7.5.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station." I smiled through 4, 5, the council in 6, and the car at the café door in 8. I stopped smiling in the bingo hall, where four jokes run at once and none gets room.
- **Characters: 8.** "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." Coralie is the person I would miss. Jeannot second. The two Lescas are distinct from the first line each speaks.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.5.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" The same joke three times, and the third time it will not come out. Also "Here," from the wheelchair, in a flooded corridor.
- **Prose: 7.5.** "It sounded like a truck tipping out a load of gravel, a long way off, and the sound didn't stop." I never re-read a sentence because of how it was written. I re-read several because of what they assumed I already knew.
- **The ending: 8.** "I know what you picked. I was the other one." It costs a death, a job, a marriage and a mayor, and nobody is let off.
- **Clarity: 6.5.** "What broke it was the last day of Lesca's flood. That day was tonight's, and it would only come across if Lesca did." Then Lesca comes for eight rings, is sent home, and the bank breaks, and the book does not say which of those did it.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. Two sittings; the second was 9 to 16 without getting up.
**Press it on a friend?** Yes, with the sentence "it gets going in chapter 4", which is a sentence nobody says about Dark Matter.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, narrowly, on ease. Level on what it does to you once it has you. Above on what the ending costs.**

- A Crouch book states a rule once, in the open, and tests it in a small scene before the plot leans on it. This book has seven rules (the chair, the right hand, the jaw, the count, the room where only the truth comes out, the water, the empty place at the table). Six are taught the right way, most of them in the chapter 4 meeting, which does exactly what The Good Place does: teaches the rule as a joke. The seventh, the water, is the one the climax stands on, and it is the only one I had to infer from gauge readings.
- By page 50 of Dark Matter the reader is carrying one man, one wife, one want. By the end of chapter 3 here I was carrying two villages, fourteen named people, a town hall, an association, a committee, a lease, a stone and four separate counts of years. I managed. A reader on a train does not.
- The Good Place's best turn is that the thing you took for the torment was the design. "It's been doing my job" is that turn, and it re-read every night scene before it. Level.
- Dark Matter ends with the family in the car. This ends with a woman digging a grave one-armed for the person her choice killed, and the man she saved picking up the second shovel without thanking her. That costs more, and I believed it more.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Chapter 3 is where a reader stops, and it is the third chapter.**
"In her first month as mayor, Lesca had brought in a rule that any member could speak for three minutes on any item. The rule applied to every member and had been needed for one. Marie-Jo Labat turned a white kitchen timer to three and stood it in front of him. She had been Lesca's deputy mayor until a night in 2018, when she handed her sash back across the council table in a plastic bag."
Chapter 2 ends on the best hook in the first half ("Come back across"). Chapter 3 then goes back four nights and takes fourteen scenes to return to that sentence. In those scenes I am given a second village, a new name for the heroine, a committee, a three-minute rule, a resigned deputy, a widow, a stone, a debate about a verb, a roof, a gravel lake, a lease, a landing, an envelope, a file, a crossing, four demolished houses, a motion, a mother, a grandmother and a weighbridge. Every one of them is used later. I did not know that at the time, and I was tired by the Darrigrand doorstep.
What I would do: cut, not rebuild. The bank walk with Darrigrand goes (his four houses survive as one clause in Arlette's mouth in 14, where they already are). The lake seen from the roof goes; Arlette's kitchen introduces it better. The two paragraphs quoted above shrink to the timer and one line. The chapter's spine is: the afternoon that isn't hers, the stone, "It hadn't had it yet", the landing, the file, the crossing, the motion. That is seven scenes and it would read in half the time.

**2. The water rule is never stated so that I can use it, and the climax depends on it.**
Ch. 7: "The river had stood still for the two nights she kept Lesca out." Ch. 8: "Every night I cross, I bring a day of my flood with me." Ch. 11: "it would only come across if Lesca did." Ch. 12: "every minute Lesca stayed was the river." Ch. 13: "The patch had opened."
Four versions. By the first, two nights kept out should put the flood two days late; instead the gauge is back on the file by Saturday afternoon ("Fifty-five under. My file has the same"), so the two nights bought nothing and nobody says so. By the last two, the fight in the van is the fight for the Port; Maylis wins it, Lesca goes home at the eighth ring, and the bank goes anyway. Was eight rings enough? Did sending her home buy the six minutes between ten and sixteen? I could not tell, so I could not weigh what Maylis had lost by going under, and I read the first page of 13 trying to work it out instead of being frightened.
What I would do: one sentence at the gauge in 7 that says the river caught up in an afternoon, so keeping her out only counts on the last night. One sentence at the patch in 13 that says the day came over with her and did not go back when she did. And decide whether the six minutes are Maylis's, and say it; "It had held for sixteen" is waiting for that.

**3. Chapter 6: Maylis crosses for too little, at a price she knows.**
"Maylis wanted to see that. She wanted one day as the woman who signed things."
She leaves a nurse beside an open-eyed body with a note that says "Back at two". For the first time I liked her less, and I did not believe it of the woman who wrote *Either* to save Coralie an hour at the stove. And the flood clock stops for a chapter: she asks who can order the gate and is read what she already knows.
What I would do: keep the chapter, change the errand. She comes back with two things that matter — Larrère's order that night was "shut", and there is a question he has never been allowed to finish. Make one of those what she goes for (what did that man say on the night, over there, where it has already happened). Then the envy is what she finds when she gets there, which is truer and funnier, and the thirteen hours are a miscalculation instead of a carelessness.

**4. The nights in 7 and 8 are the same night six times.**
"She pushed until four again." "That night Maylis pushed. The rib gave her one breath and caught, and she was in the chair." "On Monday night she tried again. The rib let her have one breath, and then the arms of the chair were warm."
I skimmed them from the third. Only three matter: the two she wins (they prove the rule) and the Friday she loses. Also, on the Saturday of the bingo Lesca drives from six in the morning until midnight and "Inguma would come for Lesca tonight" — the rule from chapter 4 says it comes when it has finished what it was given, and I was never told what it was given that morning.
What I would do: cut Sunday and Monday nights to a clause inside the next chair scene. Give the Saturday count one line at six in the morning.

**5. The bingo hall has too many people talking for the reader to hear the one thing that matters.**
"'Two words,' Josiane told the second row. 'I got none for a coffee, and he gets two.'"
Eleven speakers. Running at once: the jaw, the urn, Daguerre's "Here", Sarthou's line, Josiane's theory (four turns), the duck, Jeannot's vow, Inguma under the tablecloths, "You're the water", the Lassalle sisters, Bordenave's spelling. Jeannot saying in front of the village that he will be in his blind on the eleventh is the plot; it gets three lines and a clap. I had to go back to find it.
What I would do: Josiane gets two turns, not four (keep "Without water it's three days"; lose the watch and "Two words"). Let Jeannot's vow be followed by one beat of quiet before anyone is funny again.

**6. Maylis, Maïté, Marie-Jo, Madame Etcheto. Jean, Jeannot, Josiane.**
"Maïté Castagnet, Jean's widow, held her handbag in front of her with both hands and looked at the radiator." Two paragraphs later: "Marie-Jo Labat turned a white kitchen timer to three."
Until chapter 6 I had to stop and think which one held the timer and which one was the widow. They are introduced in the same room in the same minute. And Marie-Jo is Kévin's mother, which is the whole reason for the plastic bag, and I was not told so until "My son's van came up that hill twice" in the last chapter.
What I would do: one of the three M names changes its first letter. Marie-Jo is named as Kévin's mother the first time she is on the page, and the plastic bag gets its cause in the same sentence in 6 where his six months are mentioned.

**7. The reason for the order that starts the book is never given.**
Ch. 1: "On Monday afternoon he had rung her and told her to raise the gate." Ch. 6: "The gate was raised by its keyholder, on an order. Was the order the mayor's, or the president's?"
That order cracks the rib, drains the blind, starts letter forty-one, and brings the gendarme. Why did Larrère give it? I assumed I would find out — to spite the letters, to empty a blind, to keep a man on the meadows or off them. I got to the end without knowing, and with what I learned about him and that house in between, I wanted to.
What I would do: one line, anywhere from 6 to 16. Jeannot would know, and he numbers his reasons.

**8. The book reads in places like a log.**
"It was raining on Sunday the sixth of December, and the river gauge read forty under the line."
Counting is the subject and I accept the price of that. But scene after scene opens on day, date, weather and gauge, and I was also holding eleven, twelve, sixteen and eighteen years, forty houses, forty-two beds, forty-one letters, nineteen turns, nineteen days, nineteen bags. By chapter 7 I had stopped reading the centimetres, so "seventy under" against "a metre" went past me until Bordenave drew the cross.
What I would do: the full date only where the date is the event (the twenty-fourth, the eighth, the eleventh). The gauge in somebody's mouth, never in the opening clause. The cross on the pad is the right way to tell me a number is wrong; do it that way each time.

**9. Chapter 1 asks me to decode two men before I know what a gate is.**
"'You didn't have to be so quick,' Larrère said to her. 'In my tray. I'll be brief. The winch.'"
After the prologue and the thing on the wardrobe I was reading fast, and here I stopped. Who is quick, what tray, what winch. The explanation comes in the next paragraph, after the line it explains. Then Jeannot arrives with "One… Two… Three", a blind, a hand of water and a gate raised with the river low, all needing translation at once. Both men are funny by chapter 5. On page four they are a puzzle.
What I would do: move the two sentences about Monday's order and the rib to before the window comes down. Give Jeannot's first speech one grievance and let him keep the numbering.

**10. A handful of lines I could not read, three of them in the last chapter.**
Ch. 16, Sarthou: "I do not record that I would have stayed." A reproach, or a kindness? Ch. 16, the priest: "It's the eighth this year. I've kept count too." The eighth what, and who else is counting? Ch. 5, Larrère: "And this morning the village's girl knows what I'm going to say on a Tuesday." At that point I had one clue to what frightens him (a folder under a loaf) and did not see why a prophecy about a river sent him to Maïté's kitchen; "He had asked her twice now about a kitchen, and not once about a river" comes a chapter late. Ch. 3: "The motion had one fault" — the fault is the twenty minutes, which is not in the motion. And Sarthou is a serving adjudant in one village and retired in the other, in the same year, with nothing said.
What I would do: a word each. Move the "kitchen, not a river" line into 5.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie doing Larrère through the oven door**, all three times (1, 8, 16). The third is the best single moment of tone in the book, and it only works because the first two are exactly as they are.
2. **Chapter 9 whole.** The night told flat, the reflector going out, and then nine rings and "And six. Bugger off." It is the hinge and it is the right length.
3. **Chapter 10, the bedroom.** "Because you'd have told me." "The phone doesn't say how long it rang. It says not answered." Nothing in it should be explained further than it is.
4. **Chapter 13 from the dip to "Forty-one"**, and with it "Twelve. One, two. A chair was pulled in to a table" at the top of 14. Including "Here," "Then they'll want the bins out," the five fingers at four o'clock, and the hatbox on the wardrobe.
5. **Either becoming Both.** "I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it." And the answer she finally gives, and the second shovel.

Also untouchable if there were a sixth: "I'll be brief." / He said nothing.
