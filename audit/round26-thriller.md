# Round 26 — Thriller

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. About 43,000 words.

Overall, through this lens: **7.5**. Level with Crouch from chapter 9 to chapter 13. Below him from chapter 3 to chapter 8. The bar is 8.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** Countable, and counted in front of the reader: "'There's no both. His hatch is twenty-five minutes from that handle. When water comes through the bank, the bank has ten. Ten minutes against twenty-five. You turn the handle first.'" (8)
- **Tension: 7.** At its best it is a nine: "On the sixth ring the rib caught and held. She had no breath to push with, and the phone went on ringing in a hand she couldn't close." (11) Through the middle it hangs on meetings: "'The association will be consulted,' Larrère said. 'At four. Here will do.'" (8)
- **Progression: 7.5.** Cause runs across chapters: "'Three. Her mayor said seventy under for Friday the fourth, and it read a metre. That's on the pad too, with a cross by it.'" (8) Two chapters stall it: 3 goes back four nights, 6 takes a day off.
- **Comedy: 7.5.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (6)
- **Characters: 8.** "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'" (7)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 7.** Clean in the flood: "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'" (13) Not clean at the reversal, where the gag gets the last word: "'She wants water,' one of the Lassalle sisters said." (7)
- **Prose: 7.5.** "It wasn't loud. It sounded like a truck tipping out a load of gravel, a long way off, and the sound didn't stop." (13)
- **The ending: 7.5.** It costs the right thing: "'I know what you picked. I was the other one.'" (16) It then takes 6,500 words to close its accounts.
- **Clarity: 7.** Scene by scene the reader always knows who is driving, where, and what time it is. The one rule the climax turns on is stated two ways: "'You came, and you brought it.'" and, a page later, "every minute Lesca stayed was the river." (12)

Three scores tie for lowest: tension, the switch, clarity.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. Chapters 2, 3 and 5 end hard enough to carry a reader across 4, 6 and 8, and from the last scene of 11 there is no place to stop.

**Pressed on a friend?** Yes, with one instruction: get to the end of chapter 5 before judging it.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch, on three things.**

1. *His double stays a predator to the last page.* In Dark Matter the other Jason has the wife and the house and keeps them until he is killed, and then there are a hundred more of him. This book makes the same promise in chapter 3 — "*That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*" — and pays it off in chapter 7, on the landing, with nine chapters to run. After that the double is put out of a body at breakfast with a hand over her mouth, and at the climax she leaves by announcing it: "'I'm letting go.'"
2. *He never goes backwards after a cliffhanger.* Chapter 2 ends on "'Come back across,' her mouth said. Maylis hadn't said it." Chapter 3 opens "Four nights earlier" and takes 3,400 words to get back to that hand.
3. *His rules are bought.* In Recursion each rule of the chair costs somebody a life or a timeline before the reader is allowed to rely on it. Here the rules are read out as agenda items ("There are three points, and I'll say them once") and the biggest one is certified by a room that cannot lie.

**Level with Crouch, on two.**

1. *Chapter endings.* Nine of sixteen are of his kind: one short line that changes who is in danger. "It rang once." "'Are you coming up?'" "LESCA, MAYLIS." "The arms of the chair were warm." "'Forty-one,' Coralie said."
2. *The clock.* The café's score pad is a better ticking device than the dated file alone: the prophecy is written down in public, checked in public, and its one miss loses the vote. Crouch does not have an instrument this good.

**Above Crouch, on one.** The cost. Dark Matter lets its man keep his family; Recursion gives the lovers a last meeting. This book has its heroine choose wrong, for a reason she can name — "She wanted it not to be her." — and takes the marriage for it.

**Against The Good Place.** Level on the moral machine: this is the trolley problem with a real lever on it, pulled in one village and not pulled in the other, and "Either" on the platform at five is as good as anything that show did with indecision. Below on staging the reversal. The show gives its reversal a silence, one speaker, and a room that has to hear it. This book's reversal — the rescuer is the flood, the thing on the chest is the guard — is shouted through clenched teeth at a bar and immediately misheard for a laugh.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. At the climax, the book has two rules for what brings the water, and uses both (12, 13)

> "'I asked you not to come,' Maylis said. 'You came, and you brought it.'"
>
> "Maylis wanted her out, because every minute Lesca stayed was the river."
>
> "The patch had opened."

From chapter 8 the rule is one night in the chair, one day of flood. Chapter 11 repeats it: "That day was tonight's, and it would only come across if Lesca did." By that rule the breach is delivered the moment Maylis goes under on the sixth ring, and the fight in the van cannot stop it. But the fight is argued on the other rule — minutes in the body — which the book has never set up, and Lesca surrenders to it ("You're the water"). Maylis wins her body back in two minutes, the bank goes anyway, and the text does not notice. She gets out at the patch and feels nothing about having just thrown the water out of herself.

The reader therefore does not know what the eight rings were for. It is the hinge of the plot.

**Do:** pick the first rule and say so. Cut "because every minute Lesca stayed was the river." Let Maylis fight for the wheel for the reason she gives in the steel room — "You're not sitting in that chair when the phone wakes me." Then add one beat at the patch in 13: she has put Lesca out, she expects the sheet of water to thin, and it does not. That is two or three sentences, and it turns a blur into the cruellest moment in the chapter: she has won, and it has changed nothing but whose hand it will be. It also starts the screen clock honestly: the brawl cost her from 02:51 to 02:53, and the reader should see those two minutes come off the ten.

### 2. The reversal is told as an inference, proved by an oracle, and staged under a joke (7, 8)

> "The river had stood still for the two nights she kept Lesca out. It was raining now because Lesca was back."

That is the book's turn, and it is two sentences of narration on a platform, resting on two dry days in a wet fortnight. The proof comes a chapter later from the room: "'Every night I cross, I bring a day of my flood with me.' She could say that, all of it, so she went on." A rule that stops a woman lying is here made to supply a fact she has no way of knowing. And the one time the line is said aloud, the hall answers: "'She wants water.'" "'She's got a bar full of coffee.'"

The planting is good: "We had a dry winter here in 2014", "'I don't know why it comes. That I can say.'", the cross on the pad. The turn itself is not dramatised.

**Do:** give it a second measurement, and use a scene already written. On Sunday (8) Lesca is put out at breakfast in front of Coralie. Let the rain on the kitchen window stop inside that scene, and let Coralie be the one who remarks on it. Then the reader has it twice, a witness has it once, and the room on Sunday night only has to refuse Lesca's lie ("I was going to tell you it was the tide") — it no longer has to certify the truth. In 7, cut the sentence "It was raining now because Lesca was back" and let the gauge and the first drops on dry planks do it. In the bingo hall, let "You're the water" land on one beat of quiet before Josiane.

### 3. Chapter 3 goes back four nights and sits in committee (3)

> "Four nights earlier, at nine minutes to three in the morning on Tuesday the twenty-fourth of November, Lesca, the mayor of Peyrebarthe, was awake in her own bed, and she couldn't move."

> "'Opened what? There's a name over it. It reads that she opened him.'"

The chapter that follows the book's first possession is the longest so far, restarts the week, and spends its first third on a council table where nothing is at risk. Its engine — "That village hadn't been spared its flood. It hadn't had it yet." — arrives 1,370 words in. This is the first place a Crouch reader puts the book down.

**Do:** cut, do not rebuild. Open on the frost and the stone. From the committee keep three things only: "no one came to tell him", "'I have a verb'" with the timer, and "'Did you know he was out?'". Drop Larrère's lesson on sluice gates and the Saved/Opened exchange. Drop the Darrigrand doorstep (the four houses are paid for again by Arlette in 14). Target: "It hadn't had it yet" inside the first 700 words, the chapter under 2,500.

### 4. The double surrenders in a sentence, and has stopped being feared four chapters earlier (8, 12)

> "'Not on a Sunday,' Coralie said. 'I do this all week.'"

> "'I'm letting go,' Lesca said, with the mouth."

The fear the book raised in 3 — that she will take the bed — is settled in 7 by Coralie. In 8 she says "This time I've come to stay" and is out before the coffee is cold. Maylis then describes the life she would be left with ("You eat at my table, and I tip the spoon") instead of living a day of it. So in the van the reader is not afraid Lesca will win; and when she lets go, the reason is one clause about a reflector.

**Do:** two small things. In 8, let the breakfast eviction fail for longer: give Lesca the morning — one scene in which the one-handed life is shown and Coralie prefers her — before the kitchen fight. In 12, make letting go cost seconds the reader can count. She has held the chair down for seven rings; let the eighth be long, and let what opens her hands be seen: the reflector, the hand on the brake that was the hand on the stair post. Those are in the text now, in one sentence. They need three.

### 5. The wall holding the gate shut through the middle is paper (6, 8, 11)

> "'With no date on it, it isn't a letter. It's a sheet of paper that I keep, and you're the one who puts the date on it.'"

> "It was the smallest thing that had happened to her since Friday." (16)

Every obstacle between Maylis and the handle is a document: an undated resignation, a blank sheet with a boy's name, a registered letter, a complaint form, a vote through a car window, Hontaube's insurance form, an orange warning. Each is funny. Together they mean that from chapter 6 to chapter 11 nothing that could hurt is in front of her, and the reader's fear has to be supplied by the date alone. The folder is shown three times doing the same job (office, café window, car seat).

**Do:** show the folder twice, not three times — cut it from the association vote in 8, where Jeannot's hand is enough to lose it. Cut the gendarme's complaint and the counter-blackmail over the nets from 6 to half their length; what must survive is "That's how Larrère asks for a thing. He's done it to you, and you've come in here and done it to me", because Jeannot's vote in 8 depends on it.

### 6. Maylis's one crossing has no errand (6)

> "Maylis wanted to see that. She wanted one day as the woman who signed things."

With ten days left she goes to the other village to look. She brings back nothing she uses on the bank: the statute she hears read is one Lesca told her in 4, and the back lane she had already seen at dusk. Every trip through Crouch's box is for something. This one costs thirteen hours and a pillow, which is right, but it is spent on tourism.

**Do:** give her one thing to fetch that the chair cannot hand her — Larrère's own account of his order that night, which is exactly what the council scene already delivers ("My order that night was to keep the gate shut. I gave it by phone"). Make that the stated reason she stays sitting: she wants to hear what he will say to her on the night, from his own mouth. One sentence changed in the dusk scene; the chapter then has a goal, a result, and a price.

### 7. The rule that could kill Lesca is waived (2, 8, 12, 14)

> "'The one it cannot take home dies in her own bed.'"

> "'The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies,' she said, in the voice she used for the prefecture."

> "'Bayonne has had you for three hours and found nothing.'"

Three chapters lay the rule. She sits on Inguma, sits in the chair of the dead, stands up with a line of procedure, and wakes with nothing wrong. The line is good. The exit is free.

**Do:** charge for it. She has a kernel between finger and thumb over her own number; her fingers lock on the seat. Let one of those not come back with her — the right hand that will not close on the pen at the ceremony, or the hospital finding one thing. One line in 14.

### 8. Three chapters of settlement, ten scenes in the last two (14, 15, 16)

> "'I said that house,' she said. 'I only had the one name wrong.'"

After "Forty-one" three questions still pull: will Lesca say it, will Coralie go, will the two meet again. Chapter 14 answers the first and holds. Then 15 and 16 close one account per scene — the old woman, the widow, the lover at the gate, the council, the landing; the letter, the gendarme, the neighbour, the eels, the bus, the café, the chair, the bag, the steel room, the grave. Fifteen per cent of the book comes after its climax. A reader who came for the flood has had it.

**Do:** cut scenes whole. In 16, drop the bus and Josiane, and Marie-Jo's chair (Jeannot's shovel does that work, later and better). Keep the dealer's "Had the key and went for a walk, did you?" by moving it to the scene where she buys bread, three lines. In 15, the council vote can lose everything but the timer and "Lesca, Maylis. Surname first." Do not cut Madame Etcheto at the minibus, Maïté's verb, or Madame Darrigrand.

### 9. On the day of the flood the book spends two scenes on a visitors' book and a roll of tape (10)

> "'Then write that I never voted for her to be in here.'"

Chapter 9 ends at ten past five on Friday morning with "*before dark*". The next 900 words are Arlette in the office and the young man from Pau. Both carry something — "I'm going to sit on it", and "The others have died since then, in those beds, of the things people die of" — but neither thread is in jeopardy, and the cutaway only works when it is.

**Do:** open 10 on Coralie in the bedroom doorway with the speech in her hand. Put "I'm going to sit on it" into the last scene of 10, said across the landing. Move the tape rehearsal to chapter 6, where Maylis in the mayor's body can be the one corrected about the front row, and trim it to the count from eight to forty-two.

### 10. The reader is a chapter ahead of her at the dip, and has been told the vigil cannot work (7, 11, 13)

> "'You'd sit there till Easter.'"

> "'The thing that sits on you doesn't need you asleep,' Kévin said. 'I was wide awake, both times.'"

The dip is shown under water in 7, warned of in 11, and recalled in 13 one paragraph before she drives into it. And Kévin tells her at half past six that staying awake is no defence, so the three hours in the van are waited through, not feared for.

**Do:** cut Kévin's two sentences in 11 — he said it in chapter 2, which is far enough back for the dent in the roof to be a shock. Cut "Jeannot had warned her about the dip, and at dusk she had waded through it" from 13: let her remember it when the bonnet goes down.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The prologue, and its return in 13.** "She put her hand flat on the bank beside the water, and the clay moved under her palm." "She was scared." "For three breaths." The same sentences, another woman, and the reader gets there before being told.
2. **The café pad as a chain of cause.** Written on the 30th, crossed on the 4th because Maylis kept Lesca out, read aloud on the 8th, and used by Jeannot to vote Shut. Her one success at resisting is what loses the daylight opening. Nothing else in the book is plotted this tightly.
3. **Five o'clock on the platform.** "'Either,' Maylis said. / 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. 'That's how the vote left it.'" At its present length, with the handle on and the man above ground. And Madame Darrigrand's bill for it in 16.
4. **Chapter 13 from the dip to "Forty-one".** The three steps back towards the lamp. "She wanted it not to be her." Sixteen minutes, not ten. The key thrown to Jeannot. The chairs she has to push while Coralie is behind the fire door. "'Don't tell me what he was.'" Kévin counting five in his own headlights. The pencil line through the last name.
5. **The rings.** Once in 2014. Nine at five on Friday morning. Six and then eight in the van. And the call log in 10: "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." It is the book's clock and its guilt in one object, and it explains the minute the thing has come at since page one.
