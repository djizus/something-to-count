# Round 27: Characters

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else opened.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Ten minutes against twenty-five." Once the sum exists the stake is exact and personal. Until chapter 8 it is a date in another village's file.
- **Tension: 7.** "On the sixth ring the rib caught and held." Chapters 11 to 13 do not let go. Chapters 7 and 8 repeat a night: "On Monday night the rib gave her one breath again."
- **Progression: 6.** "Only the last night counts." The rule is revised to reset the board, and the reader feels the hand that reset it.
- **Comedy: 7.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station."
- **Characters: 7.5.** "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." Coralie is an 8.5. The woman the book is named for by its narration, Maylis, is a 6.5 until chapter 9.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Then they'll want the bins out,' he said." In a corridor with water at the footrests, and it makes the corridor worse.
- **Prose: 7.** "There was a hatbox on it. Nothing else." The same plainness turns to formula when it has to report a feeling.
- **The ending: 6.5.** "I know what you picked. I was the other one." That line costs. Almost everything around it gives the cost back.
- **Clarity: 6.** "That bank goes tonight now, whether you stay or I put you out." Four chapters earlier the rule was "If I'm not, it holds." The reader redoes the rules at the climax.

Would I have finished it unpaid: yes, without stopping from chapter 3.
Would I press it on a friend: on the friend who watches The Good Place, yes. On the friend who reads Crouch, no.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Level with Crouch. Below The Good Place.**

- **Above Crouch on the partner.** Daniela in Dark Matter is what the Jasons fight over. Coralie finds the double in twenty seconds with two fingers ("Who is that?"), prefers her ("I'd have this one again"), and still turns her down on the landing for a reason that comes out of her trade. She also has a want that has nothing to do with either woman (Ithurbide's floor, "a torch and a list"). Crouch has no one this good.
- **Below Crouch on the inside of the lead.** Jason tells you what he stands to lose on every page. Maylis's love is deduced from a road cleared of leaves. That restraint pays once, in "She wanted it not to be her." It is paid for across a hundred pages in which the reader watches her and does not fear with her.
- **Below The Good Place on the pair and the ensemble.** Its people argue each other into change and come to like each other while doing it. Lesca and Maylis hold five meetings and never one conversation. Its ensemble starts as one fault each and deepens. Here Jeannot, Kévin, Maïté and Marie-Jo deepen. Josiane, Sarthou, Bordenave, Daguerre, Nadine and Bixente stay one tic each.

The lens questions, answered:

- **Distinct:** yes. One measures in trailers of leaves, the other in motions. "They were the same height. Neither of them liked it."
- **Wanted-for:** Lesca yes, from "and Coralie Mounet in it". Maylis only from chapter 6 ("I want that").
- **Softened or excused:** Lesca no ("I wouldn't have stopped"). Maylis yes, in chapter 16. See faults 1 and 2.
- **Described from outside as a woman:** no. Neither is given a face or a body beyond a rib and a palm. The village's "girl" and "little Lesca" for a woman of forty-three are the village's failing, and the book lets it stand as that.
- **Villagers a reader would miss:** Jeannot, Madame Etcheto, Kévin, Maïté. Then Arlette, who is not given enough to do.
- **Which relationship carries it:** Maylis and Coralie. It pays in loss, and the loss is right. The book then closes on Jeannot, who is the easier debt.
- **Does the reader care who lives:** Jeannot, Etcheto, and Coralie behind the fire door, yes. Lesca at the table of the dead, no.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The word that kills is a punchline, and the woman who said it never owns it. (11, 16)**

> 'Which is it going to be? Shut, or open?'
> The river went by under the boards.
> Her weight was on the handle.
> 'Either,' Maylis said.

This is the centre of the book. One word the other way and nobody dies in either village. By this point Maylis has said "Open" twice in a full café, named Larrère's car to his face, and started turning the handle with him watching. Kévin has just released her ("Don't keep a gate shut for me"). The scene gives no reason why the old word comes back now, to this man. The reader supplies one and moves on. In chapter 16 she defends the wrong moment ("It wasn't Either at the dip... I picked"), and 5 p.m. is named by Madame Darrigrand, who has had three lines in the book.

Do: write the platform beat from inside. She wants open. She has to say it to the face of the man whose hut it fills, the one man in the village who has ever been on her side. The word arrives the way the handle sticks at nineteen. Then in 16 let Maylis say "5 o'clock" herself, to Coralie, before Coralie can.

**2. Chapter 16 forgives her through everyone the reader likes. (16)**

> 'One,' she said, in the voice she called the numbers in. 'On its own. Sit down on it.'

Also: "You looked the other way for four winters, for nothing, so I can do a morning." "I do not record that I would have stayed." Letter forty-two. The second shovel. Against these stand a walk-on and an unnamed card dealer. Bordenave wrote the petition, signed it first, and lives under the bank, and he gets "Bordenave's pencil stayed on the pad." The one death is a woman of ninety-four who walked back to the room herself, and Coralie takes a share of it aloud ("That part is mine"). Lesca's dead man gets a stone and twelve years. Maylis's gets a queue of absolvers inside forty-eight hours.

Do: keep Jeannot's letter and the shovel, which are earned and do not thank her. Cut Marie-Jo's chair or turn it. Give the charge to Bordenave, in the café, on the pad, under her heading, and let nobody answer it.

**3. The two women never meet as people. (4, 8)**

> 'The first item is how this works. There are three points, and I'll say them once.'

Every chair scene is spent on rules or terms. These two share a father, a hand, a house and a Coralie, and neither asks the other one question about her life until "Is yours in the house?" on the last night. The scene the book is built for is seeded and dropped in one line: "I gave that word up when I was thirty-one, and I won't have my own face say it to me." Lesca was Maylis until the gate. She is looking at the woman she was the day before she drowned a man, and the book gives that a subordinate clause.

Do: take the second chair scene in 8, which restates dates the reader already has, and give its space to that recognition. Lesca despising the word because she remembers saying it. Maylis seeing what not saying it cost.

**4. Lesca's two turns both happen in chapter 12, each in under a page, each without a cause on the page. (8, 12)**

> At the end of the headlights the reflector was red, and no nearer than it had been twelve years ago.
> 'I'm letting go,' Lesca said, with the mouth.

A ring earlier she "could hold it down for this one". What changes is that fighting for the wheel is repeating the night, but the reader gets that on a second reading. Then the seat among the dead. In 8 she had costed dying over there at "Nil." In 12 she sends apologies and walks out, and nothing says why her life is suddenly worth a ceremony. The cause is there (she says "I rang once" to Jean and he marks his card, so the dead will not hear it and the living must) but it is not allowed to land.

Do: one beat at the brake where she sees that her hand on the wheel is what is keeping the van from him. One beat at the table where his not looking up is what stands her up.

**5. Larrère carries the blame off both women, and his one real reason is a clock ticking behind a phone call. (13, 15)**

> 'He's in his hut, yes. He stays dry as long as you do what you're told.'

For fifteen chapters he is a window, a phrase and a folder. Then: "You knew he was out there, Guy." On the evening of Lesca's confession the reader's anger is handed a better target. The interesting fact is buried: in both villages his order to keep the gate shut is the order that keeps his mistress's husband alive, at the Port's expense. That is Maylis's own choice at the dip, made by the man she despises.

Do: let him say it to her, on the bank on Friday, in plain words. Then chapter 15's gate scene stops excusing Lesca and starts indicting the three of them.

**6. Through chapters 5 to 8, everything Maylis does is a refusal. (5, 7, 8)**

> Maylis brought her right hand across in its dish towel and tipped the spoon.

She tips the spoon, grips the stair post, shuts the tap, clamps the jaw, pulls the wrist away. Lesca ticks a box, answers five questions and holds a hand. Coralie's "I'd have this one again" is the reader's opinion too, and the book needs the reader torn, not agreeing.

Do: give the right hand one act towards Coralie while Lesca has the mouth. One, before the landing scene in 7, so that "That's her. In this hand." has something more than a pulling-away to recognise.

**7. Maylis never looks at Lesca. (4)**

> She sat much straighter than Maylis did. There was nothing wrong with her ribs.

That is the whole description of meeting yourself. Posture and a rib. What have twelve years of being obeyed done to the same face? What does Lesca see in a face that has been told "little Lesca" since she was twenty-five? A book about two versions of one person owes the reader one look each way. It is also where the reader would learn that these women are forty-three, which nothing says until the arithmetic of "thirty-one".

Do: four sentences in chapter 4, each seeing in the other the thing she envies and the thing she would not take.

**8. Sixteen years is a number. (1, 4, 5, 7, 8, 16)**

> 'Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you, and that was me.'

The number is spoken at least eight times. The years themselves get one memory of four lines (the chairs behind the tent) and three reported facts (the cleared hill, the shopping, the lamp). The reader knows what Coralie has gone without. The reader does not know what she got, so "I wanted you in the house" in the other village is felt more than anything in this one.

Do: one more memory with pleasure in it, in chapter 9 beside "Duck", before the doorstep in 11.

**9. Arlette is missing from the end of Maylis's story. (8, 16)**

> 'I'd have liked to hear the second one. I'd have missed the first, looking for my purse.'

That is her only scene in Maylis's village. In Lesca's she has three, and each one bites ("I've voted for Larrère against her three times"). Maylis loses her job, her key and Coralie across a yard from her mother, and her mother does not appear. The father is invoked by Larrère, Jeannot and Etcheto and sits at the table of the dead. The mother, who is alive, is not asked.

Do: give Arlette the chair scene that Marie-Jo has in 16, or one exchange across the yard on the Sunday.

**10. Tics run past their use and start doing other work. (7, 8)**

> 'One. A week ago this one tells me what's under my own floorboards... Two. Today she wants my hut filled to the trapdoor... Three. Her mayor said seventy under for Friday the 4th...'

Jeannot's counting is a grievance engine and a joy in 1, 4 and 9. In 8 it is a recap of three chapters for the reader's benefit. Josiane addresses "the second row" four times in one scene in 7 with the same syllogism, and Larrère borrows it in 6. She is never anything but that mouth.

Do: in 8 give Jeannot his vote in one point. In 7 keep "Without water it's three days" and cut two of the other three.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The landing, chapter 7.** From "Which of you is that?" to "You'll go back down those stairs." Coralie chooses, and for a reason only she would have.
2. **The phone, chapter 10.** "Because you'd have told me." And "I wanted you in the house, and I thought I could have you without it, if I kept a landing between us."
3. **The care home, chapter 13, from "Where's Coralie?" to "Forty-one."** Maylis pushing strangers across a yard while the one she wants is behind the fire door is the best characterisation of her in the book. The wrist held "for the full half minute". The hatbox.
4. **Kévin's fingers.** "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much." / "It was five." Also "Nobody had asked him to come", both times, and his cap in the other village.
5. **The names.** The narration's Maylis and Lesca; Arlette's "Her mother had never called her anything else"; Marie-Jo's "Maylis" on the stage; "Lesca, Maylis. Surname first."; "Good night, Maylis." She earns the first name back, and nobody says so.
