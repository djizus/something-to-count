# Critic's report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

Verdict in one line: the comedy is good and stands half a point under the bar. It is level with The Good Place at the one thing that matters most, which is turning a moral flaw into a running joke and then into the catastrophe, and below it in range, because nearly every laugh comes from one device: a person's fixed phrase, repeated.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** "Then his death is mine as well, and I've stood beside his widow for eleven Decembers." One man, forty houses, one woman on nights, and a bed with two pillows. All four are in play by ch. 5.
- **Tension: 7.** "On the sixth ring the rib caught and held." That is the level of ch. 9 to 13. Ch. 7 and 8 sit below it: "For one breath, Maylis had won" is a win the next chapter cancels.
- **Progression: 6.5.** "The two nights you kept me out bought you nothing, because the river made them up." The book tells the reader that a chapter and a half of struggle did not count.
- **Comedy: 7.5.** "Eleven years I've been timed like an egg. I have sentences in that register with no ends on them. […] I'm told I wave." I smiled from the oven door in ch. 1 to the platform in ch. 11, with one gap in ch. 8, and again on the last page.
- **Characters: 8.** "I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it." Coralie is the best company in the book. Maylis is the least funny person in it.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.5.** "A man had just sworn at her in six points, and he was alive." The joke and the dread are the same sentence. This is the book's best skill.
- **Prose: 7.5.** "She let go of the wrist to catch the wheel. That freed the right hand." Plain and well timed in dialogue; in the two-in-one-body scenes it turns into an inventory of hands.
- **The ending: 8.5.** "It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you. I was in no danger, and I'll say so to anyone." It costs a life, a job, a marriage and a good name, and the last beat is still Jeannot being Jeannot.
- **Clarity: 6.5.** "'Fifty-five under,' she said out loud, to the right hand. 'My file has the same.'" Eight river readings, five rules for the chair, two of everybody. I followed it, with effort, and effort is the enemy of a smile.

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. From ch. 9 I could not have stopped.

Would I press it on a friend? Yes, with the words "get past chapter 8".

## 2. Against the comparisons

Against Crouch there is nothing to compare: he is not funny and this is. A Crouch reader gets the comedy free.

Against The Good Place: **just below.**

**Level: the flaw is the joke, then the joke is the disaster.** The Good Place makes Chidi's indecision funny for three seasons and makes it the reason he is damned. This book does the same with one word. "I don't cook Either. There's no recipe for it." "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station." Then, with her weight on the handle and Jeannot asking "Shut, or open?": "'Either,' Maylis said. 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. 'That's how the vote left it.'" The same word, the same rhythm, and a woman of ninety-four dies of it. The moral problem is not made small. It is made of the same stuff as the jokes.

**Level: everybody is wrong in their own way, and every wrongness carries weight.** Larrère will not sign. Jeannot will not be told. Coralie will not wake a woman for orange. Madame Etcheto will not sleep in a chair. The woman at Hontaube has a form. Josiane has the right house and the wrong name. None of them is a fool, each is funny, and the flood needs all of them.

**Below: one device.** The Good Place gets laughs from wordplay, sight gags, status reversals, absurd rules, and people surprising themselves. Here almost every laugh is a catchphrase coming round again: "I'll be brief", "One. Two.", "Here", "that house", "through the chair", "for the minutes". The pay-offs are superb. Larrère at the garden gate saying "I'll be brief" and then nothing is worth every earlier use. But between ch. 5 and ch. 8 the reader hears the mechanism before the line.

**Below: the reversal is read out.** The Good Place delivers its reversal as its biggest laugh. This book has a reversal of the same kind, one that re-reads everything before it: the thing on the chest was never the monster, it was the doorman. "For twelve years I thought it was sitting on me. Over here it's been putting me out." "It's been doing my job." It arrives at the end of a recital of dates with no joke in it.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Ch. 8, the steel room: the book's central reversal is delivered as minutes of a meeting, and it cancels the scenes before it.**

"I'll open with the dates. I spent today at my desk, with my file on one side and your river on the other."

Three things are wrong. First, the reader already has it. Ch. 7 gave it twice: "It was raining now because Lesca was back" and, at the bar, "You're the water", which the hall mishears as an order for a drink. Ch. 8 then explains it for twenty-five lines. Second, there is no joke in those lines, and this is the one place in the first eleven chapters where I stopped smiling. Third, "bought you nothing" and "Only the last night counts" tell the reader that Maylis's two nights of pushing and her fight at the bingo bar were for nothing. The funniest set piece in the book ends on "That was one more day for the river", and a chapter later that day is refunded.

What I would do: use the rule ch. 4 already set up. In that room a woman can only say what is true, and ch. 4 showed it stopping Lesca mid-blame. Let her try to chair her way round the truth and have the room shut her mouth on each evasion, until the only sentence that comes out whole is the concession: "You were right." Cut the dates. Keep the doorman exchange as the turn. State the last-night rule in one line and do not say the earlier nights bought nothing. This adds no new machinery and removes a page.

**2. Ch. 5 to 8: one phrase per person, fired on every entrance.**

"One. A week ago this one tells me what's under my own floorboards, and what she'll do about it if I don't withdraw a complaint. I withdrew it. Two. Today she wants my hut filled…" (ch. 8)

Jeannot counts his points seven times before ch. 9: ch. 1, ch. 4, ch. 5, twice in ch. 7, ch. 8, then the six-pointer. In ch. 8 the numbers are carrying a recap of the plot. Josiane states her theory four times in the bingo scene alone. Monsieur Daguerre says "Here" in ch. 7 twice, ch. 11, ch. 13 and ch. 14, and is reported saying it in ch. 10.

What I would do: ration. Drop the numbering from Jeannot's café speech in ch. 7 and his vote in ch. 8, so the six points at 5 in the morning land on a device heard four times, not seven. Give Josiane two turns at bingo: "in case of what comes out" and the bread-and-water line. Cut the second "Here" in ch. 7. Do not touch any use of "I'll be brief"; each one is different.

**3. Ch. 7 into ch. 8: three contests for one body in a row, narrated as an inventory.**

"Maylis got them shut again behind it." "Maylis shut the teeth on the rest." "She got the teeth shut again behind it." (ch. 7)

The bar, the landing and the breakfast table follow each other with no scene between them, and each is the same contest: who has the jaw, who has the left hand. The kitchen is the funniest of the three ("They had one nose between them, so now neither of them could breathe." "Not on a Sunday. I do this all week."). The bar is the longest, and its comedy is in the hall's misreadings, not in the teeth. Ch. 12 does it a fourth time in the van, where it is meant to frighten, and by then the reader has learned to read hands as slapstick.

What I would do: at the bar, keep the coffee running over Josiane's shoes, "Tooth", and "You're the water"; cut every other report of the jaw changing hands. Let the kitchen be the one full wrestle. In ch. 12 cut the choreography by a third so that the eyes being covered and the handbrake are the only two moves the reader has to picture.

**4. Ch. 8, the oven: the second telling repeats the first, and Maylis never plays.**

"'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice from the back of the throat. 'The soup will be consulted. Those in favour.'"

This is ch. 1 with new nouns. It is there so that ch. 16 can break it, and a second use is needed for that. But a second use should climb. It also shows the larger problem: in sixteen chapters Maylis laughs at Coralie's jokes eight times and makes Coralie laugh once. Ch. 6 proves she can be funny ("Maylis had never kept anybody waiting in her life, so she took her time over it"; "I want that"), but only when she is in another woman's body. In her own kitchen she is an audience. A reader should see what Coralie has stayed sixteen years for.

What I would do: in ch. 8 let Maylis answer the oven, one line in Larrère's voice, the only time she joins in. It costs a sentence. It makes the middle use climb, it gives Coralie something to lose, and in ch. 16, when Coralie bends to the gap and cannot finish, there is somebody in the room who once answered.

**5. Ch. 1, 7: the page tells me people laughed.**

"The second row laughed." "The second row laughed again." "The hall clapped him." "People laughed."

A line is funny or it is not. Josiane's "She's just told him which he is" is funny. The report that the second row agreed is a laugh track, and it stops my own.

What I would do: cut all four. Keep the laughs that are events: Maylis's laugh hitting the rib, Coralie looking across the table for the other half of hers.

**6. Ch. 4, 13, 16: the meaning is stated after the scene has shown it.**

"It had always come when she needed it. It had never been true, and it wasn't true now. She did want one of them, badly." (ch. 4)

This paragraph explains why the word sticks, four lines before "Tell me it's Tuesday" demonstrates it far better. In ch. 13, "She wanted it not to be her" is said once and is enough; ch. 16 says it again to Kévin ("I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me") and a third time to Lesca. Coralie's version, "That was Either, with the river in it", is the complete statement and the only funny one. Kévin's "Five" in the yard needs no gloss; "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much" gives it one.

What I would do: cut the ch. 4 paragraph. Cut Maylis's line to Kévin in ch. 16. Stop Kévin at "I got five, and I counted them again."

**7. Ch. 6, the minute of silence: the best comic idea in the book is over in eight lines.**

"The laugh started low down. Her own rib would have stopped it. This body had nothing to stop it with."

A woman in a mayor's body, asked to mourn a man she knows only as a living nuisance, can think of nothing but his bill for two wheel tracks, and the body she has borrowed lacks the injury that would have saved her. This is the premise paying out as pure comedy. It gets a paragraph, and then Maïté ends it. The timer rings after she has left, when it should ring on the laugh.

What I would do: give it the whole minute. The timer ticking, Larrère's hat on his chest, the attempt to picture a drowned man, letter seventeen arriving instead, the right hand over the mouth, the nose. Let Maïté speak into the bell.

**8. Ch. 15, the wheelchair: the funniest old woman in the book is given an explanation and takes it like a clerk.**

"There are two of me, Madame Etcheto, and the other one has a village, with a room twelve at the end under the bank."

This is the woman who reported a care assistant because Inguma "had been told twice and given two lots of stars, and it got through them early and came at 1". She is told that she drowned in another village and she answers with a deduction. The scene is neither funny nor frightening. Only its last line is alive: "Nobody emptied hers."

What I would do: let her answer in character. She would have a view on the procedure: who says the words for the other one now, and whether the other one got her duck home. Keep the last line. Cut Lesca's sentence of explanation to "the other one has a room twelve".

**9. 00 and the first page of ch. 1: six hundred words before the book says what kind of book it is.**

"She was scared."

The opening is a straight flood thriller, then a straight night terror. The first line that sounds like this book is forty lines into ch. 1: "They opened the door, counted one of her, paid her, gave her a sandwich and went back to their university." A reader who picks it up for the comedy has no sign of it on the first page; a reader who picks it up for the dread meets a woman talking into an oven in the voice of the mayor.

What I would do: one line in 00 in the dry working voice the book owns. Ch. 11 has it: "All that kept the river off them was a bank that cost the village four wheelbarrows of clay a year." Move that register, not that sentence, to the first page.

**10. Ch. 5, 7, 8: the river is read to the centimetre eight times.**

"A metre and a half under the line." "Seventy under." "It's a metre." "Fifty-five under." "Forty under the line."

Bordenave's pad needs two numbers: the one the mayor gets wrong and the one she gets right. The climax needs "ninety over". The rest is arithmetic I skimmed, and ch. 7 has its two best jokes buried between readings.

What I would do: keep the pad's two and the ninety. Everywhere else, the river is under the line, at it, or over it.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **"Either", every use of it.** The shopping pad, "Eggs is what Either gets in this house", the word sticking in the truth room, Sarthou's "I cannot summon Either to the station", the platform, and Coralie's "I've cooked it". The count is right and each use means something new.
2. **Larrère's window and "I'll be brief".** The gap of "a few centimetres, which was as far as it went for anybody", the glass going all the way down at his first name, the association chaired from a running car ("We can't shut the door. The president's on the other side of it"), "I'm told I wave", the garden gate where he says it and says nothing, and the oven door Coralie cannot finish.
3. **Jeannot at 5 in the morning, and letter forty-two.** "And six. Bugger off." followed by "he was alive". Then the last scene: a letter that is not a complaint, "doesn't thank you", and a second shovel.
4. **The eight strips of tape in ch. 10.** "'Eight,' the young man said. 'There were forty-two in their beds that night. The others have died since then, in those beds, of the things people die of.' He stopped counting." The cleanest turn from comic to grave in the book. With it, the stone's verb: "It reads that she opened him", paid off four chapters later as "Left".
5. **The jokes that walk into the flood unchanged.** "'Here,' he said. 'I know you are.'" "Then they'll want the bins out." Kévin spreading his fingers in his own headlights: "Five." None of them weakens ch. 13. They are why ch. 13 hurts.
