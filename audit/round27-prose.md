# Critic's report: prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. 43,020 words, 4,446 sentences.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "All that kept the river off them was a bank that cost the village four wheelbarrows of clay a year." (11) The stakes are concrete and local. The price finally paid is one woman of ninety-four, a job and a pillow, which is exact but small beside what the bank scene promises.
- **Tension: 7.** "On the sixth ring the rib caught and held. She had no breath to push with." (11) Chapters 11 to 13 hold. Chapters 5 to 8 spend their tension on the same wrestle for a jaw.
- **Progression: 6.** "It had taken her right hand and both of Coralie's to put Lesca out at breakfast, and it had lasted until 2:51." (8) The book says it itself: the middle wins a round and is back where it started by night. Four chapters run the same contest.
- **Comedy: 7.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (6) The smile holds through 1, 3, 5, 6 and the bingo night. It stops in 8.
- **Characters: 7.5.** "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'" (7) Coralie, Maïté, Jeannot and Sarthou each have a sentence nobody else could say. Lesca and Maylis are distinct in what they do and too alike in how they sum up.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Then they'll want the bins out,' he said." (13) The flood is carried by the people the bingo night made funny, and neither register spoils the other.
- **Prose: 6.5.** "Mud to the ankle at every step. The torch in her left hand, her right arm pressed across her side." (13) Clean, physical, free of filler, and written at one pulse from the eggs to the drowning.
- **The ending: 7.5.** "Then he stepped over the string in his rubber boots and began to dig." (16) The last image is right. The three scenes before it explain the book to the reader.
- **Clarity: 6.** "The warmth had got up to go to it, but Lesca had stayed sitting." (3) The mechanism of the crossing is given in a sentence that has to be read twice, and the shared-body scenes lose track of which woman "she" is.

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. Would I press it on a friend? I would lend it to one who likes village comedy. I would not press it on a Crouch reader.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, on prose.** Three things put it there, and two keep it close.

1. **Crouch has two gears and this book has one.** A Crouch crisis is set in one-line paragraphs and fragments against a base of longer running sentences, so the page itself speeds up. Here the base is already the crisis gear. The mean sentence is under ten words in thirteen of the sixteen numbered chapters, and no chapter rises above 11.5. Between 26 and 45 per cent of sentences in every chapter are six words or fewer. Sentences of 25 words or more are 0 to 3 per cent. Chapter 13, the flood, measures 9.6 words and 39 per cent short. Chapter 1, the eggs, measures 10.1 and 40. When the bank breaks the prose has nowhere to go.
2. **The Good Place gives each person their own way of being wrong and their own syntax for it.** Here the narrator, Lesca, Coralie, Arlette, Jeannot and Josiane all close a thought with the same balanced pair (fault 6). The wit is one wit.
3. **Crouch never tells a reversal twice.** This book's reversal, that Lesca is the flood, is given in chapter 7 and then given again as a dated memo in chapter 8 (fault 2).

Level: chapter endings. "'Come back across,' her mouth said. Maylis hadn't said it." (2) and "The arms of the chair were warm." (11) are as good as his. Level also with The Good Place in making the rules a joke: the steel room run as a council meeting with nobody in the chair.

Above Crouch: there is no generic sentence in it. No filler, no hedges, almost no adverbs, no decorative dashes. Every comparison comes from the woman's work. Line by line it is cleaner than he is.

One fact outside the sentences: at 43,000 words this is half the length of a Crouch novel, and the second village pays for it (fault 4).

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. One pulse for the whole book (all chapters, worst in 13)

> "She got out into water that came over her boots. The gate was six hundred metres behind her. The red reflector was four hundred metres in front. The bank had six minutes left."

Four sentences of eight to ten words, subject first, each a measurement. That is how the shopping pad is described in chapter 1. The climax has no acceleration left except dropping the verb ("Torch up. Right arm across her side."), and the base was clipped already. 17 per cent of sentences open on "She" or a woman's name, 12 per cent on "The". Chapter 14 is the one place the sentences lengthen (mean 11.5, 26 per cent short), and it is the best-written chapter: "She watched her sentence travel back through the square and down the road, row by row, at walking pace."

**Do:** give the book a second gear. Let the sentences run in the three stretches of continuous motion: the flood night in 9, the wade down the Port and the corridor in 13, the drive in 12. Join the measurements into the movement that takes them. Then the short sentence at "She wanted it not to be her" lands as a stop. Leave the domestic chapters as they are.

### 2. The reversal is told twice and its rule three more times (7, 8, 11, 12)

> "For the two nights she had kept Lesca out, the river had stood still. It was raining now because Lesca was back." (7)

> "'I'll open with the dates,' she said. 'I spent today at my desk, with my file on one side and your river on the other.'" (8)

The reader has the turn on the platform in two sentences, and has it again at the bar in three words. Chapter 8 then spends two hundred words walking the same dates to the same conclusion and ends on "You were right." It is the longest block of exposition in the book and it carries nothing new until its last line. After that the rule that only Friday counts is stated in 8 ("Only the last night counts"), restated in 11 ("Once she was in, it went, and nothing done to her afterwards would stop it") and restated in 12 ("That bank goes tonight now, whether you stay or I put you out").

**Do:** cut Lesca's speech in 8 to what she alone can add: that for twelve years Inguma has been putting her out, and that only the last night counts. State the Friday rule there once. Cut the restatement in 11 and let Maylis's line in 12 stand as its only echo.

### 3. Two heads in one scene (5, 7; a third in 6)

> "He was arguing with her. She wanted him to go on." (5)

Which woman? Both are in the body, and the paragraph before was in Lesca's head and the paragraph after it is in the room's. The café scene in 5 goes Lesca ("She had carried his forty letters in her head"), Maylis ("Maylis could feel the face smiling at him"), Lesca ("Lesca saw that it wasn't her he was looking at any more"), and the section ends in Maylis. The car-window scene does it inside one paragraph: "Lesca meant to say Either. Maylis had been giving that gap the same word for eighteen years". Chapter 12, held to Lesca alone, and chapter 6, held to Maylis alone, are the cleanest writing in the book, which shows what the slide costs. Chapter 6 also cuts away to a narrator who is in neither woman: "In the other village it was raining. The clock radio said 18:40." and enters Coralie for one line ("She had been in room twelve when the old woman said it").

**Do:** one head per scene. In 5: the window as Maylis the passenger, the café as Lesca seeing a dead man scratch his ear, the soup as Maylis. In 7: bingo as Maylis throughout. Cut the cutaway in 6; Coralie's chart at the top of 7 already tells it.

### 4. Lesca's village lives in the pluperfect (3, and wherever it returns)

> "She had been Lesca's deputy mayor until a night in 2018, when she handed her sash back across this table in a plastic bag."

"Had" appears 564 times. Chapter 3 has to stand up a whole second village in 2,800 words, and it does it by stopping each entrance for two sentences of history: Larrère's rule, Marie-Jo's bag, the lake, the ponies up a ramp, the four houses the State's bank went through, the cameras. Each clause is compact and several are funny ("It applied to every member. It had been needed for one."). Together they turn the narrator into the file. Nothing of Lesca's eleven years as mayor happens on the page. The woman who is supposed to be the other half of the choice is known mostly by summary, and her loss in 14 and 15 is felt less than it should be.

**Do:** dramatise one of them. The night of the plastic bag is the obvious one: it holds Marie-Jo, Kévin, the cameras and what Lesca's kind of deciding costs. Give it a scene in 3 and delete the clauses it replaces.

### 5. The ending explains itself (15, 16)

> "'At the gate I thought I could do better than you,' she said. 'I drove past the handle to be the one who did.'" (16)

Chapter 13 has the motive at the moment it acts: "She wanted it not to be her." It is the best plain sentence in the book. Chapter 16 then gives the motive three more times and in two versions: to Kévin ("I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me"), to Coralie ("The van went in, and I had to pick one. I picked.") and to Lesca, where it has become rivalry. Lesca answers with her own gloss ("It was my own night I was putting right."). Coralie supplies the label ("That was Either, with the river in it."). Chapter 15 does the same with Madame Etcheto: "'Then it wasn't Inguma that came for her. It was you.' 'Yes.'" Each of these is the scene's meaning handed to the reader after the scene.

**Do:** keep one. Coralie's "Either, with the river in it" is the one that costs Maylis something to hear, so keep that and Maylis's "I picked." Cut the confession to Kévin down to his own speech about wanting six. In the last steel room, keep "Forty-one", the stairs, the pillow, "Don't come again", "I won't". Cut both explanations of the gate.

### 6. One wit in every mouth (throughout)

> "That woman had the key. Lesca had the date." (3, narrator)

> "'For twelve years I thought it was sitting on me. Over here it's been putting me out.'" (8, Lesca)

> "'She said them so she could stay in her own bed till morning, and you're using them to leave yours.'" (10, Arlette)

> "'I go to the gendarmes for the man who drained my hut, and I come away with little Lesca.'" (6, Jeannot)

> "'I've had sixteen and a shopping pad.'" (5, Coralie)

The balanced pair as closer is the book's one rhetorical move. The narrator ends paragraphs on it ("That made twice he had asked her about a kitchen. He hadn't asked once about a river."; "She had given the bank ten minutes, and it had held for sixteen."; "At home her name wasn't even on her raincoat. Here it was cut in granite.") and five speakers end speeches on it. A book about two mirrored villages will reach for antithesis, but when everyone does, the people stop sounding like themselves at exactly the moment they say what matters. Maïté, Sarthou and Kévin escape it and are the most alive voices in the book.

**Do:** ration it. Leave it to Lesca, whose trade is the motion and the counter-motion, and to the narrator when in her head. Give Coralie's closers to her clinical register ("Ninety, and coming down."), Arlette's to her run-on, Jeannot's to his count. Break half the narrator's paragraph-end kickers by ending on the action instead.

### 7. Coralie says the theme aloud (1, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 16)

> "'I've asked you one question for sixteen years, and drawn you boxes for it.'" (16)

The pad with its empty boxes is the best object in the book and it shows this in chapter 1 without help. Coralie then states it in seven chapters: "Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you" (1), "You've never once asked me for anything" (3), "I've waited a long time to be asked for one thing in this house" (4), "For a minute there I had somebody" (5), "I've had sixteen years of her saying nothing" (7), "Sixteen years, and that's the first time I've seen that hand hold on to anything" (8). "Sixteen years" appears seven times. By the fourth she is the book's commentator on its own subject, which is the one way she is written from outside.

**Do:** let her say it twice, in 1 and at the bag in 16. Everywhere else give her the act: tearing the page off the pad, drawing the box, taking the pillow.

### 8. The narrator names the want (1, 4, 5, 6)

> "She liked it. She slowed down to get more of it." (6)

"She slowed down" was enough. Chapter 1 uses one construction three times, wanting, then a reason, then not acting: "What she wanted was for Coralie to stay awake... so Maylis didn't ask"; "Maylis wanted the duck. But the duck meant an hour at the stove... So she wrote *Either*"; "Maylis did worry about it... so she didn't knock on it." One establishes her. Three is the narrator doing the reader's work. Chapter 4 explains the word the steel room has just made physical: "It had always come when she needed it. It had never been true, and it wasn't true now. She did want one of them, badly." "Wanted" appears 32 times.

**Do:** in chapter 1 keep the duck, because the pad shows it, and cut the other two glosses. Cut the three sentences in 4. Cut "She liked it." and "She liked seeing it there." in 6. Keep "Being told what to do was a rest."; that one is a discovery.

### 9. People enter as a posture and a prop, and the props are re-issued (throughout)

> "Coralie stopped at the bedroom door in her uniform, with her keys in her hand." (8)

> "Arlette stood in the office doorway in her coat, with her bag on her arm." (10)

> "Coralie was standing in the bedroom doorway with the speech in her hand" (10)

The tail ", with" occurs 152 times, once every 280 words. "Stood" occurs 94 times, "in her hand" or "in his hand" 21. Most entrances are a figure placed in a doorway holding something. Beside that, objects are described again in full each time they return: the "white kitchen timer" (always white, always kitchen), the "cardboard folder" six times, "the long rubber boots he hunted in" three times and "rubber boots" ten, the window lowered "a few centimetres" seven times, the bingo numbers "whole and then in parts" five times. Durations are re-stated like props: "for twelve years" twelve times, "eleven years" nine, "every night" thirteen, "never once" seven. "Nineteen" is twenty times and four different things: the days of rain, the turn of the handle, the sandbags, Coralie's age on her first ward. The comparison "the way she [did a job]" is used about twenty-five times, and Inguma is a sack six times.

Some repeats are echoes that mean more the second time: two fingers on the wrist, the kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice, the crooked reflector, 2:51. Those are the book's architecture. The rest is identification, and in a book this short the reader has not had time to forget.

**Do:** keep an epithet only where its return changes its meaning. Otherwise "the timer", "the folder", "his boots". Halve the durations. Make nineteen one thing, the handle, and change the bags and the ward. Vary the entrances: let some people arrive by what they say or do.

### 10. Usage that is not consistent

- **American words in a British text.** "truck" (seven times, 10 and 13) and "baby carriage" (16) beside "grey", "colour", "torch", "pavement", "rubbish bin", "dressing gown", "mum" and "Bugger off". Use "lorry" and "pram".
- **Resident or patient.** "resident" sixteen times; "the way she read from a patient's chart" (10) and "getting a patient out of it" (13). A care home has residents.
- **One hut or several.** Everywhere a single duck hut and "the one man who went out on those fields"; then "along the track to the duck huts" (6) and "out to the hunters' huts" (11). If there are other huts there are other men to warn.
- **A second gate.** "She unlocked the platform gate, then the box under the rail" and "locked the platform gate behind her" (11). In a book where "the gate" is the central object, the platform cannot also have one. Chapter 2 calls it "the padlock on its platform"; keep to that.
- **a.m.** Three times only: the dateline of 1, "6:10 a.m." (5), "4 a.m." (13). Elsewhere bare figures ("At 9", "at 5 on a Friday morning"). Drop the three.
- **Who is speaking.** "'Nobody. She was by herself.'" then a new paragraph, "'How many seats has your van got?' Maylis said." (11) Two consecutive paragraphs by the same speaker read as a change of speaker until the tag. Join them.
- **Agreement.** "Lesca put the four sheets back together and left it unread." (14) "It" is the fourth sheet, three clauses back.
- **A shared mannerism.** Jeannot tells the past in the present ("A week ago this one tells me"). So does Larrère ("And this morning she laughs in the minute"). It is Jeannot's; take it off Larrère.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Room twelve and the count (13).** Coralie taking the dead woman's pulse "for the full half minute", the pencil line through the last name, "There was a hatbox on it. Nothing else.", and "Forty-one" beside the empty chair. Nothing is explained and everything set up in chapter 2 is paid.
2. **The sentence going through the square (14).** The loudspeaker saying "drowned him" a moment late, the deputy turning the sheet over to see whether there is more on the back, the man who claps, and Marie-Jo's "Somebody has to stack them."
3. **The article for a body under dispute, and all of chapter 12.** "The mouth", "the jaw", "the left hand" against "her right hand" is the book's best technical decision and it is kept without a slip. The eight rings are the tightest action in the manuscript.
4. **Maïté's broken sentences and Sarthou's whole ones.** "'That's winter things,' she said. 'In the— It's blankets.'" and "I do not record that I would have stayed." These two voices owe nothing to the narrator.
5. **The lines that are carried to a payment.** "I'll be brief", ending in Larrère saying nothing at the garden gate and in Coralie unable to finish the impression at the oven. Jeannot's count, ending in "And six. Bugger off." Monsieur Daguerre's "Here". Kévin's five fingers, ending in "I wanted six, Maylis." "Either" sticking in the mouth at nineteen turns.
