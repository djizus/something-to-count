# Round 27 — Reader

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. About 43,000 words. Nothing else opened but the brief.

Overall against the bar: **7**. Good. One point under the comparison, and the point is lost in two places: the middle (chapters 6 to 8) and chapter 12.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "That woman had the key. Lesca had the date." (ch. 3). One man, forty-two beds and a bed with two pillows, all named by the end of chapter 3. They blur from chapter 8, when the flood turns out to travel with Lesca and the question becomes who is allowed in a chair.
- **Tension: 7.** "It was an hour and nine minutes short of 4." (ch. 11). Chapters 9 to 14 are a 9. Chapters 6 to 8 are a 5: I knew the date of the flood and waited for it through three chapters of meetings.
- **Progression: 6.** "Maylis got them shut again behind it." (ch. 7). Between chapters 5 and 8 the same contest for one body is fought seven times, four of them for the mouth, and each ends the same way.
- **Comedy: 8.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station." (ch. 6). I smiled from "counted one of her" in chapter 1 to the Hontaube telephone call in chapter 11, with one gap, the first third of chapter 6.
- **Characters: 8.** "I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it." (ch. 16).
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'It's Saturday.' 'Then they'll want the bins out,' he said." (ch. 13), said in a flooded corridor, and it makes the corridor worse.
- **Prose: 7.** "It hit the floor like a full sack dropped off a trailer" (ch. 1). I never read a sentence twice for its grammar. I read several twice for their logic (see clarity).
- **The ending: 8.** "He went and got it. Then he stepped over the string in his rubber boots and began to dig." (ch. 16).
- **Clarity: 6.** "That bank goes tonight now, whether you stay or I put you out. But you're not sitting in that chair when the phone wakes me." (ch. 12). At the top of the book I could not say what the two women were fighting for.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. From chapter 9 I could not have stopped.

**Would I press it on a friend?** Yes, on a friend who likes village comedy. Not yet on the friend who lent me Dark Matter: that friend stops in chapter 6.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, by a little.** Above them at one thing.

1. **Below Crouch on rules.** In Dark Matter each rule of the box is learned once, by watching it go wrong, and in a chase I always know what each person wants. Here I counted eleven rules by Friday: lie still or push; stand or stay sitting; the owner keeps the right hand; knowledge leaks in the chair; Inguma fetches the visitor when its count is done; nobody standing in the steel room can say what is false; the water follows Lesca; only the last night counts; the bank goes once she is in; the one it cannot take home dies; the dead play bingo. Most arrive as a character's announcement. Three wobble (fault 3). I kept a ledger, and a reader keeping a ledger is not afraid.

2. **Below The Good Place on the reversal.** Its great reversal is a scene: one person works it out aloud, in front of the others, and everything before it re-reads. This book has a reversal of the same rank. The woman who crossed over to stop the flood is the flood, and chapter 3's "Lesca had the date" turns sour on re-reading. But it is handed to me in three sentences of narration on a platform in chapter 7, then shouted at a bar where I already know it, then explained again with dates in chapter 8.

3. **Above both on what the choice costs.** Crouch ends his choices in a chase. The Good Place makes the trolley problem funny and resets it. This book runs the trolley twice, once each way, and makes both women wrong: one turned the handle and drowned a man, the other would not turn it and drowned an old woman. "Nobody emptied hers" (ch. 15) is better than anything at the end of Dark Matter. The village is also better company than any supporting cast in Crouch.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Chapter 12: I do not know what the fight in the van is for.**
"That bank goes tonight now, whether you stay or I put you out. But you're not sitting in that chair when the phone wakes me." Then: "Let go of it. I can get him out. I know that track, and I know where the dip is. Let me drive." Then: "You're the water."
Both women want Jeannot up the ladder. Maylis has just said the bank is lost either way. So when her right hand knocks the van out of gear, covers the eyes and pulls the handbrake, I cannot tell what she wins. I can see what she loses: two minutes, from 2:51 to 2:53, on a night the book counts by the minute, and the driver who knows where the dip is. Four pages later Maylis sinks the van in that dip. I think the book means all of this ("At the gate I thought I could do better than you", ch. 16), but on the page it reads as a muddle at the one place a muddle cannot be afforded.
*Do:* give the fight one stake and say it in the steel room before the phone rings. The stake already exists: Lesca said on Sunday "This time I've come to stay" and on Wednesday "I won't be sent home". Have Maylis say it: if you drive tonight you never leave. Then let chapter 13 or 16 count the two minutes against 03:11, once, in Maylis's own sum.

**2. Chapters 7 and 8: the book's best turn is told three times and never played.**
First: "For the two nights she had kept Lesca out, the river had stood still. It was raining now because Lesca was back." (ch. 7, the platform). Second: "You're the water." (ch. 7, the bar). Third: "There's no flood in your village. There's mine, and I bring it across with me, a night at a time." (ch. 8).
Maylis reaches it from one reading of a marker post, in narration, faster than I did. The bar scene, which should be the discovery, comes sixty lines after I have been told. The third telling is a report with seven dates in it, and I skimmed it.
*Do:* take the conclusion out of the platform scene. Leave the fact there (a metre yesterday, fifty-five under today, rain since noon) and let me do the sum. Let "You're the water" at the bar be the first time anybody says it. Cut Lesca's dates speech to the admission: "I was behind your teeth, and I didn't believe you either. I've been through the dates now. You were right."

**3. Three rules wobble, and I noticed each one.**
(a) How long a visit lasts. In chapter 3, with nothing to count, Inguma fetches Lesca "after twenty minutes". In chapter 5 it is at the kitchen door ten minutes after she wakes. In chapter 7 she wakes in Maylis at 6 on Saturday, says no words that I am shown, and it comes for her at bingo that evening. Chapter 6 made a whole comedy of the count running five hours late, so I was watching the clock.
(b) What "come" means. "I've been with you every night since, except two" (ch. 8) means sharing the chair. "If I'm in your village at 2:51" (ch. 8) and "Don't come on Friday" (ch. 8) sound like crossing in the body. Pushed out, in the chair and driving are three states, and "come", "in", "back" and "stay" are used for all three.
(c) The hall of the dead. Lesca says of the empty chair "That's where I sit if I stay with you" (ch. 8). In chapter 12 she lets go, which is the opposite of staying, and lands in that hall anyway.
*Do:* (a) one line on Saturday morning giving Inguma something that lasts till evening; (b) fix one word for each state and keep to it; (c) one sentence in chapter 12 that she held the chair down too long, so the hall is the price of the seven rings and not of letting go.

**4. Chapter 6 is two chapters, and the first one is paperwork.**
"Seven and seven. In a tie the chair's vote decides. The motion fails. The mayor speaks after the prefect."
It runs nine scenes: the undated resignation, the gendarme's form, the threat about the nets, the crossing, the minute of silence, the file, the floor, the soup, the council. Before Maylis gets to the other village I have read three pages of forms and threats in three different offices. In the council I had to stop and work out what had been voted. The stake of that vote, who speaks on the 12th, meant nothing to me until chapter 14.
*Do:* break the chapter at "On Wednesday 2 December". In the council, have Larrère's motion said in words a stranger follows: the mayor does not speak this year. Keep the timer, "timed like an egg" and the back lane.

**5. Chapters 5 to 8: one move, seven times.**
"Maylis threw herself at the jaw and got it." (ch. 5). "Maylis shut the teeth on the rest." (ch. 7). "Maylis brought her right hand up and clapped it over her own mouth." (ch. 8).
Kitchen jaw, stair post, bar jaw, landing wrist, breakfast mouth, then the wheel and the brake. "Jaw" and "teeth" come eleven times in chapter 7 alone. By the breakfast fight I knew how it would end before the bowl went over. The café's joke about which Castagnet she is after runs five times (ch. 5, 6, 7 twice, 11) before it pays in chapter 16.
*Do:* cut the bar fight by a third; it is the longest and the only one that repeats itself inside its own scene. Keep the stairs, the landing and the breakfast, because in each of those Coralie does something new. Cut two of the five Castagnet rounds, Josiane's second at the bar and Larrère's in chapter 11.

**6. Chapter 11 keeps Maylis's plan from me until 23:34.**
"Back in the van she did what she had told Lesca she would do."
All afternoon she fills sandbags, tries to borrow two men and tells Jeannot the bank goes at 3. Meanwhile her real plan is to stay awake so that it never goes. I had forgotten that plan from Wednesday, so at the trapdoor I did not understand why she walked away after three knocks from a man she hauls out by the collar at 03:03.
*Do:* move the two sentences ("It would only go tonight if Lesca was in the village at 2:51… So Maylis had to be awake at that minute") up to 5 p.m., before Jeannot climbs the platform.

**7. Chapter 15 makes me wait a second time.**
After "Forty-one" I wanted Maylis and Coralie. Chapter 14 earns its place. Chapter 15 is four short scenes of the other village's aftermath, and I read them leaning forward for the wrong reason. I also stumbled at "the commemoration was ending as a party on the bank": a band and a fire bull, the same evening the mayor has said into a microphone that she drowned a man, and nobody remarks on it.
*Do:* run chapter 15's scenes on as the tail of chapter 14, with no chapter break. Give Maïté one line that the tent was paid for, or that the Port dances whatever is said in the square.

**8. Nobody ever says why Larrère had the gate raised on Monday the 23rd.**
"On Monday afternoon the president of the association had rung and told her to raise it." (ch. 1). The order cracks her rib, drains Jeannot's water and starts letter 41, the complaint and the vote. Sarthou asks whose order it was. Nobody asks what it was for, and I did, to the end.
*Do:* one line, in Jeannot's mouth or Larrère's.

**9. Chapter 1, the square: the most machinery on the pages where I had least patience.**
"'I've got a registered letter for the mayor,' the postman said into the gap. 'Who is also the president of the landowners' association, it says here.'"
In one scene I am asked to hold a mayor who is also a president, an association that owns a gate, a village that keeps its key, a handle, a basin and a forty-first letter. I held them because the thing on the wardrobe had come first. The scene is funny. It is a thousand words in which the only event is a signature.
*Do:* cut the paragraph that walks the bank end to end ("First came the care home, then the patch…") down to the gate and the hut. The prologue has already shown me the bank, and chapter 5 explains the basin again.

**10. Two objects are carried to the last page without my knowing what they are for.**
"She took the letter and buttoned it into her chest pocket, with her card." (ch. 16). The card with *left* on it goes into that pocket in chapter 2, falls out in chapter 13 and is still there at the grave. The two lamps are lit in every meeting. I can guess. I should not have to.
*Do:* one plain sentence about the card when it falls out in chapter 13, or drop the two late mentions.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

2. **The prologue and its three returns.** "It rang once." Then the reflector going out under the water (ch. 9), "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. 02:51." (ch. 10), and "drowned him" arriving late from the second loudspeaker (ch. 14).
3. **Madame Etcheto.** The care plan ("Staff to wait. Staff not to join in."), bingo with the dead, the duck on her knees, and "Nobody emptied hers."
4. **Jeannot.** The numbered points, nine rings and "And six. Bugger off.", "I was in no danger, and I'll say so to anyone", letter No. 42 and the second shovel.
5. **"Either", from the eggs to the platform.** "Eggs is what Either gets in this house", the word sticking in the steel room, the word coming out at the gate in daylight, and the oven door in chapter 16 that Coralie opens and cannot finish.

## What I will remember in a month

A thing made of leaves and steel that can be sent off to count stars. An old woman who reports a boy for giving it two lots of stars. Two fingers on the inside of a wrist. A car window that had never gone all the way down. A man who answers the phone in a hole in a field with "Who's dead?" A red reflector going out. "Nobody emptied hers." I will not remember how the chair worked.
