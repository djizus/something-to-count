# Round 27 — Thriller

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the care home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the fields there was one man" (3). Clear, countable, and repeated until the reader can do the sum. Marked down because chapter 8 turns the flood into something a single breath can cancel, and because the man with the folder stops being a threat in chapter 11.
- **Tension: 7.** "On the sixth ring the rib caught and held" (11). Chapters 11 to 13 are a 9. Chapters 7 and 8 are a 6: the reader has watched her fail to push four times before the night that depends on pushing.
- **Progression: 6.** "On Monday night the rib gave her one breath again" (8). A whole night spent on a repeat. The first six chapters and the last four move; 7 and 8 run the same three beats twice, and new rules are still arriving in chapter 10.
- **Comedy: 8.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station" (6).
- **Characters: 8.** "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them" (7).
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'It's Saturday.' 'Then they'll want the bins out,' he said" (13), in a flooded corridor, and it makes the corridor worse.
- **Prose: 8.** "Then the water rose up the door and covered it, and the red light went out" (9). It reads at Crouch's speed and never asks to be admired.
- **The ending: 7.** "the car door shutting on a pillow" (16) is a 9. Chapter 15 is a 5, and it sits between the two chapters that end the book.
- **Clarity: 6.** "Only the last night counts" (8). The rule the climax stands on is three sentences of assertion, and the creature it concerns behaves three different ways.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. From the end of chapter 2 I was never going to stop, and from 11 to 13 I could not.

**Press it on a friend?** Yes, with a warning that the middle circles before the night comes. A reader who only wants Crouch would be told to push through 7 and 8.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, narrowly. A 7 against an 8.**

What Crouch does: the premise is running by the end of the first chapter; there is one rule (the box, and what you carry into it) and every later chapter is a consequence of it, not an amendment to it; each chapter stops on a fact that changes what the reader thinks is happening; and the hero always has a plan the reader believes might work.

**Level with him: chapter endings and the double.** "*That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it*" (3) is a Dark Matter ending: the other self wants the wife and has just written it down. Chapters 0, 2, 3, 8, 11 and 13 all close on a line that makes the next one get read. The stairs in chapter 5 are frightening in exactly the way Jason2 at Daniela's table is frightening.

**Above him: the marriage and the choice.** Crouch makes me afraid for Daniela as a thing that can be taken. This book makes Coralie the one who decides, twice ("You'll go back down those stairs"; "I was the other one"), and so the fear is of her verdict. And the central choice is finer than anything in Recursion: "If she went on, whatever the river did behind her was the river's doing. She wanted it not to be her" (13). Lesca acted and drowned one man. Maylis arranged not to act and drowned one woman. The Good Place would have built a season on that.

**Below him: the machinery.** The rules keep coming until chapter 10 (the chair, the right hand, the truth room, the count, the water, the last night, the hall of the dead, sitting on Inguma). The reader cannot judge Friday's odds because the creature at its centre has no fixed behaviour. And the heroine's plan for the night is one she has been told will not work. Crouch never lets the reader be ahead of the hero at the climax; here I was ahead of her at 2:51 and again at the dip.

**Where a Crouch reader puts it down:** the square in chapter 1, before anything has happened to anyone; the chrysanthemums in chapter 4; the café vote in chapter 8; chapter 15.

**Where I was afraid, and of what:** Lesca's foot on the first stair (5), of a rape nobody would be able to name. Maylis's body with its eyes open and Coralie's thumb over the doctor's number (6), of the old woman's rule. The reflector going down the track on Maylis's own night (11), of the repetition. "Start at the bank end" (13), of what Maylis has just done to Coralie with four words. The torch stopping at twelve (13).

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. Inguma has three behaviours, the old woman's remedy is never tried, and so 2:51 on Friday is foregone. (2, 5, 6, 8, 11)**
"It needs more than that. A great many things, and small ones" (3). For Madame Etcheto and for Arlette's mother the words keep the thing *off the bed* until it has counted: "it was at that till daylight." For Lesca in chapter 5 the words stop it *fetching a visitor home*. For Maylis in chapter 6 the words are said at 11 and "Inguma came down at its usual minute and sat on her" anyway. Nobody remarks on the difference. Then the climax is made to depend on keeping it off one bed for one minute, and the heroine never once tries the one thing the book has told us keeps it off a bed. Her plan instead is "I'll sit up in the van, awake", and Kévin has already told her, and tells her again that evening, "The thing that sits on you doesn't need you asleep." She has failed to push on the 4th, 6th, 7th and 9th. So at 2:51 I had no hope to lose.
*Do:* fix the creature first. One sentence in chapter 4's meeting that says which it is: the count holds it from fetching, never from sitting; or the count holds it from both. Then give Maylis a Friday plan the reader believes. The book has it lying ready: she has never got the words out without "Either" stopping them (2). Make that the plan from chapter 8 on. She practises, it sticks, the bingo hall hears her get halfway. On Friday the reader waits to see whether she can say one whole sentence to it before 2:51. She can't, in time. Then "Inguma, enauk hire beldur", whole, in chapter 12 pays off a thing the reader was pulling for, instead of arriving as a surprise skill. Lesca's "only the last night counts" should come out of her mouth in the truth room with somebody noticing that it came out: that is the only warrant the rule can have, and it costs one line.

**2. The fight in the van is over nothing the reader can name. (12, 13)**
"Let go of it. I can get him out. I know that track" (12). Lesca wants to drive to the hut. Maylis wrecks the attempt with handbrake, gear lever and a hand over the eyes, puts Lesca out, and then: "She drove past" (13), to the hut. The left hand and the right hand have been the book's best device for twelve chapters and at the climax they are fighting over who holds the wheel on the way to the same place. Lesca's exit also has two causes (Inguma counting five fingers; "I'm letting go") and each weakens the other.
*Do:* oppose them. Maylis left the handle fitted and the chain tight at 11; her pad put the gate first until Friday morning. Let the right hand pull for the gate and the left for the track: the book's question, in one body, at 60 an hour on a bank. Then "Go to the hut first" is an order she is free to refuse, the handle comes level with her eyes for three breaths, and "She drove past" carries the whole weight it wants. Chapter 16's "I sent you down that track with five words out of your own mouth" is then true. Keep one cause for Lesca's going: she stops holding Inguma down.

**3. The book's one true reversal is told in narration, then read into the minutes. (7, 8)**
"It was raining now because Lesca was back. She had nobody to tell and no mouth to tell it with. The one who had her mouth was the flood" (7). That is the turn that re-reads the first six chapters (the dry winter, the rain starting the night of the 23rd, the blame that would not come out in the truth room), and it is given to me as a finished deduction on a platform, four lines after the evidence. By the time "You're the water" is said at the bar it is a repeat. Then chapter 8 opens with Lesca reciting it again with dates.
*Do:* cut the three sentences on the platform. Leave the dry boards, the metre that didn't move, the rain at noon, and Maylis shutting her teeth without the reader being told why. Let "You're the water" be the first time the idea is spoken, with the hall hearing a request for a drink. The reader gets there a half-second before Josiane does. Cut Lesca's recap in 8 to the two lines only she can supply: "I've been through the dates now. You were right," and Inguma "putting me out."

**4. She drives into the dip as if she had never been warned. (11, 13)**
"The track's soft at the dip, softer than where I got stuck, the winter you towed me out. You'd sit there till Easter" (11). Nine hours later: "In the van she could still have both, the man and the gate. She worked it out the way she worked out a job" (13). The sum has no dip in it. She waded that dip at 5:30 with the water halfway to her knees. The death in room twelve hangs on the van sticking, and the van sticks because the heroine has forgotten the last thing the man said to her. A thriller reader calls that the plot steering.
*Do:* make it a bet she knows she is making. One line at 02:55: she hears him say Easter, and takes the van because on foot there is no both. Then the van going down on its belly is her fault and not the author's, which is what chapter 16 needs it to be.

**5. The prologue spends chapter 9's secret, and the same night is staged three times. (0, 9, 11)**
"Then she took out her phone. The number was already in it... It rang once" (0). From page one I know she looked down the track and rang somebody. By "Did you know he was out?" (3) I know who. So chapter 9's "One man knew she had looked down the track first" tells me a thing I have held for nine chapters, in a summary of a scene I have already been shown. Then chapter 11 walks the same bank, handle, headlights and reflector a third time.
*Do:* stop the prologue one beat sooner, on "The number was already in it." The hook survives. Whose number, and how long she let it ring, become chapter 9's, and Coralie's "Not answered. 02:51" in chapter 10 lands on a reader who learned it a chapter ago and not nine. Leave the third staging alone; that repetition is the point.

**6. Chapters 7 and 8 run their beats twice. (7, 8)**
"Maylis heard the pillow go down on the single bed in the spare room" opens chapter 7's first scene and "Maylis heard the pillow go down on the single bed" closes its last. Between them and through 8: the platform is read three times, the rib gives "one breath" three times, Lesca is put out and comes back, a vote is lost by a casting hand for the second chapter running.
*Do:* one pillow, at the end. Fold "On Monday night the rib gave her one breath again" and the Wednesday failure into the Sunday one. The café vote can lose its opening exchange about heating the square and start at "The petition."

**7. Larrère's question is loaded in chapter 6 and never fired. (6, 14)**
"A metre from that stone, with a camera on her. I'll have my answer there" (6). In chapter 14 he stands at the foot of the steps with his book and says nothing; she confesses unasked, and he shuts the book afterwards. The promise of a public cross-examination kept me turning pages for eight chapters and is quietly withdrawn. On the other side of the river he fares no better as an opponent: Kévin's "I'd do them, Maylis" (11) takes away his only hold on her before the night starts, and his order at 2:53 costs her nothing to ignore.
*Do:* let him open the book and his mouth as she turns to the third sheet. "When you stood on that platform, did you" for the second time, and she answers before the bell he is waiting for. One line; the confession is then taken from him, which is worth more than a confession nobody was about to demand.

**8. Lesca's reason for coming on Friday changes between chapters without a scene. (8, 10, 12)**
"I can raise that gate. I've got the scar to prove it, and I'll have your Port dry by 4" (8). "Somebody who had done that night before ought to be at the floodgate tonight" (10). Then, in the chair: "I left a man at the end of that track. I'm bringing this one up the ladder myself" (12). She knows by now that she is the flood, so her coming is the act that kills Madame Etcheto, and the reader needs to know what she wanted enough to do it. Chapter 10 gives one sentence ("to be looked at by the one who didn't know") and it is about Coralie, not the hut.
*Do:* turn her in chapter 10, after "2:51. I've known that for twelve years." When she tries the two sentences aloud and finds only "a handle, turning", have her decide what she will do with a second go at it: not the gate. Then chapter 12 opens on a want the reader already holds.

**9. Chapter 15 is slack, and it stands between the two endings. (15)**
"Eleven hands went up. Larrère kept both of his on the table." Nobody doubted the vote. After "Forty-one" the book has two questions left: will Lesca say it, and will Coralie stay. Chapter 14 answers the first. Chapter 15 then answers nothing until its last three lines.
*Do:* keep Madame Etcheto at the minibus, keep the gutter and the phone on the wall (the kitchen clock paying off is the last twist in the book and is nearly thrown away), cut the council to the timer turned back to nothing and "Lesca, Maylis. Surname first." Consider ending the chapter on Larrère finding the wheel tracks in the dark and opening chapter 16's landing scene with "Good night, Maylis" set against the pillow leaving.

**10. Chapter 1 stops for the registered letter before anything has cost anybody anything. (1)**
"I've paid for forty-one registered letters, just to get that man's name on a card, and it's little Lesca who signs for them." I need the letters; no. 42 is the last page. But the square runs from the plane leaves to "Then that goes in forty-two as well" with no consequence yet attached to the thing on the wardrobe, and a reader who came for the prologue's river is asked to wait.
*Do:* don't cut the scene, load it. Put the picture of the stone into the square: she is standing on the spot where the granite stands in her head, and the man's name she couldn't read is the one who is talking to her through a van window. One sentence, and every line Jeannot speaks in chapter 1 is spoken by a man the reader suspects is dead somewhere.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The reflector.** "At the end of the track the reflector went on shining in her headlights for four more minutes. Then the water rose up the door and covered it, and the red light went out" (9). And its return, screwed on crooked, on her own night (11).
2. **Coralie and the phone.** "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. 02:51" and "Because you'd have told me" (10). The landing was a decision. Every chapter before it re-reads.
3. **The pad, the cross and the handle.** Maylis keeps Lesca out for two nights, so the river misses the figure on Bordenave's pad, so Jeannot votes "Shut", so she stands with her weight on the handle and says "Either", so "Then it's shut. That's how the vote left it" (7, 8, 11). Her one success causes her failure. It is the best piece of plotting in the book and it is invisible.
4. **Room twelve to "Forty-one."** The torch reading door numbers, the two fingers on the wrist "for the full half minute", the hatbox on the wardrobe, Kévin counting five in his own headlights, the empty chair (13). And its echo before we are told: "'Twelve. One, two.' Somebody pulled a chair in to a table" (14).
5. **The sentence crossing the square.** "The loudspeaker on the bank road said 'drowned him' after the square had finished hearing it... A child asked what the lady had done. Somebody told the child" (14). With it, the two last answers in the truth room: "I won't. It came out whole" (16).
