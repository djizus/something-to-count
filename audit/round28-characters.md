# Critic report: Characters

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.5.** "'The gate, or Castagnet? Say which, Maylis. For once.'" From chapter 9 the stakes have faces. Before that they are a date in a file.
- **Tension: 7.5.** "She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it broke." Chapters 11 to 13 are a 9. Chapters 6 to 8 are a 6.
- **Progression: 7.** "I've tried for three nights to keep you out, and I can't." The middle runs one contest three times: Lesca reaches for Coralie and a hand stops her, on the stairs (5), on the landing (7), at the kitchen table (8).
- **Comedy: 8.** "'Opened what? There's a name over it. It reads that she opened him.'"
- **Characters: 7.5.** "'I gave that word up when I was thirty-one, and I won't have my own face say it to me.'" It is the only line in the book in which either woman admits they were once the same person. See faults 1 to 4.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.5.** "'Five,' he said." A joke from chapter 2 comes back in flood water at 4 in the morning and is not a joke.
- **Prose: 7.5.** "It had always come when she needed it. It had never been true, and it wasn't true now. She did want one of them, badly." Three sentences explaining what the stuck word had just shown.
- **The ending: 8 for Maylis, 6 for Lesca.** "Then he stepped over the string in his rubber boots and began to dig." Against: "On Tuesday the council gave Lesca three minutes to speak against her own removal", a whole fall in one sentence.
- **Clarity: 7.** "In this village he had been mayor until the spring after the flood." The book needs a sentence like that every few pages to keep its two casts apart, and I still stopped to work out which Larrère, which Maïté, which Kévin.

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. From the nine rings in chapter 9 I could not have stopped.
Would I press it on a friend? Yes, on the friend who liked The Good Place more than Dark Matter.

## 2. Against the comparisons, for Characters

**Above Crouch. Below The Good Place. Short of the bar by half a point.**

- **Above Crouch on people.** His doubles differ by what they did. These two differ by how they talk: one in motions ("All those in favour." "There are two of us."), one in job sheets ("Twenty-five minutes was a trailer of leaves to the dump and back"). His villagers are furniture. Here Jeannot numbers his grievances, Maïté cannot finish a sentence, and a suitcase with "no dust on" it tells a marriage.
- **Below Crouch on the double as self.** Dark Matter frightens because the other man wants your wife for your own reasons and you know it. Here the two women hold five meetings and never once speak of the thirty-one years they share. The premise is two versions of one person. On the page they are two women of the same height.
- **Level with The Good Place on people wrong in their own way** (Larrère's gap in the window, Josiane's syllogism, Sarthou's "I cannot summon Either to the station"), and level on the reversal that re-reads: "'2:51,' she said. 'I've known that for twelve years.'" re-reads every "Madame le maire" before it. **Below it on the central pair.** Eleanor and Chidi change each other by argument over time. Maylis and Lesca exchange rules and threats.

The lens questions, answered:

- **Distinct?** Yes, by the second line each speaks.
- **Alive?** Lesca from her first page. Maylis fully from chapter 9. In chapters 5, 7 and the start of 8 she is a right hand and a jaw.
- **Softened or excused?** Each once. Maylis at the handle at 5 p.m. (fault 2). Lesca on her own landing (fault 5). Everywhere else the book is hard on both: "She wanted it not to be her"; "I wouldn't have stopped."
- **Described from outside as a woman?** No. Neither is given a face or a body. Each is given a trade.
- **Villagers a reader would miss:** Jeannot first, then Madame Etcheto, Kévin, Maïté, and the Larrère of the egg timer. Not, one by one, Josiane, Bordenave, the dealer or Sarthou; they are a chorus.
- **Which relationship carries the book?** Maylis and Coralie, measured by a shared laugh and a shopping pad. It pays: "the car door shutting on a pillow." Maylis and Jeannot pays best. Maylis and Lesca, the relationship the premise promises, pays least.
- **Does the reader care who lives?** Yes. Jeannot from "And six. Bugger off." Madame Etcheto from "That's my door." The forty-two from "The others have died since then, in those beds, of the things people die of."

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The two women never touch the life they share. Chapters 4, 8, 12, 16.**
"They were the same height. Neither of them liked it." That is as near as either comes to looking at herself. They share a father, a signature, a night behind a tent in 2010 when each said "You". None of it is spoken between them. Each walks past the father in the hall of the dead alone (8, 12), and he looks up at neither. So the sameness is a mechanism (memories pass in a chair) and never a recognition, and the stairs in chapter 5 read as a stranger's assault where they should read as Maylis fighting her own want.
*Do:* in chapter 8, when the wall opens, turn Lesca around. Put both of them in front of the father's place and give each one line about him. In chapter 16, let one of them say what she did behind the tent. Ten lines in all.

**2. The hinge of the book is staged as a slip of the tongue. Chapter 11.**
"'Either,' Maylis said." Then: "He was off the platform and down the steps before she could take it back." On Tuesday this woman said "Open" twice to Larrère's window with her job in his folder. At noon on Friday Kévin freed her: "Don't keep a gate shut for me." At 5 the handle is on, the man is safe on the bank, and she says the word with no inside to it at all. The clause about taking it back excuses her; Jeannot's speed does the failing for her. In chapter 16 she says "I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me", which is a motive, and the page at 5 p.m. shows an accident.
*Do:* cut "before she could take it back". Keep Jeannot on the platform until she has lifted the handle off with him watching. Give the word its object in one sentence: she could say Open to a car window and cannot say it to the face of the man it costs, the same deference as the duck and the hour at the stove.

**3. Lesca's fall is summarised, and the man she jailed never speaks. Chapters 14 and 15.**
"Eleven hands went up, and when the secretary's pen stopped at 'Madame le—', Lesca said, 'Lesca, Maylis. Surname first.'" The timer has been the book's best prop since chapter 3 and its last use is half a sentence. In chapter 6: "He kept the cap in his hands." Kévin did six months on her cameras and wears the coat she gave him. He is in the square in chapter 14 and on the bank in chapter 15, and he is given no word to her. Chapter 15 is a third the length of 16. The woman who carried the best scenes leaves by memo.
*Do:* play the Tuesday council for half a page: Larrère finally finishes "When you stood on that platform, did you—", she turns the timer to nothing. Bring Kévin back from the bank with the loudspeakers while she stacks chairs, and give him one line.

**4. Coralie says three times she would have Lesca, and never weighs what only Maylis gives her. Chapters 5, 7, 8.**
"I'd have this one again." "I'd have you tomorrow." "I'd have had you. You answer." Against these, the book has planted the answer in a gesture: "Then she looked across the table for the other half of the laugh." Lesca fills the spoon. Maylis is the one who laughs with her. Coralie never notices this aloud, turns Lesca down on principle (7) and then for the river (8), and so never once chooses Maylis for what Maylis is. For three chapters the reader is handed Coralie's verdict and agrees with it. That lowers what chapter 16 costs.
*Do:* one clause on the landing in chapter 7 or at the table in chapter 8: this one answers, and does not laugh.

**5. Lesca lies to her own Coralie and the lie is never paid. Chapters 10 and 15.**
"'For a man who hasn't drowned yet.'" The narrator knows better: "She had told her it was for the man." She crossed to take another bed "and Coralie Mounet in it". She confesses the drowning to a full square and never tells the woman four steps away that she spent a fortnight leaving her. "Good night, Maylis" is then given by someone who has not been told. That softens Lesca at the exact point the book rewards her.
*Do:* on the landing in chapter 15, before the name, Lesca says what she crossed for. Coralie then says the name, or does not.

**6. The narrator says Maylis's wants for her. Chapters 1 to 8.**
"What she wanted was for Coralie to stay awake and keep her fingers on that wrist." "She wanted something else too. She wanted one day as the woman who signed things." "She wanted to tell her not to go." About fifteen of these. Coralie has waited sixteen years to learn what this woman wants; the reader is told every few pages and never has to wait. It also blunts the one that matters: "She wanted it not to be her."
*Do:* cut each one where an act already says it. "So she wrote *Either* across the bottom" needs no "Maylis wanted the duck" in front of it. Keep the last.

**7. The person with the most right to speak is kept off the page. Chapter 16.**
"Her daughter is having her buried here on Tuesday." The daughter is named twice and never appears. No resident and no relative faces Maylis. Madame Darrigrand does ("fetching a man out of a dry hole"), and it is the hardest speech in the chapter; then Kévin tows the van, Marie-Jo stands her a chair, and Jeannot brings a letter and a shovel.
*Do:* put the daughter at the cemetery gate in place of the priest, with the words written out, asking who digs.

**8. Coralie never asks about her own double. Chapters 4 and 8.**
In chapter 4 she is sharp on it: "a landing I've never slept across, with the wrong number of years in it". In chapter 8 she is told there is another village, draws three columns, and has "one question. Is that chart right?" A woman told that her other self was left on a landing for eleven years asks why. She asks what Jeannot is to Lesca. She never asks what she is.
*Do:* one question in chapter 8, and Lesca unable to answer it standing.

**9. The Larrère Maylis faces is the flat one. Chapters 6, 11, 16.**
In Lesca's village: "'Eleven years I've been timed like an egg,' he said. 'I have sentences in that register with no ends on them.'" That is a man. In Maylis's village: "That's prison, for eels." That is a lever. The climax is fought against the second.
*Do:* on the bank on Friday, give him one sentence about the kitchen he is driving to, and let him fail to finish it, as he does with "They tell me," in chapter 16.

**10. Two of the most quoted people have no names. Chapters 5 to 8, 16.**
"'She's got a mayor in her,' the dealer said." The dealer speaks in four chapters and delivers the book's verdict ("Had the key and went for a walk, did you?"). The secretary has its driest lines ("The chair votes once, Madame le maire."). Nobody misses "the dealer".
*Do:* name both.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 10, the bedroom.** "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. 02:51." and "'Because you'd have told me.'" Every line of it.
2. **The right hand.** "The owner keeps her right hand", and all it buys: the tipped spoon, the shut tap, the stair post, the timer wound to nothing, the handbrake, "That's her. In this hand."
3. **Jeannot's refusal to be grateful, dead or alive.** "He put a grain of corn on a card." "Jean's for the stone." "It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you."
4. **Kévin's fingers.** "'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much.'"
5. **The laugh and what ends it.** The oven door in chapters 1 and 8, Coralie's hand on the rib in chapter 9, the impression she cannot finish in chapter 16, and "'I know what you picked. I was the other one.'"
