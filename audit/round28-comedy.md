# Critic's report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** "'Then which do you want?' Behind Coralie somebody was calling a name. 'The gate, or Castagnet? Say which, Maylis. For once.'" The public stake and the private one are the same question.
- **Tension: 7.5.** "On the sixth ring the rib caught and held." Chapters 11 to 13 hold. Chapters 7 to 9 let go between set pieces.
- **Progression: 7.** "'It's the ti—' Lesca said. She started again, and it stuck on the same word." The steel room is visited six times and the last four repeat one move.
- **Comedy: 7.5.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" I smiled without a break through chapters 1 to 6, 10 and 11. I stopped in four places it was not meant to stop (faults 1, 2, 4, 5).
- **Characters: 8.** "'Eleven years I've been timed like an egg,' he said. 'I have sentences in that register with no ends on them.'" Every villager is wrong in a form of words of their own.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.5.** "'Here,' he said. 'I know you are.'" One bad switch, at the bar in chapter 7 (fault 3).
- **Prose: 7.** "She had worked the roadsides out at the speed of the village tractor. Inguma was doing them on foot." Against it: "It was out before she had time to think about it" (1), "It had come out before she had a reason to give for it" (4), "It was out before she could stop the mouth" (6).
- **The ending: 7.5.** "'It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you.'" Chapter 16 is an 8.5. Chapter 15 is a 6.
- **Clarity: 6.5.** "'I was sitting down.'" That line is the only statement that a woman can lie seated and not standing, and the book's last third leans on it.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. **Pressed on a friend?** Yes, with the instruction to read to the end of chapter 5 before judging.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**The Good Place: a shade below.** Level in chapters 1 to 6 and 10 to 11. Below from the start of chapter 7 to the end of chapter 9, and in chapter 15.

- **Level: each person is wrong in their own form.** Larrère speaks through a gap and says "I'll be brief". Jeannot numbers his grievances. Sarthou files. Marie-Jo speaks "through the chair" to a wall. Lesca has a sentence that is true and hides everything. Maylis has "Either". The Good Place builds its four the same way, and here the form is first the joke and later the wound: "Either" on the handle in chapter 11, "I'll be brief" followed by nothing in chapter 15.
- **Level: the moral problem is funny and stays large.** A woman who cannot choose soup or duck has to choose who drowns. The stone that cannot be given a verb ("It reads that she opened him") is the trolley problem as a committee item. Neither makes the death small.
- **Below: the rules of the world stop being funny.** The Good Place gets a joke out of every rule it has, every time it is used. Here the hand rule is funny three times (the tipped spoon, the coffee tap, two hands voting) and the truth rule once (ch. 4, "It's Saturday"). After that both are used gravely, in private, in the same shape. And the big reversal, "You're the water", does not get a clean beat the way "This is the Bad Place" does.

**Crouch: above, on this lens.** He does not try for comedy. What he does with dread for a marriage, this book does with an oven door, and does better: by chapter 16 the reader is afraid of Coralie not finishing an impression.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The truth rule is the book's best comic machine and it is run in private, solemnly, five times. (Ch. 4, 5, 8, 12; structural.)**
"'I w—' Lesca's fist closed." / "'It's the ti—'" / "'This time I'm coming to get—' It stuck there." A politician who cannot say an untrue thing on her feet is a gift, and the only audience she ever has for it is herself. Its scope is also unclear. Chapter 4 says "Standing in this room". Chapter 5 has her fail on "Ei—" in the square with no explanation. Chapter 8 says "I was sitting down" about a kitchen. Chapter 14 says "She was on her feet" as if it mattered in daylight.
*Do:* decide that it holds anywhere, standing, since the first crossing. Show it once in public in chapter 5 so the café sees a sentence fail. In chapter 8, when Lesca says "It's the tide" at the table, have Coralie make her stand up and say it again. That is one funny, decisive beat, and it replaces the first steel-room scene of chapter 8 entirely.

**2. Chapter 15 summarises the scene the comedy has been building to. (Structural.)**
"On Tuesday the council gave Lesca three minutes to speak against her own removal, and she turned the timer back to nothing herself." The timer, "through the chair", the three-minute rule and the secretary's "For the minutes" have been wound since chapter 3. Their last use is one sentence. "Lesca, Maylis. Surname first" is the best line in the chapter and it is thrown away inside a subordinate clause. Lesca's ending is three pages against Maylis's ten.
*Do:* play the council. Marie-Jo sets the timer in front of the mayor. The secretary's pen stops. Larrère has the floor with no bell and nothing to say. One page.

**3. The reversal that re-reads the book is delivered under three jokes. (Ch. 7.)**
"'You're the water.'" Then: "'She wants water.'" "'She's got a bar full of coffee.'" Then Josiane on bread, then Bordenave on the date. Only after all that: "Maylis felt the pull go out of the jaw." The reader is still working out what the three words mean while the hall is being funny about them. The beat that tells us Lesca has heard comes four gags late.
*Do:* put Lesca's reaction (the pull going out of the jaw, the cloth stopping) directly after the three words. Then let the hall misread. The misreading is funnier once we know what was said.

**4. Coralie, the wittiest person in the book, is made to send the same bill seven times.**
"Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you" (1). "I've had sixteen and a shopping pad" (5). "I've had sixteen years of her saying nothing" (7). "I've asked you one question for sixteen years, and drawn you boxes for it" (16). Plus "What do you want, Maylis?" in 1, 1 again, 8 and 16. She is wonderful at work ("The emergency services can stand. Mine can't") and at the oven. At the kitchen table from chapter 4 to chapter 8 she is an invoice, and she stops being good company.
*Do:* keep the tally in chapters 1 and 16 only. In 4, 5 and 7 let the pad, the boxes and the two fingers on the wrist do it. They already do.

**5. Chapter 16 states the moral four times.**
Madame Darrigrand: "you were a kilometre away, fetching a man out of a dry hole." Maylis to Kévin: "I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me." Coralie: "That was Either, with the river in it." Maylis to Lesca: "I drove past the handle to be the one who did." Coralie's line is the joke carrying the moral, which is how the comparison does it. The other three are the author making sure.
*Do:* cut the line to Kévin (he did not ask, and his "I wanted six" is stronger unanswered). Cut the chapter 13 sentences "If she turned that handle … She wanted it not to be her" to the last five words.

**6. The chart scene has Maylis brief Coralie in a voice she does not own. (Ch. 8.)**
"'She's the flood,' Maylis said. 'There's another Peyrebarthe, where the river came through the bank on 11 December 2014. She's the mayor of it. Jeannot drowned in his hut that night, and that's what he is to her.'" The woman who wrote "Either" on a shopping pad delivers four fluent sentences of premise. The idea of the scene is funny (a haunting charted like a bedsore, three columns). The execution is an information block, and Coralie believes it without friction.
*Do:* Coralie fills the columns herself from what she has seen, reads them back, and Maylis gets one-word answers and the right hand. "I do this all week" stays.

**7. Three different things are forty-two and four are nineteen.**
"Put it in forty-two" and "what to do with forty-two people" sit forty lines apart in chapter 5. Nineteen is the turn the handle sticks at, the days of rain, the bags of sand ("'Nineteen,' Madame Etcheto said. 'That's not many.'") and Coralie's age at her first hand. Numbers are this book's punchlines. A punchline that means three things lands on none. When Maylis says "Forty-one" in the last steel room, a reader hears letters before heads.
*Do:* forty-two belongs to Jeannot's letter. Give the care home another count. Nineteen belongs to the handle.

**8. The association meeting through a car window stops as soon as it starts. (Ch. 8.)**
"'We can't shut the door,' the oldest member said. 'The president's on the other side of it.'" That is the best comic premise for a scene in the back half, and within thirty lines it has been handed over to the blackmail sheet and a count of hands. Compare the chapter 6 council, which builds through five turns before it goes dark.
*Do:* fifteen more lines of procedure through glass before the folder comes up: the wipers, a motion nobody can hear, a hand counted twice. Then the sheet.

**9. Josiane's rumour is one joke told three times, and the third is laboured.**
"A woman takes flowers to the wife because she's after the husband or because she's after the wife" (5). The same sentence in Larrère's mouth (6), which works because it has a sting. Then: "You can go three weeks without bread. Without water it's three days. She's just told him which he is" (7). A reader has to stop and assemble that.
*Do:* cut the bread line. The Lassalle sisters have already won the moment.

**10. Chapter 9 retells in summary what the reader has been given six times.**
"After that a prefect pinned a medal on her coat and read out a speech that didn't say whose order she had disobeyed. Seven councillors walked out on Larrère, and she was elected mayor in his place. A stone was put up in the mayor's parking space…" The night has already come through the prologue, the council statement, the file, the steel room, the gendarmes' page. What is new is the reflector, the one ring and the boot. The rest is twenty lines with nobody in them to be company, in front of the best switch in the book (Jeannot's six points).
*Do:* cut that paragraph and trim the approach to the bank, which the prologue owns.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie at the oven door**, chapters 1 and 8, and "'I'll be brief,' she said… 'The—'" in 16. The whole marriage is in the impression she cannot finish.
2. **Monsieur Daguerre's "Here"**: at bingo, at the sandbags, in the corridor ("I know you are"), at the prefect's date. And "He was asked in 2019, and he said Here."
3. **The chapter 6 council**: the pinned wrist, "timed like an egg", "I'm told I wave", two hands going up, "The chair votes once, Madame le maire", and Inguma coming along under the table past a row of shoes.
4. **Jeannot's numbering**, from "One. Those are my boots" through "And six. Bugger off" to No. 42, by hand, unregistered.
5. **"Either" on the handle** in chapter 11 and "Then it's shut", with Lesca's "apologies received" at the dead's table in chapter 12 as its pair. Each woman's comic form, used at the worst moment of her life, exactly once.
