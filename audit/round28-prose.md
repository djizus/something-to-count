# Round 28: Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full (41,900 words). Counts below are across all seventeen files.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.5.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the care home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the fields there was one man, at his duck hut, and no one came to tell him." (3) The sum is stated early and never changes, so every later scene can lean on it.
- **Tension: 7.** "On the sixth ring the rib caught and held. She had no breath to push with. The phone went on ringing in a hand she couldn't close." (11) From 9 onward it holds. Before that the tension is mostly administrative.
- **Progression: 6.** "Your river made up two days in an afternoon, so only the last night counts." (8) Chapters 4 to 8 keep re-stating the terms of the story, and the rule the climax depends on arrives as one asserted line.
- **Comedy: 7.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (6)
- **Characters: 7.** "'And six. Bugger off.'" (9) The villagers are alive from one line each. The two women are distinct in speech and blurred in narration, see fault 1.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'" (13) The bingo joke from 7 is carried into the flood without a wink, and it works.
- **Prose: 6.** "Larrère asked to speak. In this village he had been mayor until the spring after the flood. He was still president of the landowners' association. Any member could speak for three minutes on any item. Lesca had made that rule in her first month. It applied to every member. It had been needed for one." (3) Seven sentences, one length, one shape. Clean, exact, and in one gear for eight chapters.
- **The ending: 7.5.** "Then he stepped over the string in his rubber boots and began to dig." (16) It costs a death, a job and a marriage, and the last image is earned. The chapter before it explains too much.
- **Clarity: 5.5.** "That was Maylis's gate falling." (5) In the shared-body chapters the reader has to work out whose "she", whose hand and whose thought, sentence by sentence.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. I would have slowed in 4 and again in 8, and read 9 to 14 without stopping.

**Would I press it on a friend?** Not yet. I would have to say "it gets going at chapter 9", and a book that needs that sentence is a 7.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch on prose, by about a point and a half. Level with The Good Place in dialogue.**

1. **Crouch changes speed; this book has one speed until chapter 13.** Mean sentence length is 9.6 words, the median 8. Half of all sentences are eight words or fewer and 2 in 100 run past twenty-five. A Crouch page puts a forty-word run of panic next to a two-word line. Here the two-word line has nothing to stand against until 13 ("03:08. Six hundred metres to go. She ran."), where the same short sentences suddenly work because the scene is finally as fast as the syntax.
2. **Crouch puts the reader inside the fear; this narrator stands outside and then labels it.** The body is reported as gesture and prop for pages, and when feeling is needed it is named: "She was scared." (00, 13), "What scared her was Monday" (4), "Now she was more afraid of her own stairs." (5), "that frightened her more than asking did" (11). The one place the book does it Crouch's way is the rib: "Whatever had given in her side at the trapdoor now let her have a quarter of a breath at a time, so she took them fast." (13) That sentence is an 8. There are too few like it.
3. **Above Crouch, level with The Good Place: every speaker is identifiable without a tag.** Jeannot counts his grievances, Sarthou never contracts, Larrère announces brevity, Maïté cannot finish a sentence, Marie-Jo speaks to the wall. Crouch's secondary characters all talk alike. These do not, and the comedy lives in that.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. No scene in the shared body belongs to one woman (5, 6, 7, 12)

> "Lesca meant to say Either. Maylis had been giving that gap the same word for eighteen years, so the window was already on its way back up to meet it. One word, and Lesca could pass for her until noon, when the café would be full. / Maylis felt the mouth get ready for the word and stop." (5)

Chapter 5 opens in Maylis's head ("Maylis felt her breathe in for it"), moves into Lesca's in the café ("None of it had got her ready for the back of his neck", "She wanted him to go on arguing"), returns to Maylis at the soup, and on the stairs steps into a third: "Coralie had felt cold air go past her and nothing else." Chapter 6 then cuts to a room nobody in the chapter can see ("In the other village it was raining"), the only omniscient section in the book, and chapter 7 opens by giving the same information again (the chart, the number on the screen, "her thumb over it", twice in two pages).

This is the chapter where the premise first walks into the village, and it is the chapter where the reader is least sure who is thinking. The body convention ("the mouth", "the left hand", "her right hand") is rigorous and it is already a load. Adding a floating point of view on top is what drops clarity to 5.5. The convention also slips where the bodies are swapped: in 6 Maylis is driving the mayor's body, yet "The right hand came up and shut over her mouth", "Under Maylis's left hand the wrist pulled", "Maylis took her hand down."

**Do:** give every scene one owner. Chapter 5 is Maylis as passenger from first line to last: what Lesca thinks is read off what the body does (the eyes that will not leave Jeannot, the smile Maylis feels on the face). Lesca's interior in the café moves to chapter 10, where she is alone with it. Cut the Coralie sentence on the stairs; "Are you coming up?" does that work. Cut the omniscient insert in 6 and let 7 deliver the thirteen hours cold. Then run the article convention once through 6.

### 2. The rules are read out as minutes, three times, and the decisive one is asserted (4, 5, 8)

> "'Then here's Friday, and I'll say it once. Your river made up two days in an afternoon, so only the last night counts. If I'm in your village at 2:51 on Friday night, the water comes through your bank as it came through mine, and your bank goes. If I'm not, it holds.' / Nothing had stopped a word of that." (8)

By my count the book has ten rules: knowledge passes in the chair; whoever stays sitting drives; the owner keeps her right hand; Inguma fetches the visitor when it has finished counting; standing in the steel room only the truth comes out; pushing keeps you out of the chair; the visitor brings the river; only the last night counts; the one it cannot take home dies and plays bingo; sitting on Inguma stops it. Chapter 4 recites four as numbered points, chapter 5 demonstrates them again with commentary ("That's a day," "The owner keeps…"), chapter 8 recites them to Coralie as a three-column chart and then to Maylis in the chair. The comic form (a meeting with no one in the chair) is good once. By 8 it is a manual.

The rule the climax stands on, "only the last night counts", does not follow from "made up two days in an afternoon"; it is a convenience, and the truth-telling room is used to certify it. A room that stops lies cannot vouch for a prediction.

**Do:** cut the recited list in 4 to the two rules that have not been shown (2 and 3 already showed the driving and the right hand). Delete the chart recital in 8 down to Coralie's one question and its answer. Make "only the last night counts" something Maylis finds on the marker post on Saturday 5 December, where the scene is already standing ("Yesterday morning that same post had said a metre"), not something Lesca announces.

### 3. Paragraphs that explain what the scene has just shown (4, 5, 8, 10, 16)

> "She had written that word on the shopping pad on Tuesday, when she wanted the duck. It had always come when she needed it. It had never been true, and it wasn't true now. She did want one of them, badly." (4)

The word has just stuck in her mouth in front of the reader. Others of the same kind:

- "She believed the date. She wanted that gate opened on Friday afternoon as much as Lesca did. What scared her was Monday, when her own mouth would say so to the man who paid her." (4)
- "Keeping Lesca out meant having a better plan, and she didn't have one. Somebody had to tell the village. She would rather it was the one who signed things." (4)
- "Maylis had stood over that pad every morning for sixteen years and had never once got as far as a tick." (5)
- "Maylis stood at the counter. If she couldn't keep Lesca out on Friday, she wanted the river let onto the fields in daylight, by order." (8)
- "I wanted you in the house, and I thought I could have you without it, if I kept a landing between us." (10) The landing has said this for seven chapters.
- "'I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me.'" and "'At the gate I thought I could do better than you. I drove past the handle to be the one who did.'" (16) Chapter 13 showed both: "She wanted it not to be her", and nine rings against one, then "She drove past."

**Do:** delete each of these. In 16 keep "Forty-one", "Don't come again", "I won't", "It came out whole", and nothing between them that glosses 13.

### 4. One gear for eight chapters

> "The legs moved when she told them to. So did the left hand. The right hand lay on the blanket in a fist, and a fist was how it stayed. That one was Lesca's." (6)

"She" opens 517 of 4,327 sentences, "The" 483, "Then" 110 (one every 380 words). There are 86 narration paragraphs of six words or fewer ("She knew that smell." "She liked it." "He had." "Inguma got down."), one every 490 words, and most of them are doing the same job: a kicker. A kicker every page stops kicking.

**Do:** not ornament. Join sentences whose only relation is cause or sequence, so that the short line is kept for the place where something turns. Halve the one-line paragraphs in 1 to 8 and leave 13 exactly as it is.

### 5. Chapter 9 hands over the book's secret as a synopsis

> "After that a prefect pinned a medal on her coat and read out a speech that didn't say whose order she had disobeyed. Seven councillors walked out on Larrère, and she was elected mayor in his place. A stone was put up in the mayor's parking space, with Jean Castagnet's name cut above hers." (9)

The flood night in 9 is the fourth pass over ten minutes (prologue, the file in 3 and 6, now this; 10 and 14 make six). Up to the reflector going under it is the best-written of them. Then the narrator turns chronicler and lists what chapters 3 and 6 already gave. The voice changes from scene to record in the paragraph where the reader has just watched a man drown.

**Do:** end the memory at the boot ("Under the water the torch found the sole of a rubber boot."). Cut the medal paragraph. Keep the priest, in one line, because 10 needs him.

### 6. Fear is named, not given

> "For a week she had been afraid of the handle on that gate. Now she was more afraid of her own stairs." (5)

"scared", "afraid" and "frightened" appear eleven times and each time as a statement of account. The narrator otherwise refuses to state feeling, so these read as the author stepping in. The exception that proves it is "She was scared" in the prologue and again at 02:55 in 13, a deliberate echo, and it only lands because it is rare there.

**Do:** keep the two echoed lines. Replace the rest with what the rib, the hand or the breath does, which the book is good at when it tries.

### 7. Coralie speaks in one formula

> "'I've held hands for a living since I was nineteen, so I know when a hand is holding on, and I know when it wants to be let go.'" (4)

Her big speeches are built from the same three parts: a count of years, a credential, a verdict. "I've had sixteen years of her saying nothing, and I know it when I've got hold of it." (7) "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." (7) "'I do this all week.'" (8) "'I'm trained. I know what a coincidence is.'" (10) The narrator does the same for her: chart and pulse supply her comparison twelve times ("the voice she used for reading a dose off a chart", "the way she read from a patient's chart", "the way she counted a pulse against the clock", "for as long as she would have given a pulse"). She is the best-argued person in the book and the only one who never says anything clumsy, which makes her sound written.

**Do:** leave the credential in 4 and in 10. Elsewhere let her be short, or wrong, or let her see something a nurse would not.

### 8. Everybody is holding something, and everybody looks twice

> "He looked at the lit kitchen window, then at the woman at the bottom of the ladder." (15)

A trailing ", with …" phrase occurs 145 times; in 54 of them someone has an object in a hand, under an arm or on a knee ("with the coffee pot in her hand" in 4 and again in 5; Arlette "with her bag on her arm" in 10 and again in 14). "looked at" occurs 54 times, and the pair "looked at A, then at B" before a decision eight times, six of them in the last four chapters: the timer and the hands (6), the seven and the van floor (13), Coralie and Maylis (13), the coins and the suitcase (16), the loaf and the side (16), the string, the grass and the shovel (16). Reactions are also signalled by setting a thing down: "put … down" seventeen times (pot, pan, fork, spoon, glass, pens).

**Do:** keep Jeannot's look at the corridor ("He looked for two breaths. / 'I was at school with his son.'"), which is the one that carries a decision the reader cannot predict. Cut the other pairs and a third of the poses.

### 9. Counters doing the work of emphasis

> "'Your father slept on that platform with the gate locked shut, and he never rang me once.'" (11)

Jeannot says the same sentence two chapters earlier: "and he never rang me once" (9). "never once" or "never … once" appears nine times in six different mouths, the narrator's included. A year-count ("twelve years", "sixteen years", "eleven years", "eighteen years") appears thirty times, "twelve years" fourteen of them. "every" 56, "nobody" 56. Each is true to the story; together they are the author's stress mark, and they make Coralie, Lesca, Jeannot and Larrère sound like one person when they insist.

**Do:** one year-count per scene. "never once" for Coralie only, since being asked is her subject. Change one of the two "never rang me once" lines.

### 10. Usage that does not hold

- **Two Englishes.** "pavement", "rubbish bin", "cool boxes", "dressing gown", "fuelled", "My mum" on one side; "dish towel" (7 times), "baby carriage" (16), "truck" (10, 13), "the dump" on the other. Pick one.
- **Marker post or pillar.** The river is read on "the pillar" of the old gate in 3, 8 and 14 and on "the marker post" in 5, 7, 8, 9, 11 and 13. A reader cannot tell whether these are one object.
- **How many huts.** "One man" goes out on the fields throughout, but the white van goes "out along the track to the duck huts" (6) and the track runs "out to the hunters' huts" (11).
- **Sarthou's clock.** "'3:11. I looked at my watch. That is for the file.'" (13) against "'At 02:57 she left it. That is recorded.'" (16).
- **Forty or forty-one.** "She had carried his forty letters in her head since the first night" (5); she remembers signing for the forty-first.
- **Tag order.** "said Larrère", "said Marie-Jo", "said old Bordenave", "said Jeannot": seven inversions against some 300 in the other order. Make it none.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The call log.** "'Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. 02:51.'" (10), and Coralie's "'The phone doesn't say how long it rang. It says not answered.'"
2. **The reflector, twice in the same words.** "Low on its left-hand door a round reflector, screwed on at an angle, shone red." (9, 11), with "She looked at it, looked away at the marker post and looked at it again. It hadn't changed. She was awake."
3. **The evacuation, sentence for sentence.** The crab-walking wheelchair, "'Here.' / 'I know you are.'", "'Then they'll want the bins out'", Kévin counting five fingers in his own headlights, "She held it for the full half minute", and "'Forty-one,' Coralie said."
4. **The village's ways of speaking.** Jeannot's numbered points, Sarthou's uncontracted sentences, "I'll be brief", Maïté's broken ones, and the oven door, including the time it fails: "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" (16).
5. **The two best turns of phrase in the plot.** "That village hadn't been spared its flood. It hadn't had it yet." (3) and "The loudspeaker on the bank road said 'drowned him' after the square had finished hearing it." (14)

## 5. Chapter by chapter

- **00.** A woman, a bank, a handle she does not fit, and "It rang once." Nothing wasted. **8**
- **01.** The village in three windows and an oven door. The want-but-so-she-didn't pattern is laid down three times where twice would do. **7**
- **02.** Madame Etcheto, bingo with the dead, and a last line that is the premise: "'Come back across,' her mouth said. Maylis hadn't said it." **8.5**
- **03.** The best expository chapter: the file, the dates, the motion amended in the same ink. The committee paragraphs are where the one-gear rhythm first tires. **8**
- **04.** The meeting in the steel room is funny for a page and a rulebook for two. The chrysanthemums and Maïté's suitcase are the chapter. **6.5**
- **05.** The premise goes public and the point of view comes loose. The window going all the way down and the fight on the stairs are both first-rate. **7.5**
- **06.** Maylis as mayor; the hand on the timer is the best comic device in the book. The omniscient cut breaks it. **7**
- **07.** Bingo night runs long and its confusion is the reader's too. The landing ("'Which of you is that?'") is exact. **7**
- **08.** The slowest chapter: chart, recital, vote, recital. Ends well on the card. **6.5**
- **09.** The night itself, then six numbered insults from a living man. Lose the chronicle paragraph. **8**
- **10.** The chapter I would show someone. Three lines of explanation too many in Coralie's long speech. **9**
- **11.** "Either" at the handle, the reflector, the rings. The Hontaube call is the right joke in the right place. **8.5**
- **12.** The handbrake, and the mayor sending apologies to the dead. **8**
- **13.** The book at its bar. I would not change a sentence. **9**
- **14.** The confession travelling through the square "at walking pace", and "Somebody has to stack them." **8**
- **15.** Etcheto and Maïté are right. The removal from office in one sentence is a scene summarised; the timer deserved to ring. **6**
- **16.** The mud, letter forty-two, the second shovel. The steel-room exchange explains 13 back to the reader. **7.5**
