# Critic's report: Reader

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

I read as somebody who has never seen a floodgate, a French village council, a duck hut or a Basque night spirit, and who knows Crouch and The Good Place.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** "That village hadn't been spared its flood. It hadn't had it yet." From that line in chapter 3 I knew who could die, when, and who held the key.
- **Tension: 7.** "On the sixth ring the rib caught and held." Chapters 11 to 13 are a 9. Chapters 6 to 8 wait for a date that has already been fixed, and the threat in them is a file and a job.
- **Progression: 6.5.** "'Fifty-five under,' she said out loud, to the right hand. 'My file has the same.'" That is the third of seven readings of the marker post. The book goes back to the platform, the handle and the sum too often between chapters 5 and 11.
- **Comedy: 8.** "'And six. Bugger off.'" I smiled from the eggs in chapter 1 to the dealer's new line in chapter 16 and stopped only where the rules were being explained.
- **Characters: 8.5.** "'There's a key, and a handle, and Larrère, and a van. And then there's me, as a distance.'" Coralie, Jeannot, Larrère and Madame Etcheto are people I could pick out of a crowd.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 7.5.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" That one is perfect. The mark comes down for the bingo hall, where the joke sits on top of the book's biggest turn and hides it.
- **Prose: 7.5.** "It hit the floor like a full sack dropped off a trailer". I never stopped on a sentence. I did notice the same dozen phrases coming round.
- **The ending: 8.5.** "'She's being buried here on Tuesday. I want to dig the grave myself.'" Both women lose what they came for and neither is let off.
- **Clarity: 6.** "In the steel room Inguma got up to do its work. The plate rose under Lesca and took her heels off the floor." At that moment she is in a van, in a steel room and on top of a monster, and a phone is being counted. I read chapter 12 twice.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. From the end of chapter 5 nothing would have stopped me. The only place I could have put it down for good is the middle of chapter 3.

**Would I press it on a friend?** Yes, and I would tell them to read to the stairs at the end of chapter 5 before deciding.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, by a short distance, and all of the distance is clarity.** An 8 on this scale is a book I read as easily as I read Dark Matter. This one I read with a finger on the page in five places.

What puts it below:

1. **Crouch gives a reader one machine and three rules. This book gives eleven rules from four mouths.** Lie still and you are in (Kévin). Count your fingers (Kévin). Name it and give it things to count (Etcheto, then Arlette). The one it cannot take home dies and plays bingo (Etcheto). What one knows the other gets; stay sitting and you drive; the owner keeps her right hand; standing, only the truth comes out (Lesca, as an agenda). The visitor brings her river. Only the last night counts. Sit on it and it cannot take you home. In Dark Matter I never once asked what the box could do. Here I asked at chapters 7, 8 and 12.
2. **The Good Place lets me see the reversal at the second the character sees it.** "You're the water" is the same class of turn: the rescuer is the cause, and everything she predicted she brought. I worked it out the morning after, from a chart on a shopping pad.
3. **Crouch has the body in danger inside thirty pages.** Here the double is on stage after about 4,000 words, which is fast enough, but until chapter 11 the danger to Maylis is a resignation letter with no date on it. What kept me reading through the middle was the marriage, which the book does not treat as its engine until chapter 7.

What puts it level or above:

1. **The supporting cast is better than Crouch's.** His are functions. I would know Jeannot by his numbering, Larrère by his window, Sarthou by "I am reporting a line".
2. **Coralie is better than Daniela.** She wants something of her own, it has been behind the fruit bowl since March, and she takes it. I was afraid for this marriage in the way the brief asks, and the fear paid.
3. **The ending costs more than any Crouch ending.** Nobody resets and nobody escapes. The saved man does not say thank you.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. The book's biggest turn is delivered backwards: the joke first, the explanation after, the discovery never (chapters 7 and 8)

> She had nobody to say it to and no mouth to say it with.

> 'She wants water,' one of the Lassalle sisters said.

> *What. Since when. How do you know.*

That is the order I got it in. On the platform Maylis notices that the river stood still for the two days Lesca was out. The text does not say what she concludes. That evening she shouts three words I did not understand, the hall mishears them, Josiane makes them adultery, and Madame Etcheto undoes the one thing Maylis was trying to do. The scene carries six things at once: the silent barmaid, Jeannot's announcement, the fight for the jaw, the reveal, the mishearing, and a rescue that is a defeat. The reveal is the one that gets lost. I finally understood it at breakfast, read out as a chart in three columns.

This is the moment that should re-read the whole book, and I got it as minutes of a meeting.

**What I would do.** Let me have it on the platform, with her, on the Saturday afternoon. The post said a metre on dry boards on Friday; Lesca is back in her, it is raining, and the post says fifty-five. One plain thought in Maylis's head there. Then "You're the water" in the hall is something I already know, shouted at the worst moment to the wrong room, and the mishearing is funny on top of the knowledge and not in place of it. The chart in chapter 8 then shrinks to Coralie checking it, which is what she is for. Also say outright in the hall what Etcheto's help has cost: "Maylis felt the night go out with them" did not tell me that Lesca now had the body until morning.

### 2. The moment the whole book turns on goes by in four lines (chapter 11)

> 'Either,' Maylis said.
>
> 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. 'That's how the vote left it.'
>
> He was off the platform and down the steps before she could take it back.
>
> She lifted the handle off.

It is daylight. The tide is right. The one man who goes out on the fields is standing behind her. Her weight is on the handle. This is exactly the opening Lesca crossed a world to arrange, and chapter 16 convicts Maylis for wasting it, twice ("At 5 on Friday it was light", "That was Either, with the river in it"). The word I believed. Taking the handle off I did not. Jeannot has only walked to his van. Nothing has changed in the ten seconds since she leaned on it. I thought she was being stupid, and the book needs me to think she is afraid.

**What I would do.** Keep the word and keep it that short. Give me the ten seconds after it. She leans on the handle again; a hundred metres along the bank the car is still there with the folder on its seat and Kévin's name on the sheet under her letter; Jeannot's engine starts. Let her take the handle off as something she does, with the car in her eye. Then the verdict in chapter 16 lands on a choice I watched her make.

### 3. Too many rules, and three of them turn up on the day the plot needs them (chapters 2, 4, 8, 10)

> 'Your river made up two days in an afternoon, so only the last night counts.'

> 'So that's a fourth point, and I didn't have it on my list. Standing in this room, a woman can only say what's true.'

> 'I'm going to sit on it. It can't take me home while I'm sitting on its back.'

Each of these is announced by a character at the moment it becomes useful. The truth rule earns its keep many times over and is funny. "Only the last night counts" exists to put the calendar back after the two dry days, and I saw the hand that put it there. Sitting on Inguma is used once and produces the hardest page in the book to picture.

**What I would do.** Cut the sentence about the last night. I had already watched the river make up forty-five centimetres in a day; let Maylis draw the conclusion at the post. Keep "I'm going to sit on it" for the secretary's hand over her mouth, and in chapter 12 keep the consequences in the van (see fault 5). Give Kévin's instructions and Etcheto's to me further apart: chapter 2 is two lessons back to back.

### 4. Chapter 3 is where a reader stops (chapter 3)

> Marie-Jo Labat, Kévin's mother, turned a white kitchen timer to three and stood it in front of him. She had been Lesca's deputy mayor until a night in 2018, when she handed her sash back across this table in a plastic bag. Since then she had spoken to Lesca only 'through the chair', which meant to whoever was running the meeting.

The chapter opens with the best jolt so far, a woman waking in the wrong bed with the wrong hand, and ends on the best last line so far. Between them I was given a second village with its own history, all at once: the stone, the committee, the three-minute rule, the timer, the plastic bag, "through the chair", the verb, the State's new bank and the four houses, Darrigrand's step, the roof tile, the file, the gravel lake, the ponies, the landing. Eight sections. I had only just learned the first village.

The Darrigrand doorstep is the section where I started skimming. I did not know why I was there.

**What I would do.** Chapter 3 needs four things: the wrong bed, the stone, the file, the motion. Move Darrigrand's step to the rehearsal in chapter 10, where the square is being measured anyway. Let the gravel lake wait for Arlette's visit to the office. Tell me about the plastic bag in chapter 6, next to Kévin's cap, where I can see why.

### 5. Lesca's one great act is over before I know why she did it, and I cannot see where she is (chapter 12)

> The hand locked on the brake was the hand that had held the stair post against her. At the end of the headlights the reflector was red, and no nearer than it had been twelve years ago.
>
> 'I'm letting go,' Lesca said, with the mouth.

Three lines earlier: "She could hold it down for this one." Then she lets go. I think I know why. The hand that is stopping her from reaching the man is the hand that stopped her from reaching the bed, so she sees which of the two she came for. I had to build that myself, on a page where she is sitting on a steel plate in one room, fighting for a handbrake in another, while the rings of a phone are counted off between the paragraphs. The woman who has carried the book since chapter 3 leaves it in a subordinate clause.

And I did not understand what happened to her next. She lets go and arrives in the hall of the dead, not in her bed. Was she dying? What got her out? "Apologies received" is so good that I forgave it, but I did not follow it.

**What I would do.** Keep the fight in the van and keep it in her head only. Drop the cutaways to the plate and the heels. Give her one sentence, in her own council voice, of seeing what she is doing with that hand before she says she is letting go. One line in the hall to tell me why she is there: she sat on the thing that takes her home.

### 6. The same measurements, over and over (chapters 5 to 13)

> 'There's no both. His trapdoor is twenty-five minutes from that handle. When water comes through the bank, the bank has ten. Ten minutes against twenty-five.'

I was given twenty-five minutes against ten eight times in chapters 7 to 11, twice each in 7, 8 and 9. The handle sticks at nineteen in seven chapters. The forty houses are counted eleven times and the forty-two beds ten. The marker post is read seven times. Somebody stands on the platform in chapters 5, 7 (twice), 8 and 11 before the night itself.

I am a reader with no special knowledge and I needed the sum once, when it was walked in chapter 7, and once when it was lived in chapter 13. By the third telling I was reading the numbers without reading them, and the walk down the track in chapter 7 is the one stretch after chapter 5 where I was bored.

**What I would do.** Cut the sum from the steel room in chapter 8, from the second mention in chapter 9 and from chapter 10. In chapter 5, the test of the handle and the cut palm is the scene; the reading of the post before it can go.

### 7. Two heads in one paragraph, and a woman with no name (chapters 5, 7, 12)

> Lesca meant to say Either. Maylis had been giving that gap the same word for eighteen years, so the window was already on its way back up to meet it. One word, and Lesca could pass for her until noon, when the café would be full.
>
> Maylis felt the mouth get ready for the word and stop.

When both women are in one body, the telling goes into both, sometimes in the same paragraph. "The mouth", "the eyes", "the left hand" help. I still had to stop and ask whose thought I was reading in the café, at the soup and on the landing.

In the bingo hall the book gives up and calls her something else:

> The keyholder was serving at the bar, beside the big coffee pot.

For a line I thought a new character had come in.

**What I would do.** One head per section. In the café and at the soup, make it Maylis, the passenger, so that I watch Lesca from the one seat nobody else has. Lesca's feelings about the back of Jeannot's neck can come through what the face does and what Maylis feels it do. Call her Maylis in the hall; it is her body and everybody there calls her that.

### 8. The first domino has no reason, and the dates match by accident (chapter 1, chapter 8)

> On Monday afternoon the president of the association had rung and told her to raise it.

That order cracks the rib. The rib puts her on her back. On her back the thing can sit. That lets Lesca in, and Lesca brings the rain. Everything in the book follows from one phone call, and I was never told why Larrère made it. It drains Jeannot's water, which keeps Jeannot at home, which is the last thing Larrère wants on a duck night.

It also happens on the eve of 24 November, so that the rain starts on the same date as in the 2014 file "day for day". When I learned that the rain was Lesca, the matching dates stopped being fate and became luck.

**What I would do.** One line of motive for the order, in Larrère's mouth or Jeannot's. And let somebody notice the date: Lesca, standing, trying to say it is a coincidence and getting it out, or not.

### 9. Lesca's fall is told in one sentence while Maylis's gets eight scenes (chapter 15)

> On Tuesday the council gave Lesca three minutes to speak against her own removal, and she turned the timer back to nothing herself.

The timer is the best thing Lesca owns. It has cut Larrère off in the middle of "hydraulic" for eleven years, it rang over Maïté's verb, and its dial is what her right hand grabbed to stop his question. I wanted to be in the room when it was stood in front of her. I got a summary and a clever echo of the bingo card ("Lesca, Maylis. Surname first") that I only caught on the second reading.

**What I would do.** A short scene, half a page. Marie-Jo stands the timer in front of the mayor. Not to the wall, this time.

### 10. Things an outsider cannot parse

> 'It takes a small m,' Lesca said. Bordenave went over the M and made it a small one.

I did not get this. It depends on a spelling rule I do not have.

Others in the same class:

- **The map.** "The fields were a basin." Until the walk in chapter 7 I could not have drawn where the Port, the bank, the gate and the fields lie, or said why water let onto the fields does not run into the houses. One sentence in chapter 1 that puts the Port and the fields side by side behind the same bank, with high ground between them.
- **The crumbs.** Brown leaf has fallen out of the bed "most nights for twelve years", yet Coralie asks "When has it ever sat on me?" and the first visit seems to be this week. I think it came every night and had nowhere to sit. I should not have to think it.
- **"It would have been right for both lines."** In a book with a card that says *left* and a card that says *right*, I stopped to ask whether this was a pun. It reads as one.
- **Saint Catherine's Day, Adjudant, the hunters' seat.** None of these cost me more than a second, but they come in a first chapter that is already carrying an association, a president who is also the mayor, a keyholder and a registered letter.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Jeannot's numbering, whole.** "One. Those are my boots", the six points down the phone at 5 in the morning, "Jean's for the stone", letter No. 42 that "doesn't thank you", and the second shovel. He is the man two women ruin themselves over and he is worth it because he is ungrateful.
2. **The pulse on the landing (chapter 7).** "That's her. In this hand." Coralie telling two women apart with two fingers, then "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them."
3. **Room twelve to "Forty-one" (chapter 13).** The coat buttoned on the chair, the half minute on the wrist, the line through the last name, the hatbox on the wardrobe, the empty chair in the hall. I will remember this longest.
4. **The square after "I drowned him" (chapter 14).** The sentence going back through the crowd at walking pace, the man who claps, the child who asks, the medal case handed over the tape, and "Somebody has to stack them."
5. **"I'll be brief", from the first window to the two silences.** The window that goes all the way down, the oven door, the oven door that will not do its joke in chapter 16, and Larrère at the garden gate with nothing after the words. With it the kitchen in chapter 16: "That was Either, with the river in it", the grave as the answer to sixteen years of boxes, and the car door shutting on a pillow.
