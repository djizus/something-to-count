# Round 29: Characters

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "'I'd have had you. You answer. But what I'd have had is a river over forty-two beds.'" (8) The stake is a bed and a village at once, and one woman names both in three sentences.
- **Tension: 7.** "On the sixth ring the rib caught and held. She had no breath to push with." (11) Chapters 11 to 13 hold. Chapters 7 and 8 do not: the nights of pushing are told in summary.
- **Progression: 6.** "'I've tried for three nights to keep you out, and I can't. So I'm asking. Don't come on Friday.'" (8) She has already said "Then stop coming" (4) and "Then stay in your own bed on Friday" (8, the scene before). Larrère says shut three times (5, 8, 11). The middle makes the same move again.
- **Comedy: 7.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (6)
- **Characters: 7.** "They were the same height. Neither of them liked it." (4) Two leads and a third woman who are people. A village that is mostly one line each.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'" (13) The bingo joke is carried into the flooded corridor and is still the same joke.
- **Prose: 7.** "It had left a line on the wallpaper a metre above the pillow." (13) Plain and exact. The people share too many sentence shapes (fault 6).
- **The ending: 7.** "'It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you.'" (16) Maylis's ending pays. Lesca's is five endings in 768 words (fault 2).
- **Clarity: 6.** "'No. The owner keeps her right hand. I couldn't get it off you.'" (4) The rules of who drives which limb have to be chaired as a meeting, and the reader then has to track a jaw, a wrist and two hands through chapters 5, 7, 8 and 12.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. **Pressed on a friend?** Yes, on the one who liked The Good Place. The Crouch reader gets it with a warning about chapter 3.

Overall against the bar: 7. Below the bar by one point.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Above Crouch on the double.** In Dark Matter the second Jason is envy with a face. Here the second woman has the better job, the worse house, a mother who votes against her, and a form of speech of her own ("All those in favour." / "There are two of us."). The two are wrong in opposite ways: one decided in ten seconds and has lied by true sentences for twelve years, the other has never decided anything and calls it tact. A reader can want either life, which Crouch never manages.

**Above Crouch on the marriage.** Daniela is the prize. Coralie judges the contest, prefers the wrong woman, says so to her face ("I'd have you tomorrow"), and then refuses on behalf of the one who cannot speak. Nothing in Crouch's marriages is as adult as chapter 7's landing.

**Below The Good Place on the ensemble.** There, four people are each wrong in a way that changes under pressure, and they change each other by talking. Here the village is a set of catchphrases that fire on entrance (fault 7), and the two leads spend about a twentieth of the book in a room together (fault 3). The reversal is at that level, though: "You're the water" re-reads Lesca's rescue as the cause of what she came to prevent, and "Go to the hut first" re-reads her one generous act as her own night put right at Maylis's cost.

**Lens questions, answered.**
- Distinct: yes, within a line. "Nothing. Go back to sleep." against "Nothing. Go back to bed." is the whole difference done in one word.
- Wanted-for: Lesca clearly (the motion at the end of 3). Maylis by design only negatively until the last chapter; that is the book, and it works.
- Softened or excused: Maylis is not, at the hinge ("She wanted it not to be her"). Lesca is, once, and it is fault 2.
- Described from outside as women: no. Neither has a face; both have hands, a rib, a wrist. Nobody is looked at.
- Villagers a reader would miss: Madame Etcheto, Kévin, Jeannot, Sarthou, Marie-Jo, Monsieur Darrigrand with his broom. Not missed: Nadine, Bixente, the dealer, Bordenave, the secretary, Josiane.
- The relationship that carries the book: Maylis and Coralie. It pays in 16. Lesca and her Coralie pay short.
- Does the reader care who lives: yes. For Jeannot from the back of his neck in 5; for Coralie at the fire door in 13; for Madame Etcheto from her first line.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. Coralie has a double and never asks about her. (8; pays in 16)**
"'She's the flood,' Maylis said. 'There's another Peyrebarthe, where the river came through the bank on 11 December 2014. She's the mayor of it.'" Coralie's next question is "Since when?" This is the woman who wrote "I've left you on that landing eleven years" on the pad and carried it in her coat. She is told there is another village and does not ask where she is in it. The reader knows: over there she runs the care home and sleeps four steps away. Here she says "I'm a care assistant" twice (5, 11). The two women get a double each; Coralie is only the thing they swap. Her own want, Bayonne, is therefore asserted (the Ithurbide speech, given in 7 and again in 16 in nearly the same words) and never shown to come from anything.
*Do:* one exchange, at the soup in 5 or over the chart in 8, where she asks Lesca what she is over there, and Lesca, who answers, tells her: you run it, you call me Madame le maire, you took your pillow across the landing. Coralie then leaves in 16 knowing what she becomes beside a woman who decides and beside one who does not. That makes Bayonne hers. It is also the best joke not yet in the book: she got the promotion and lost the bed.

**2. Lesca gets her name back from a woman who does not know what she tried to do. (10, 15)**
"This Coralie knew. She was four steps away with her door open, but Lesca still meant to go. She had told her it was for the man." Lesca tells the square she drowned him, tells Madame Etcheto "Yes", tells Maylis "I wouldn't have stopped". She never tells her own Coralie that she was leaving for good, for the other one's bed, and that Coralie would have found her with her eyes open. She had priced that Coralie at "Nil" (8). Then: "'Good night, Maylis,' she said, and went into the spare room." It is the one place the book lets Lesca off. Chapter 15 has no room to do otherwise: a death acknowledged, a verb, Larrère at a gate, a removal from office in one sentence, and this, in 768 words.
*Do:* give 15 the length of 14. Put the council on the page. On the landing Lesca says where she was going and for whom, in her own form, as one more item. What Coralie calls her comes after that, or does not come.

**3. The two leads meet five times and memory does the rest. (4, 6, 9)**
"She had been given the reason in the chair. Lesca had put cameras on the gates and sent the film to the gendarmes, and Kévin had done six months in prison, for eels." And in 9: "Lesca wasn't in it yet, but the night of the flood came to Maylis anyway." The steel-room scenes come to about 2,600 words out of 42,000. Everything else one woman learns of the other arrives free. Two results. Lesca's worst acts as mayor (Kévin's prison, Marie-Jo's sash in a plastic bag) are a paragraph of report in someone else's chapter, while her grief is staged at length, so she is softened by staging. And the night she has hidden for twelve years reaches Maylis without Lesca having to stand in front of her own face while it plays.
*Do:* in 9, have Lesca come in while the night is running and be unable to stop it; "You rang once" then belongs there, and 12 can open on the hand. Give the prison a scene in 3: Kévin with his cap off, Marie-Jo telling the wall what it was for.

**4. The 5 p.m. "Either" has no inside. (11)**
"The river went by under the boards. / Her weight was on the handle. / 'Either,' Maylis said." This is the act the ending charges her with ("At 5 on Friday it was light... You had the key on your belt then"). She has said "Open" twice at the counter (8), Kévin has released her that afternoon ("Don't keep a gate shut for me"), and she has fitted the handle of her own accord. Then one question from Jeannot undoes her, and the reader is given a river and a line break. It reads as the plot needing the gate shut.
*Do:* no explanation. Let the catch take one more tooth under her weight before he speaks, so her body has started. Then let her see the two things in front of her, the man whose cellar it is and the car a hundred metres off with the folder in it, and have the word arrive the way it arrives at the shopping pad.

**5. Jeannot and Maïté are never in a room together. (4, 16)**
"'I saw the wardrobe.'" The whole book is spent on getting this man out of a hole, and nobody on the page loves him. His wife keeps a packed suitcase over the bed and another man's folder under the bread; his widow, over there, held the phone that night. The reader's wish for him to live rests on café comedy and on Lesca looking at his neck. When Maïté takes the bus, a marriage ends that was never shown.
*Do:* one scene. In 4, he comes home for his lunch as Maylis is leaving with the chrysanthemums in the sink behind her. Three people, a pot of flowers, and two bowls on the rack.

**6. The women refuse and tally in one voice. (10, 11, 13, 14, 16)**
"Don't tell me now" (Lesca's Coralie, 10). "Don't tell me now" (Maylis's Coralie, 11). "Don't tell me what he was" and "Don't give me the minutes" (13). "Don't give me the measurement" (16). "I'm not asking you why. I don't want to be told" (Arlette, 14). And everybody counts their years in the same cadence: "Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer", "I've had sixteen and a shopping pad", "Eleven years I've been timed like an egg", "I've paid for forty-one registered letters", "I've known that for twelve years". The two Coralies diverged twelve years ago, one into a director's office and a secret; they speak identically, and so does Arlette.
*Do:* keep the refusal for Lesca's Coralie alone. Not asking is her twelve years. Take it from the Coralie who asks, and from Arlette. Let the director speak in instructions ("Madame le maire will sit down" is already that) and the assistant in questions.

**7. Most villagers are a catchphrase that fires on entrance. (throughout)**
"I'll be brief" eight times. Daguerre's "Here" in 7, 11, 13 and 14, and reported in 10. Jeannot's "One." in six chapters. Sarthou's "I shall". Maïté's broken sentence. Josiane's rule ("because she's after the husband or because she's after the wife") is said in 5 and then given word for word to Larrère in 6. Where the tic breaks, a person appears: Larrère's "I'll be brief" followed by nothing (15), Coralie's oven door stopping at "The—" (16), Jeannot's count falling silent at the dip. Where it does not, there is nobody: Nadine holds the corridor at the climax and her line is another character's ("Ask Nadine. I only lift them").
*Do:* merge the dealer into Bordenave. Give Nadine one sentence of her own before 13. Cut the "Here" in 11 so that 13's is the second, not the third.

**8. Maylis is never angry. (6, 8)**
"'I'll lock up,' Maylis said." That is her answer to having held the stair post against her own legs while another woman tried to take her to bed with Coralie. She washes two clean bowls, and next morning goes to Larrère to take a sentence back. With Lesca, three nights later, she is asking politely. She has fear and shame in quantity; the book rations her fury to three words at a bar. The stairs are not named between the two women until the last page ("And you know what you went up my stairs for").
*Do:* in the first meeting of 8, standing, where only true things come out, let the true thing that comes out be ugly. One sentence.

**9. Lesca's turn in the van is reasoned for her. (12)**
"But the motion she had written at her own kitchen table was for a seat and a bed. There was no man in it." Then: "This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel." Then: "She had picked. She could be trusted with the rest of the night". Three paragraphs of argument inside one ring of a phone, at the moment the co-lead reverses. The image does it alone: "the reflector was red, and no nearer than it had been twelve years ago."
*Do:* keep the wheel, the brake hand, the reflector and "I'm letting go". Cut the reasoning between.

**10. The Larrère on Maylis's side is the blackmailer the plot needs. (6, 11, 13)**
"'That's prison, for eels.'" Over there he is a man: "Eleven years I've been timed like an egg. I have sentences in that register with no ends on them." Here he is a folder held against a window, and he carries the obstruction that would otherwise be hers. His one human fact, the back lane, is seen as headlights. "'He stays dry as long as you do what you're told'" is said from the wife's kitchen and nobody in the book looks at what that sentence means about him.
*Do:* let Maylis hear it. One beat in 13 where she understands why he wants that man in his hut at night, before she drives.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The landing, chapter 7.** Two fingers on the wrist, the left hand on the pillow. "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them."
2. **"Because you'd have told me." (10)** And the whole of that scene: the phone log, the two answers, the landing kept on purpose.
3. **Kévin's hand.** "'Five,' he said." (13) and "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much." (16)
4. **Lesca at the table of the dead. (12)** "The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies." Jean putting a grain of corn on his second card. Her father not looking up. And its echo: "Twelve. One, two. / Somebody pulled a chair in to a table." (14)
5. **The oven door.** Set in 1, repeated in 8, broken in 16 at "The—". With it, the eggs, and "I know that word. I've cooked it."
