# Critic's report: Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. About 41,900 words.

Counts below are from the text. "Narration" means paragraphs that do not open on speech.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Forty houses, forty-two beds, a car in the yard of the care home and a van at the end of a track." (10) The sum is exact and the book pays it.
- **Tension: 8.** "The phone rang a second time. One breath to a ring was all the rib would give her." (11) Chapters 11 to 13 hold as hard as a Crouch set piece.
- **Progression: 6.** "'Then stop coming'" (4), "'Then stay in your own bed on Friday'" (8), "'Don't come on Friday'" (8). The same request three times across five chapters of push, fail, push.
- **Comedy: 7.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (6)
- **Characters: 8.** "'It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you. I was in no danger, and I'll say so to anyone.'" (16)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'It's Saturday.' 'Then they'll want the bins out,' he said." (13) Said in a flooding corridor, and it costs the scene nothing.
- **Prose: 7.** "Lesca didn't know there was another half. Lesca filled the spoon." (5) An exact observation, then a gloss on it, with the name twice in eleven words. That is the book's prose in one line: precise, and not yet easy.
- **The ending: 7.** "Then he stepped over the string in his rubber boots and began to dig." (16) The last page earns an 8. Chapter 15 gives half the ending away in summary.
- **Clarity: 6.** "The gate would have gone up at 3." (13) I read it four times and still cannot say whose reckoning it is.

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. From chapter 9 I did not stop.
Would I press it on a friend? Yes, with a warning that chapters 5 to 8 are work.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, by a margin that a line edit and three rewritten scenes would close.**

- **Below Crouch on ease.** His reader never has to parse. Here, in the three chapters where the premise is most in play (5, 7, 12), the reader must assign an owner to every hand, jaw and boot. Crouch does shared identity in the first person and lets "I" carry it. This book does it in the third person with two women called "she", and solves the problem by writing "Lesca" 41 times in the 2,869 words of chapter 5.
- **Below Crouch on pace.** He changes gear hard: a page of one-line paragraphs, then a paragraph that runs a page. This narration has one gear. Mean sentence length is 11.2 words. No chapter falls outside 10.0 to 12.9. Only 3% of narration sentences reach 25 words. The council table and the breaking bank are told at the same pulse, so chapter 13 has nowhere to accelerate to except fragments.
- **Above Crouch on objects, level with The Good Place on the running gag.** Where the narrator keeps quiet, things carry the meaning: the shopping pad's boxes, the kitchen timer, the car window lowered "a few centimetres", the reflector "screwed on at an angle", "The cap stayed on." The gags come back changed, as that series does it: the oven door three times, Daguerre's "Here", "Put it in forty-two". The prologue's sentences return word for word in 13 for the other woman, and that is better than anything Crouch does with repetition.
- **Below The Good Place on trust.** That series lets a stomach ache state the ethics. This narrator states them: "She wanted it not to be her." (13)

Formula tells of the cheap kind are absent. There are no dash asides, no semicolons, five colons in narration, no stock intensifiers, few figures of speech (about sixty comparisons in the whole book). What remains are four habits: a uniform rhythm, a narrator who explains, a closing line on every scene, and "never", "nobody" and "for N years" doing the work of emphasis.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The turn is argued where it should be done. Chapter 12.**
"Twelve years ago she had let his phone ring once and then done what she had come to do. This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel. / And the woman on the brake had said the words tonight with no Either after them. She had picked. She could be trusted with the rest of the night, if somebody told her one thing."
The reversal the whole book is built for arrives as three paragraphs of the narrator's reasoning, between the eighth ring and "I'm letting go", in a stalled van with a river coming. Each step is something the reader already holds: the motion from 3, the single ring from 9, the Either from 11. Shortly after, the dead hall is introduced with another rule read aloud: "Inguma hadn't taken her home. She had held the wheel against it for a whole ring, and this was where that put her."
Do: keep what she sees. A dead engine, a hand that will not open, 02:53 on the dashboard, the reflector "no nearer than it had been twelve years ago". Keep one thought, the only new one: "There was no man in it." Cut the rest and go to "I'm letting go."

**2. The narrator gives the verdict on the choice before the consequence arrives. Chapter 13, then 16.**
"If she turned that handle, a man drowned and her hand had done it. If she went on, whatever the river did behind her was the river's doing. / She wanted it not to be her."
The scene had already shown it: three steps back towards the lamp, then her back turned on it. The gloss tells the reader what to think of her several pages before room twelve can. When she says it to Kévin in 16 ("I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me") the reader has heard it, and the line that should be the book's hardest is a repeat.
Do: cut those two paragraphs in 13. Let 16 be the first time it is said, and let her be the one to say it.

**3. Lesca's fall is two sentences long. Chapter 15.**
"On Tuesday the council gave Lesca three minutes to speak against her own removal, and she turned the timer back to nothing herself. Eleven hands went up, and when the secretary's pen stopped at 'Madame le—', Lesca said, 'Lesca, Maylis. Surname first.'"
The voice changes to synopsis. The timer has been the comic instrument of her power since chapter 3, and the moment it is turned on her is reported, in a chapter of 768 words against an average near 2,500. This is the cost of the ending for one of the two women.
Do: play it as a scene of a page. Larrère on his feet with nothing to say, Marie-Jo choosing where to look, the timer, the hands.

**4. The driven-body convention costs the reader in the chapters that matter most. Chapters 5, 7, 12.**
"Lesca unlocked the box with the key from the belt, fitted the handle and began to turn it with the left hand. The chain came up wet. Lesca counted the clicks of the catch as it came." (5)
"Lesca didn't slow down for it... Lesca walked through it with the water over the ankles of the boots... Lesca walked her up onto it... Lesca didn't step off." (7)
Two things go wrong together. The name replaces the pronoun in every sentence (41 times in chapter 5, 32 in chapter 7). And the definite article, which is right for the contested parts, spreads to things nobody is contesting: "the belt", "the boots", "the hood", "the teeth". The reader stops at each noun to work out whose it is.
Do: keep "the mouth", "the jaw", "the left hand", "the eyes" for the moments of contest. Give the clothes back to Maylis ("her belt", "her boots"). Where Lesca acts for a whole paragraph, name her once and tell the rest from the passenger's side: "her legs took her down off the bank".

**5. The chair is used as a chute for backstory. Chapters 4, 6, 9.**
"Maylis knew why. She had been given the reason in the chair." (6)
"The night before, in the chair, before she stood up, she had been given one of Lesca's afternoons." (4)
Whenever Maylis needs to know something from the other village, the narrator says after the fact that it "had been given". The passive has no giver and the rule was never set. In 6 it delivers Kévin's prison, the cameras and Marie-Jo's sash in one paragraph in the middle of a scene. In 9 it delivers the central night of the book, which then ends in a précis of things the reader has had since chapter 3: "After that a prefect pinned a medal on her coat... Seven councillors walked out on Larrère, and she was elected mayor in his place. A stone was put up..."
Do: make it one of Lesca's numbered points in chapter 4, so that it is a rule. Then drop the apology each time. Cut the précis paragraph in 9 and end the night on the boot.

**6. The certifying gloss. Throughout; densest in 5 and 6.**
"Nobody in Peyrebarthe had ever heard Maylis's mouth forbid anything." (5)
"Maylis had never smiled at Jeannot Castagnet in her life, and the whole café was watching her do it." (5)
"Maylis had never kept anybody waiting in her life, so she took her time over it." (6)
"Nobody at that table had heard him finish a sentence since 2015." (6)
After a gesture, the narrator certifies that it is unprecedented and gives the count. "Never" appears 41 times, "nobody" 59, a span of sixteen, eighteen, twelve or eleven years 35 times. By chapter 6 the beat is predictable. A second formula sits beside it, the simile from the character's job: Maylis reads a file "the way she read a job sheet", writes a plan "the way she wrote out any job", works out a rescue "the way she worked out a job"; Coralie counts, reads and waits as for a chart, a dose or a pulse five times. A third opens the book: chapter 1 explains the same refusal in the same syntax three times ("so Maylis didn't ask", "So she wrote *Either*", "so she didn't knock on it") before Coralie explains it a fourth time aloud.
Do: one certifying sentence a chapter, kept where the count is news ("Until tonight it had never been off her belt"). One job simile per character in the book.

**7. The book repeats its own facts. Throughout; chapter 10 for the clearest case.**
"She had seen him, eleven days ago, on a stool in a café... She had stood there smiling at him with another woman's face, and all she had found to say to him was a bill for a tank of diesel." (10)
That is chapter 5 told again. The Sunday of the chrysanthemums is retold by Larrère, Josiane and Coralie in 5, by Jeannot in 9 and by Larrère in 11. "Forty houses" comes 11 times, "forty-two" 13, the window's "few centimetres" 7. "Rib" comes 52 times and is given a will of its own ("the way the rib wanted it done", "The rib let her have an hour of it", "Her rib let her walk, and no faster"). Because every fact is a refrain, the true refrains lose force.
Do: choose five and say everything else once. Mine would be *No one came to tell him*, 2:51, *I'll be brief*, *Either*, and *What do you want, Maylis?* In 10, cut the closing recap to the two things that are new: the back of his neck, and "The man on the stone had never had the time."

**8. One rhythm for everything.**
"The bank was her job too. She knew it from one end to the other... The fields were a basin. They were made to take the river through that gate when it ran too high and to give it back on the next tide. The Port had nowhere to put a river." (1)
39% of narration sentences have eight words or fewer. 497 sentences begin with "She". 87 paragraphs are a single sentence of six words or fewer ("She was scared." "She liked it." "She knew that smell."), and several of those name a feeling the scene was already giving. In the quiet chapters the result reads as a primer, or a translation. In 13 the only way up is the verbless fragment ("Torch up. Right arm across her side."), a register the book uses nowhere else, along with a "you" the narrator has barely used before ("the kind you turn by hand", "the way you carry a child").
Do: in 1, 3, 6, 10 and 14, join runs of explanation into longer sentences by subordination ("because", "which", "where"), not by "and". Keep the short line for 11 to 13, where it then means something.

**9. Every scene ends on a line made to be quoted, and Coralie speaks in the narrator's sentences. Chapters 7, 10, 16.**
"I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." (7)
"I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it." (16)
"For sixteen years Maylis had sat down and said nothing, in the wrong kitchen." (6, narrator)
Each is good. At one a scene, the reader hears the scene winding up for it. Jeannot, Larrère, Sarthou and Maïté each have a way of talking that is theirs. Coralie's three long speeches are composed paragraphs with balanced clauses, delivered at midnight on a landing or with a river in the building. She is the one the reader most needs to hear think aloud.
Do: let one scene in three stop a beat early, on something unshaped. Give Coralie what Maïté has: a sentence she cannot finish.

**10. One word for several things, several words for one, and two dictionaries.**
- "Box" comes 44 times for eight objects: the steel room, the tick boxes, the handle box, the cool boxes, the confession box, the first-aid box, the bread box, the hatbox. The village joke ("Still in the box, were you?") depends on the reader knowing which.
- "Gate" is the floodgate, the "old gate", the "platform gate", Arlette's gates and a garden gate. "She came down and locked the platform gate behind her" (11) falls in a scene about whether *the* gate is open. In 13, "Maylis had the handle of the chair in her left hand" puts a wheelchair in the two words the book has reserved for the crank and the steel room.
- The land has three names: "flood fields" (8 times), "low fields" (2), "the village field" (3), plus "the lake". I could not tell whether Lesca's lake lies on the flood fields. The third picture in chapter 2 ("grey water... where the low fields should have been") reads as a flood and turns out, three chapters on, to be a gravel pit.
- The gauge is "the red line on the pillar", "the marker post" and "the marker on the pillar of the old gate". The track leads to "the duck huts" (6) and "the hunters' huts" (11) on fields where "the one man who went out" has the only hut. Bread comes from "a crate" (4) and "its box" (16). Coralie keeps "a resident's chart" (8) and "a patient's chart" (10).
- Spelling and most vocabulary are British (colour, fuelled, pyjamas; torch 29 times, pavement, rubbish bin, cool boxes, mum). Against that: "dish towel" 7 times, "truck" 3, "baby carriage" 2, "the dump".
- Times: the 24-hour screen readings are consistent and good. Narration is bare ("At 6", "at 9") except "2:51 a.m." (1), "6:10 a.m." (5) and "4 a.m." (13).
- The handle: "Eighteen turns lift the gate a hand. It sticks at nineteen" (8, and 5 agrees). In 11 the first tooth is already taken and "Eighteen more turns would lift it a hand. The nineteenth would need her shoulder", which makes it the twentieth.
- Opaque lines: "'Nobody gives it that much to sleep, Maylis.'" (3). "Maylis felt the night go out with them." (7). "One Either, and Lesca could pass for her until noon" (5). "Down in the Port a rocket went up from the bank" (15): a firework or a flare?
Do: one name per thing, one dictionary, and a pass on those four lines.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Jeannot's phone call in six points** (9), through "And six. Bugger off." and the line after it: "A man had just sworn at her in six points, and he was alive."
2. **Chapter 13 from "It wasn't loud" to "Forty-one."** The corridor, "Here." / "I know you are.", Kévin counting five fingers in his own headlights, rooms nine, ten and eleven open and twelve shut, Coralie drawing the last line through the list.
3. **The oven door, all three times** (1, 8, 16), and above all the third, where the impression stops at "The—".
4. **The square after the confession** (14): the sentence travelling back "row by row, at walking pace", the man who claps beyond the last loudspeaker, "A child asked what the lady had done. Somebody told the child.", the young man from Pau with nothing on his sheet, and "Somebody has to stack them."
5. **The last page** (16): letter No. 42 that "doesn't thank you", the second shovel "where her father had kept it", and the final sentence.
