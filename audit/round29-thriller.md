# Critic report: Thriller

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** The stake that frightens is written down in chapter 3: "*That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*" For three chapters after that the working pressure is a personnel file: "'That's prison, for eels.'"
- **Tension: 7.** At its best it is as tight as anything in Dark Matter: "Lesca took the body up one stair. Maylis's arm pulled it back down." It is not held. After chapter 8 nobody is hunting anybody, and the book waits for a date.
- **Progression: 6.** Three attempts to get the gate opened by order end in the same place: "'Four to three,' Larrère said. 'It stays shut.'" The book's biggest secret then arrives without anyone doing anything to get it (fault 1).
- **Comedy: 8.** "'Opened what? There's a name over it. It reads that she opened him.'"
- **Characters: 8.** "'That's her,' she said. 'In this hand. I've had sixteen years of her saying nothing, and I know it when I've got hold of it.'"
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** In a flooded corridor, in the dark: "'Here,' he said." / "'I know you are.'" The joke from bingo night comes back as a roll call and neither register gives way.
- **Prose: 8.** "It wasn't loud. It sounded like a truck tipping out a load of gravel a long way off, except that the sound didn't stop."
- **The ending: 8.** "'I know what you picked. I was the other one.'" Maylis pays in full. Lesca pays in a subordinate clause (fault 9).
- **Clarity: 6.** "'Every night she crosses, mine does one more day of her flood.'" The book's own river does not obey that sentence (fault 2).

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. From the end of chapter 10 to the end of 13 I could not have stopped.
**Press it on a friend?** Yes, on the friend who reads Crouch for the marriage. Not yet on the one who reads him for the engine.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, by about a point, as a thriller. Level or above in three places.**

- **Below: the threat curve.** Crouch escalates the pursuer to the last page. Dark Matter goes from one rival Jason to a hundred. Here the rival is at her most dangerous in chapters 3 to 7, when she wants the bed. Coralie disarms her on the landing in 7 and pins her wrist in 8. For the last third Lesca is a deadline.
- **Below: the rules.** Crouch gives two or three rules early and then uses them against the hero. This book has nine (stay seated, the owner's right hand, the jaw, the counting, truth when standing, memories that leak, the river, the hall of the dead, counting at walking pace). Several appear when the plot needs them, and the one the climax depends on cannot be worked out by the reader.
- **Level: chapter ends.** 00, 02, 03, 05, 08, 11 and 13 end as a Crouch chapter ends, on one short fact that changes the next page. "It rang once." "The arms of the chair were warm." "'Forty-one,' Coralie said."
- **Above: the wife.** Daniela in Dark Matter is fought over. Coralie takes a pulse, names both women and refuses the one she prefers. No Crouch spouse does as much.
- **Above: the choice.** Crouch's endings rescue the family. This one lets the hero choose, shows that her choice was a way of not choosing ("She wanted it not to be her"), and takes the marriage for it. The reversal that re-reads the book, the rescuer as the flood, is of the kind The Good Place builds. It is under-lit on the page (fault 3).

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The secret is handed over, not taken. (Chapter 9; also 4 and 6.)**
"Lesca wasn't in it yet, but the night of the flood came to Maylis anyway, the one Lesca had gone over every night for twelve years, until it was worn as smooth as a stone step."
The whole book has been asking whether she knew he was out there. Maylis crossed for a day to read the file and got nothing. She asked in the chair and got "'That's not a no.'" Then the answer arrives as a dream, in five hundred words of summary, while she lies still. The same free transfer happens in 4 ("she had been given one of Lesca's afternoons") and 6 ("She had been given the reason in the chair"). Information crosses whenever the plot wants it and costs nothing.
*Do:* play it as a scene in the steel room, where a woman on her feet can only say what is true. That rule was built for this moment and is not used for it. Maylis makes her answer standing, the twelve-year sentences stick in Lesca's mouth, and what comes out is the van, the reflector and the single ring. Give it a price (see fault 2). Then cut the other two free transfers or put them under the same rule.

**2. The river's rule cannot be computed, so the Friday vigil has no clear stake. (Chapters 7, 8, 9, 11, 13.)**
Chapter 8 states the rule as one day per crossing. But after two nights of keeping Lesca out the river is back on her page within hours: "'Fifty-five under,' Lesca said out loud, to the right hand. 'My file has the same.'" So the two nights bought nothing. Then: "'One night. That's all I have to keep you out for.'" On Thursday night Maylis lies still and lets her in, with no reason given. By 5 on Friday the river is already at her knees, before any Friday crossing. At 02:55: "She had put Lesca out, and it had come too late: the river was through the bank already." A reader doing the sum cannot tell what staying awake until 4 was meant to save.
*Do:* state one rule the reader can check and keep to it. Say plainly at the post that the river made up two lost days in a morning. Then make Thursday the night she lets Lesca in on purpose, to get the truth in fault 1, and wakes to orange. The secret costs her the river's last day. That closes both faults with one scene.

**3. The midpoint reversal gets three words and a mishearing. (Chapter 7.)**
At the marker post: "She had nobody to say it to and no mouth to say it with." Then, across a bar: "'You're the water.'"
This is the turn that should make the reader go back to chapter 1: the woman who came to save the village is what is drowning it. The page hides the moment Maylis works it out, and the hall takes the line as a request for a drink. The explanation comes a chapter later as a chart at breakfast. The mishearing is a good joke and should stay. The reader must not mishear too.
*Do:* give the sum at the post in one plain sentence (two dry nights with her out, one night with her in, and the river on her page by noon). Then the three words land on a reader who already knows.

**4. After chapter 8 Lesca is no longer a danger. (Chapters 7 to 11.)**
"'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak.'" / "'I do this all week,' she said."
Both are among the best moments in the book and both remove the fear. Once Coralie can tell them apart by a wrist and will hold the left hand down herself, nothing Lesca does in that house can frighten. Lesca does not drive the body again from Sunday the 6th until 02:52 on the 12th. "A whole life with one hand" is said in the chair and not shown.
*Do:* keep both refusals. Put Lesca in the body once more between the 6th and the 11th, outside the house, where Coralie cannot pin her, and let her do one thing that cannot be undone. She signs things. The dated resignation, or the word "open" at the association vote with Kévin's name on the glass, would do it. The reader should reach Friday afraid of her as well as of the water.

**5. The rival argues herself out, and the price she named is waived. (Chapter 12.)**
"She could stay sitting in this van until the river came over it. As long as she held the wheel, nobody was going down that track at all."
The fight by ring count is the best action in the book. It ends in three paragraphs of reasoning. Then the hall of the dead, promised since chapter 2 and shown with her name on the card in 8, lets her go for a line of committee wit: "'The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies.'" She had written down what staying would cost her: "Nil." She pays three hours on a stretcher.
*Do:* cut the reasoning to the act. The handbrake and "'Go to the hut first'" carry it. In the hall, do not let her fingers open by will. Keep her in the chair until the voice calls "Twelve" and somebody else pulls a chair in, and only then let her stand. Her seat is paid for, she hears who paid, and chapter 15's "they called twelve" has something behind it.

**6. The first half of chapter 10 is slack two chapters before the water.**
"He read it to her off his sheet, from the anthem to the photograph."
Arlette in the doorway and the taping of the stage are plants for chapter 14 (the cross, the kiss, eight chairs, the medal). They take half a chapter at the point where a Crouch reader is counting hours. The second half, Coralie and the call list, is the best quiet scene in the book and arrives late.
*Do:* halve the stage rehearsal. Keep the eight strips of tape and "'The others have died since then, in those beds.'" Open the chapter closer to the landing.

**7. The attempts through channels do not escalate. (Chapters 5, 8, 11.)**
"'Then it should have gone last Monday.'"
Asking Larrère in 5 makes things worse: Jeannot decides to be in his hut. The vote in 8 makes things worse: Jeannot's hand goes up. The Hontaube call opens the chapter of the night itself and changes nothing. It is a funny page with no consequence, in the one chapter that cannot spare one.
*Do:* cut it to two lines, or make it cost her something (the call reaches Larrère, and that is why he is parked on the bank at 5).

**8. The decisive moment asserts something that is not so. (Chapter 11.)**
"He was off the platform and down the steps before she could take it back."
This is the true choice of the book: daylight, the man above ground, her weight on the handle, and "'Either.'" Madame Darrigrand names it in 16. But she can still take it back. The handle is on and Jeannot is at his van on the bank with the engine running. She lifts the handle off with no reason given. The reader shouts at the page for the wrong reason.
*Do:* do not add a reason. Add the gap: one beat in which she could turn it and does not, with him still on the bank. Then his lights go down the track and it is too late in fact.

**9. Lesca's cost is told in a sentence. (Chapter 15.)**
"On Tuesday the council gave Lesca three minutes to speak against her own removal, and she turned the timer back to nothing herself."
Eleven years of office go in a line. Chapter 15 is the thinnest in the book and sits between the two endings that matter.
*Do:* play the timer as a short scene, or fold 15 into the end of 14 so that Maylis's Sunday follows the confession directly.

**10. A line at the climax that cannot be read. (Chapter 13.)**
"The gate would have gone up at 3."
It means "if I had turned back at the dip". On the page it sits after Jeannot's "3:20" and reads as a contradiction.
*Do:* say whose hands and which turning.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **"It rang once" and the nine rings.** The prologue's last line, and chapter 9's answer to it: Maylis lets the phone ring nine times and is sworn at in six points by a living man.
2. **The stair post.** Chapter 5, from "Lesca put the left foot on the first stair" to "'Are you coming up?'" This is where the reader is afraid for the marriage.
3. **"Either" on the platform at 5 in daylight, and Madame Darrigrand saying so in the mud.** The fatal moment is ten hours before the water. Keep it that small.
4. **Chapter 13 from the dip to "Forty-one."** The stuck van blocking Jeannot's on the way back, the doors of nine, ten and eleven open and twelve shut, the hatbox on the wardrobe, Kévin counting five fingers in his own headlights.
5. **The bill.** "'I was the other one.'" "I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me." Letter 42, which does not thank her, and the second shovel.
