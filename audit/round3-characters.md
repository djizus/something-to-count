# Critic's report: Characters

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `25.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

In this report "Maylis" is the commune's employee and "Lesca" is the mayor, as the narration names them.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." (ch. 2) The sum is concrete and named from the second chapter. It stays the mayor's sum until chapter 19; for eighteen chapters Maylis's own stake is a staff file and forty euros a month.
- **Tension: 6.** "He asked for the register before she had blotted it, and read all three entries through, twice, with his finger under the lines." (ch. 8, last line) Too many chapters in the first half end on a comic button and not on a threat. From chapter 18 it is a 9.
- **Progression: 6.** "'Counts her fingers on the hour. Signs for a parcel like a prefect. Paces a bank through four gardens and puts a bench on the gendarme's cabbages.'" (ch. 16) Lafitte's list is the book admitting that chapters 5 to 13 are one move made seven times: a thing crosses over, the village sees it, somebody writes it down.
- **Comedy: 8.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade.'" (ch. 9) The comedy comes out of what each person is, and it carries the moral problem.
- **Characters: 7.** "'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything as much.'" (ch. 25) against "'You rang once,' she said." (ch. 21). The first is a poacher, and he is unforgettable. The second is the only sentence the two women ever say to each other. Villagers 9, each woman alone 7, the two of them together 5.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the neck. 'The—'" (ch. 25) The joke from chapter 1 is tried again after a death and will not come. That is the switch done by a character and not by the narrator.
- **Prose: 7.** "Coralie looked at her for some time." (ch. 11, and again ch. 24) Exact and consistent, but one register for everything, and it refuses feeling so steadily that the refusal becomes a tic: "for some time" nine times in nine chapters.
- **The ending: 8.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (ch. 25) It costs a life the reader liked and it pays Jeannot in full. It loses its two points on Coralie (fault 5).
- **Clarity: 5.** "She took it for a thing she had. It was a thing Inguma kept, on her side, under its weight. Then in one week she lay on her back for Inguma, got up at four and went in to meet it, and the night she carried went where the letters came from." (ch. 6) I read that paragraph three times and I am still taking it on trust. The same goes for who is sitting, who is standing and who goes with whom in chapter 21.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. From chapter 13 without effort; from 5 to 12 for the villagers and not for the two women.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who reads for people, yes, with a warning about the first hundred pages. On a friend who reads Crouch, no, not as it stands.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Verdict for this lens: below the bar. A 7 where an 8 was asked for.** The supporting cast is above both comparisons. The two leads are level with Crouch's and below The Good Place's. The relationship between the two leads, which is what the premise sells, is below both.

What puts it there:

1. **The village is better than anything in Crouch and level with The Good Place.** Crouch's secondary people are functions. Here each villager is wrong in a way that is his own and has a grammar for it: Sarthou's files ("That is a file. It is mine. It has a number."), Jeannot's numbered grievances, Marie-Jo's chairs, Madame Ducasse's columns, Kévin's forum rules, Arlette's "I'm not asking". That is exactly the Good Place method, where each person's fault is also how they talk. Jeannot's telephone call in chapter 19 would be the best scene in a Crouch novel by a distance.

2. **The marriage is set up as well as Crouch sets one up, and paid less.** Dark Matter makes the reader afraid for a marriage by showing one ordinary happy evening and then putting another man in the house for a month. Chapter 1's kitchen is that evening, and it is better written than Crouch's. But the usurper gets one breakfast (ch. 16), is beaten by a single word, and withdraws in a sentence seen from outside (ch. 22). Crouch would have given her the bed for a week and made us watch.

3. **The two versions never have it out.** Jason meets Jason and they talk, bargain and fight; the four leads of The Good Place do nothing but talk to each other, and that talk is the show. Here the two women share a room for one page and one line. Everything else between them is done through a wrist, a pencil, a jaw and a sash. Those scenes are funny and exact. They are also the whole of the relationship.

4. **Maylis is Chidi, and the book is as hard on her as the show is on him, which is to its credit.** "Either" loses Marie-Jo the mairie, loses Coralie nine months behind a fruit bowl, and on the night becomes "She meant to do both", which is a tick in both boxes and kills a woman. The Good Place names that and makes it land. This book builds it perfectly and then does not let anyone see it (fault 4).

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. Lesca's second choice happens off her own page (ch. 22, 23)

> "The hand was hers. It lay on the wheel with the fingers open and nobody in it. Something went out of the cab as the warmth had gone out of a chair, and she was by herself in a stalled van with five words."

> "She knew what put a woman on a track at three in the morning. She had said it with that woman's mouth." (ch. 23)

Lesca wrote a motion to take another woman's bed "and Coralie Mounet in it". She fought her way into the chair for it. At seven minutes to three she has the hand and the ignition, and she gives all of it up: the bed, Coralie, the man alive at the counter. It is the mayor's second decision at that gate and the reverse of her first, and the reader gets it as a sensation in somebody else's wrist. Chapter 23 then reports it in two sentences. The book has two protagonists and at its crisis only one of them is present.

It also buries its own best answer. In the same half page Maylis's hand goes "to her belt, for the key of the handle box. Coralie was behind that bank with a list round her neck". Put where Lesca was put, the other one reaches for the gate, and for Coralie. That is the answer to "Which were you counting?", and it goes by inside a scuffle.

**What I would do.** Write the cab from inside the right hand, as a Lesca section between 21 and 22 or as the opening of 22 before it turns to Maylis. She means to drive it herself. She feels the other one go for the key and knows why, because it was her own reason. She finds that a body two people are fighting over cannot drive a kilometre of track. Then she chooses between holding on, which keeps the bed, and letting go, which puts a woman on the track. Make her know what she is letting go of when she does it.

### 2. The two women never have their scene (ch. 21)

> "'You rang once,' she said."

That is the entire exchange between two versions of one person, after twenty chapters of approach. Lesca's reply is to shut her hand and sit down. It is a fine page and it is a page.

There is also a contradiction in it. "The woman watched her do it. She had come for that seat as well." But chapter 20 has Maylis fighting to stay awake: "She meant to be awake when the telephone rang, with it in her hand". She did not come for anything. She fell asleep on duty.

**What I would do.** Keep it short, but let each say the one thing only the other can hear. Maylis says what she came for, or that she did not come. And the question that Coralie will not ask and Maïté asks to silence is put by the one person entitled to an answer, the one who is about to stand at the same gate. Lesca can fail to answer. She must be asked. Then settle whether Maylis wanted the seat that night, and make chapter 20 agree with it.

### 3. Maylis's want is dropped, never put down (ch. 7, 15, 17, 19, 25)

> "*I mean to stay in the chair. That is where the sash is.*" (ch. 15)

> "She knew what was on the stone, and she wanted it with the stone in." (ch. 17)

This is the most alive Maylis gets: a woman who has said "Either" for forty-three years wants something, wants it against another woman, and goes on wanting it after she has read the stone. Then chapter 19 shows her the night, she telephones Jeannot, and the want is not mentioned again. She neither renounces it nor finds she still has it. The form with both motions on the back rides in her chest pocket into the cemetery ("and the form for the signs") and nobody looks at it.

**What I would do.** One beat in chapter 19 or 20, after the sole of the wader, in which she reads her own line again and the reader learns what is left of it. And pay the form: Larrère has asked for it four times. What she hands in, or does not, is the end of that want.

### 4. At the gate Maylis obeys again, and "both" is never named (ch. 22, 25)

> "Across the sum she had five words, said with her own mouth."

> "She meant to do both, and the folder on his passenger seat could go to Pau on Monday."

Her fault is that she does what she is told. At the crisis she does what she is told by the mayor in place of the president. The five words tip it, and they are Lesca's. Her own contribution is arithmetic: "Three minutes out. Two to get him up a ladder. Three back."

And "both" is "Either". It is the tick in both boxes from chapter 15. It is what puts the van in the dip and the river in room twelve. The book has built that line with great care over twenty-two chapters, and in chapter 25 the death is shared out between Coralie's list ("That's mine") and a general statement about the key. Nobody says what Friday was.

**What I would do.** Make the decision at the clay patch come from what is hers: nine rings and "Who's dead?", and the walk she made at dusk before anybody told her to. Let the five words arrive as confirmation, not as the cause. Then in chapter 25 let Coralie, who has cooked Either for sixteen years, say once what "both" was. One line. It makes "I want to dig it myself" an answer to something.

### 5. The relationship that carries the book ends twice on the same evasion (ch. 24, 25)

> "Coralie's door stood open, and her pillow was still on the far side of the landing." (ch. 24)

> "Maylis heard her on the landing, and a door, and did not listen for which." (ch. 25)

One withheld ending is restraint. Two, for the same woman, in consecutive chapters, is a policy, and the reader feels it as the author declining to decide. This after the book made a promise in so many words: "I'm off at seven. I'll carry the pillow over myself. Be in the house." (ch. 20)

And the heaviest thing Coralie does in the whole book, posting her application to the hospital at Bayonne (ch. 15), is never mentioned again. I checked: not a word after the yellow box.

**What I would do.** Keep Lesca's Coralie as she is: door open, pillow across, "Go to bed, Maylis". That one is right, and the name is the payment. Decide Maylis's Coralie on the page. She was asked, at last, at a back door, and said she would come. Let the reader hear which door, and let the letter to Bayonne be answered in the same scene, whichever way it goes.

### 6. Coralie is made to say the theme on a loop (ch. 1, 9, 14, 15, 16, 25)

> "'Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you, and that was me.'" (ch. 1)
> "'I've been asking you for sixteen years what you want.'" (ch. 9)
> "'In sixteen years you've never once asked me for anything.'" (ch. 14)
> "'Sixteen years I've waited to be asked for one thing in this house.'" (ch. 15)
> "'Sixteen years I've drawn those. What do you want, Maylis?'" (ch. 16)
> "'I've asked you one question for sixteen years and drawn you boxes for it.'" (ch. 25)

"Sixteen years" nine times in eight chapters. The first is one of the best lines in the book. By the fourth she has stopped being a woman with a job, a closing workplace and forty-two people to think of, and become the person whose function is to wait for Maylis to want something. She is the most alive person in the manuscript and she is being spent on repetition.

**What I would do.** Three times: chapters 1, 15 and 25. Elsewhere give her wants that are not Maylis. The home shuts in June and she runs the night there; she has almost nothing to say about either that is not a question to her partner.

### 7. Larrère takes the blame for both women (ch. 24, 25)

> "'From whose telephone did you ring me, Guy?'" (ch. 24)

> "'Which were you counting?' / Lesca gave her no answer." (ch. 24)

In the chapter where Lesca goes to the widow's kitchen to say it in a room, the widow asks the only question that matters, gets silence, and answers it herself with a confession about Guy. Ten lines later Lesca is at the garden gate prosecuting him. In chapter 25 Sarthou does the same service for Maylis. Both villages end with the same man walking off silent through mud, and the reader's anger has somewhere comfortable to go. Lesca's order came from the dead man's kitchen; Maylis's too. By comparison each woman is half excused.

It also takes the scene from Maïté. She has not opened that gate for twelve years. The person with the right to ask Guy anything is at the foot of the ladder, and she is given the rung to hold.

**What I would do.** Keep the clock. Keep his silence, which is the best thing he does. But make Maïté hold Lesca to "Which were you counting?" before she gives up her own secret, and give the question at the gate to Maïté, with Lesca up the ladder, hands full of leaf mould, unable to come down. And pay chapter 2: "'I have a verb,' Maïté Castagnet said." The timer cut her off and the book never lets her finish.

### 8. Lesca's mind is open where it is mechanical and sealed where it is hers (ch. 2, 6, 12, 18)

> "What she carried in her head by day went over at night, and for a week it had gone over unsorted, so she took the prefect's medal out of the mairie safe, in its case, and gave the day to the morning in February 2015 when he pinned it on her in the square." (ch. 6)

The narration sits inside Lesca for twelve chapters and gives her deductions at length: findings numbered, columns ruled. What it never gives is the thing she has thought about at 02:51 every night for twelve years. That is fair as a withheld reveal. It is unfair as character, because after the reveal it stays shut: we are never told whether she knows which she was counting, or cannot tell. The narrator protects her exactly as the village does.

And the first thing she chooses to send her double is her medal. A woman who has kept a drowning to herself for twelve years opens the traffic with her decoration. Is that vanity, bait, or a test of what crosses whole? The book treats it as method. It is the most revealing thing she does before chapter 14 and it has no motive on the page.

**What I would do.** Give her one attempt at the answer and let it fail, in chapter 18 after Coralie has gone across the landing: she tries to say which, to herself, and finds she has only the sum. Then the silence in Maïté's kitchen is ignorance and not management. For the medal, one sentence of why, and let Coralie's "Tell me why you're going over it" get a truer answer than "So as to have it right."

### 9. The villagers repeat their turn where they should be surprised out of it (ch. 2–18)

> "'I ask that that be minuted,' Guy Larrère said." (ch. 6; the same request three times in ch. 8, and again in ch. 10, 14 and 18)

Counted across the manuscript: "be minuted" 9, "They tell me" 11, "I'll be brief" 7, "shrugged" 14 times in 11 chapters and nearly all of them Kévin, "I'm not asking" 7, "Your father" 11, Maïté's handbag held "in both hands" 5, each time with the black coat or the radiator beside it. Each is right the first three times. By chapter 14 the reader says the line before the character does, and a person has become a mechanism.

The Good Place gives everyone a tic and then breaks it under pressure. Three people here are broken out of theirs, and they are the three the reader will remember: Kévin ("I wanted six"), Jeannot ("Both done, Lesca. You've what you asked for."), and Larrère when the window goes "all the way, into the door". Sarthou, Marie-Jo, Tastet and Madame Ducasse never are.

Marie-Jo is the real loss. On Lesca's side she is given the best line about the two women in the book ("You don't stop speaking to a person for doing nothing. You stop when they've done something.") and "She had almost used the name". Then she is not in the square for the confession and not in chapter 24 at all. Her name does not occur in either.

**What I would do.** Halve every count above. Put Marie-Jo in the square in chapter 23, and let her do one thing with the name.

### 10. Kévin is borrowed to explain the hall, and the chair he warns of is never faced (ch. 13, 15)

> "'I'd say it's the loto before the priest has called a number. Everybody's sat down with every card they could have had. Your professor opened the door and counted one of you. That's looking.'" (ch. 15)

For fourteen chapters Kévin knows what a forum taught him and gets it endearingly wrong. Here he reads the book's physics off in five tidy sentences and tells Maylis what the empty chair is: "Sit down in that and you're not hers and you're not yours. You're nobody's." How does he know?

The chair matters for her as a person. Chapter 13 says of the hall: "nothing had been so quiet in a fortnight". For a woman who says "Either", a seat where nobody can ask her anything is the strongest temptation in the book, stronger than the sash. It is planted, glossed by a poacher, and she is never drawn to it again. The chair comes back in chapter 22 as Madame Etcheto's, which is good, but that is the reader's payoff and not Maylis's.

**What I would do.** Cut Kévin's gloss to a guess he does not trust. Let Maylis look at that chair once more, in the room in chapter 21, as the third thing she could do.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 1's kitchen and its failed repeat in chapter 25.** "'I don't cook Either,' she said. 'There's no recipe for it. Soup or duck.'" Then the oven door, Larrère's voice from the back of the neck, the rib strapped so that she can laugh. And at the end, the same door opened two fingers and the sentence that will not come. This is the marriage, and it is why anyone cares.

2. **The post box (ch. 15).** "'It's twenty minutes to Bayonne,' she said. / 'Yes,' Coralie said. 'That's what I thought you'd say.'" The cruellest thing Maylis does, done by saying nothing. Do not soften it and do not explain it.

3. **The call log (ch. 18).** "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." Coralie's whole speech from there to "I wanted you in the house." It turns eleven years of a landing into a decision she made, and it makes her guilty too without excusing anyone.

4. **Jeannot on the telephone (ch. 19), and what the book does with "little".** Nine rings, "Who's dead?", six numbered points, "And six. Bugger off." He is alive because he is furious. "Little Lesca" becoming "Lesca" in the café, coming back as "Little Lesca, you've gone off your head" at the hatch, and the last line in the grave: leave every one of them where it is.

5. **The home on the night (ch. 22), from the yard to "Forty-one".** Monsieur Daguerre's "Here" to the roof of the van. Kévin counting five in his own headlamps. Coralie closing the eyes with her thumb and then putting the pencil line through the name. The hatbox on the wardrobe. Nobody in it says what they feel, and it is the one place in the book where that rule is exactly right.
