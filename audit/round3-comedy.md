# Critic's report — lens: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `25.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes — 8.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." (2) The sum is concrete, it is stated early, and by 18 Coralie is inside it.
- **Tension — 7.** "That village had not been spared December 2014. It had not had it yet." (4) From that line there is a clock. Before it, 9,000 words have none.
- **Progression — 6.** "Until then the nights had given her scraps." (7) So had the book. The two women share a body for the first time at 51% of the text and spend a day in each other's village at 59%.
- **Comedy — 6.** "She said it had been told twice and given two lots of stars, and it got through them early and came at one." (3) A dozen moments of that quality in 59,000 words, almost all made by one device, and none of them made by either lead.
- **Characters — 7.** "'I've put your rib in it,' he said. 'Don't thank me.'" (7) The village is alive and each of them is wrong in their own way. The two women at the centre are the least funny people in it.
- **The switch between dark and comic — 8.** "'Who's dead?' said Jeannot Castagnet." (19) Said by a man the reader has just watched drown, to the woman who has just watched it. In three places the book is better at this than the comparison.
- **Prose — 7.** "On Monday she cracked a rib on the right side, and until it mended she was to sleep on her back." (1) Exact and controlled, but it refuses the pluperfect throughout, so every step back in time reads for a moment as the present story, and comic set-ups that depend on "earlier" arrive blurred.
- **The ending — 8.** "It's four sides, and it doesn't thank you." (25) The saved man was never in danger, says so in writing, and then gets into the grave of the woman who died instead. It costs, and the last image is funny and terrible at once.
- **Clarity — 5.** "She did on the way out what Kévin told her to do on the way in. She took the right-hand lamp, which had not been hers in 2014, and looked at the glass of it and nothing else." (13) A reader who has to do this much bookkeeping is not laughing.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes, from the last line of 4. Before that I would have stopped twice: in the committee meeting of 2, and at the lake in 12.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who reads Penelope Fitzgerald or watches *Detectorists*, yes. On the friend the bar describes, who wants what *The Good Place* gives, no: it would be handed back at chapter 3 with the words "you said it was funny".

## 2. Against the comparisons

**As comedy: below *The Good Place*.** Three things put it there.

1. **Density and range.** *The Good Place* lands something every few lines and has six ways of doing it: insult, wordplay, slapstick, absurd rule, character blindness, visual gag. This book has one way, used well: official language laid on a private matter ("There's nothing here that lets me un-receive it. There's no column."). It gets a smile most pages and a laugh perhaps twelve times. Chapter 12 has no joke in it. Chapters 18, 19 and 21 have one each.
2. **Who is funny.** In *The Good Place* the leads are the funniest people on screen: Eleanor is wrong loudly, Chidi is wrong at length. Here the comedy belongs to Jeannot, Kévin, Coralie, Larrère, Lafitte and Madame Etcheto. Maylis is silent, and Lesca is funny only when she is in the wrong village (16). The jokes happen near the heroines, not through them.
3. **When the engine starts.** *The Good Place* has its premise running in the first minute and breaks it open at the end of its first season. Here the idea that makes the book a comedy, two versions of one woman loose in each other's lives with the wrong habits, gets about 4,000 words at chapters 16 and 17 and is then put away.

**On making the moral problem funny without making it small: level.** Three things put it there.

1. **The verb.** "Opened what? There's a name over it. It reads that she opened him." (2) The ethical question of the whole book is turned into a point of grammar, argued by a committee, lost on a vote, and then answered twenty-one chapters later by the mayor herself with the one verb nobody dared move: "and I drowned him." That is the comparison's method exactly: the philosophy is the set piece.
2. **"Either."** Indecision as a running gag is Chidi's territory, and the book finds its own version: the refusal to choose keeps being taken down as an official act. "The keyholder has no preference" (5), "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade" (9), "I have that as consent" (17), "I can't put Either in a tray" (20). It escalates every time and it is the moral problem.
3. **Darrigrand's step.** "It's four when you're the one counting. When it's your house it's one, and they knock it down." (6) The trolley problem, from the man on the other track, with a broom.

**On the switch and on reversals: level, and in three places above.** Running gags turn into grief without a change of voice: Kévin's finger count at four o'clock in the flood (22), Monsieur Daguerre's "Here" from a wheelchair in the water (22), Coralie's oven turn that she cannot finish (25). The comparison does this in its last season. It does not do it better. The late reversal (Larrère rang from the Castagnet kitchen) re-reads the folder, the lane, the two bowls, the suitcase and his four motions for a verb ("A stranger reads it as a married couple"), and every one of those was planted as a joke.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The comic engine of the premise is rationed to two mornings, late.** (16–17)
"She was here to stop a flood, with three days and a mouth." Lesca is in Maylis's village from six until two; Maylis is in Lesca's from six until noon. Chapter 16 is the funniest in the book ("She thinks she's the mayor. Put that." / "It takes a small m.") and it proves what the book has been holding back. For fifteen chapters it builds mirrored pairs and then never lets them meet across: Lesca, who lost Marie-Jo's friendship over a carrier bag, never stands in front of the Marie-Jo who would tell her where to put the trestles. Maylis, who has been talked at through a car window for eighteen years, never chairs a council with a timed Larrère in it. Maylis gets five lines with the Coralie who runs the home. *Fix:* bring the first full crossing forward to where 12 now stands and give each woman a whole day. Stage the missing scenes: Marie-Jo putting Lesca on the bar and Lesca trying to put it to a vote; Maylis in the chair with the white timer, unable to ring it on Larrère, who gets his first uninterrupted speech since 2015 and does not know what to do with it. Pay for it by cutting 12 to a page.

**2. The two leads are not funny in themselves.** (throughout)
"Maylis rinsed a cup." (7) "She had no answer." (13) "She had nothing to say to that." (25) Maylis's silence is the joke once, superbly ("The keyholder has no preference"), and after that it is only silence. Yet chapter 1 gives her a comic mind and then drops it: "She put it at thirty-five kilos, which was a sack of cement." A woman who weighs a demon in sacks of cement is funny. She should weigh, pace and price everything she meets, in her head, to the last page. Lesca's equivalent is the minute-book, and it too is used once at full strength: "*J. Castagnet, living, 2014 to date. Owes me for diesel.*" (18) The other findings are bare ("*4. When the other one gets up and I am still sitting, I go with her.*"). *Fix:* write every finding and every ledger line as that mayor would minute a haunting, with the pedantry on show, and let Maylis's measuring run through her chapters. Neither change needs a new scene.

**3. The rulebook for crossing is too large, is delivered straight, and stands between the reader and the jokes.** (3, 4, 6, 8, 13, 14, 15)
"She fixed on the left lamp, which was not the one that came on for her in 2014. She rubbed her palms together on her knees, the seam against the skin." (8) By chapter 15 the reader is carrying: Kévin's forum rules (fingers, read twice, don't push, the four o'clock trick, the alarm, the thing fetches you), the grandmother's four rules, Madame Etcheto's words and work, the two lamps, the warm chair, what is gone over by day crosses at night, holding a lamp to stay past the alarm, leaving with the other one, and a hall with an empty chair that Kévin explains at length and nobody ever sits in. *The Good Place* states a rule as a joke and breaks it in the next scene. The book does this exactly once, and it is the model: "'That's one gone.' … 'That's two.'" (15) *Fix:* three rules, each given by Kévin or Madame Etcheto, who are funny when they legislate, and each broken on the page. Lose the lamps. Either someone sits in the hall's empty chair or Kévin's theory of it goes.

**4. The opening hides the premise and explains everything else.** (1–2)
"He was Guy Larrère, who grew maize and was also president of the landowners' association, which owned the sluice gate on the flood meadows below the village. Maylis kept the key to its winch. It brought her a little each month on top of her wage as the one outdoor employee of the commune, which was what the village was called when it had a council and a budget." Chapter 1 glosses the mairie, the commune and the loto, and does not say what the book is. Chapter 2 replays the morning with a cast of nine, a committee and four dates. Its best mirror joke, Larrère's "I'll be brief" now under a kitchen timer, only works for a reader who has already understood that this is the same woman, and the book does not say so until the last line of 5, at 23% of its length. *Fix:* open on the postman asking the mayor where he might find the mayor, which is the clearest funny page in the chapter. Let chapter 2 state by its first page break that this is Maylis Lesca in a village where she turned a handle. The mirror jokes then start paying ten thousand words sooner.

**5. One device, and four officials with one voice.** (7, 9, 11, 16, 20, 21)
"You are entered as evidence." "I cannot summon Either to the brigade." "There's no column." "I cannot file a forecast." "I can't put Either in a tray." "It's a line on the sheet." Sarthou, Madame Ducasse, the woman at Hontaube and the young man from Pau are the same joke in four coats. By 20 the reader can write the next one. *Fix:* keep Madame Ducasse and Sarthou and make them different (she protects the register, he wants his pension; let those collide). Cut the young man's routine in 21 by half. Spend the room on the other kinds of comedy the book shows it can do and then abandons: farce (two hands, one sash knot, in 17), mimicry (the oven, in 1), and plain abuse (Jeannot's six points, in 19).

**6. Coralie's wit is spent in chapter 1.** (1, 25)
"'I'll be brief,' she said, in Larrère's voice, which was lower than hers and came from the back of the neck. 'The eggs.'" It is the warmest page in the book and the only time anyone is funny on purpose. After chapter 3 the two women never laugh together again, and Coralie is reduced to one question, put in five chapters (1, 9, 11, 16, 25): what do you want? The failed turn in 25 ("'I'll be brief,' she said … 'The—'") is the right ending for her and it rests on a single earlier instance. *Fix:* she does somebody at the oven again in the middle of the book, Marie-Jo on the telephone about chrysanthemums would serve. And Lesca, in her own house, remembers the last time her Coralie did one, before February 2015. Then the unfinished one in 25 has three points on its line and the loss is measured.

**7. Chapters 12 to 15 go grey.** (12–15)
"Lesca had the time now. Whatever she sent across had to put that woman on the track with twenty-five minutes in hand." Eight thousand words between the un-received letter (11) and the café's list (16), in which the comedy is embarrassment (chrysanthemums) and the scenes end by stopping: a chair scrapes, an envelope drops, a door closes to a hand's width. Chapter 12 is three errands and the drafting of a sentence. This is the stretch where a reader who came for the comedy leaves. *Fix:* fault 1 solves most of it. For the rest, the lake walk needs its site manager to be a scene and not a passer-by (he takes her for a clerk and thinks the mayor is a man: build on it), and the drafting of the five words should be done aloud in front of somebody who answers.

**8. The narrator explains the beat after it has landed.** (2, 3, 14, 16, 23, 25)
"Two women were keeping that count, in two hands, and the form began to show it." (16) "Inguma came by day for what was not hers. She had laughed with another woman's January, and Inguma found her before Maïté was off the stairs." (14) "It was not what she was asked. It had been the answer for eleven years." (2) Worst, because it touches the best moment in the book: Kévin counts five in the flood, "as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another" (22), and three chapters on he is made to say "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything as much." (25) The reader had it. *Fix:* cut the tag wherever the scene has shown it. In 25 let Kévin say only that he flew and did not like it.

**9. Mannerisms run as gags without changing.** (throughout)
Kévin "shrugged" fourteen times in eleven chapters. Arlette says "I'm not asking" seven times: its reversal, "So I am asking. What have you been turning, Maylis?" (11), is the payoff, and four more plain uses follow it and blunt it. "For some time" nine times, Inguma "with its back to" eleven. A running gag has to arrive different each time, as "Either", "I'll be brief" and "They tell me" do. These only arrive. *Fix:* three shrugs. Arlette says "I'm not asking" twice before 11 and never plainly after it.

**10. A joke that stops an evacuation.** (22)
"'It's stopped,' he said. 'Your winch. I've three things to say about it. First, I counted, as you do, by the click…'" A hundred and ten words of numbered grievance on the sill while the water goes from shins to knees and a man waits to be carried. It is also Jeannot's third numbered speech in seven chapters, and the second one (19) is the peak. *Fix:* he says "I've three things to say about it," gets as far as "First," and Coralie reads the next name over him. "It's up a hand" and "I put it in number six" survive as fragments while he lifts. It is funnier interrupted and the scene keeps its water.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Madame Etcheto and the two lots of stars** (3), with the two women laughing against the corridor wall and being heard: "Staff to wait. Staff not to join in." And its return at the same door in 22, where the narration recalls only that they "had got the giggles, and been heard."
2. **The viewing of the winch** (5), from "There's nothing wrong with that winch" through the ewe of seventy-one to "It wants grease." Seven men, one handle, a perfect build, and the heroine's rib is the price of it.
3. **The end of 19.** The drowning told flat, then the telephone, nine rings, and "Who's dead?" through to "And six. Bugger off." Do not shorten the six points.
4. **Kévin's count at four o'clock** (22): "'Five,' he said. He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." With Monsieur Daguerre's "Here" / "I know you are" in the same yard, and Coralie's "Forty-one."
5. **The three "I'll be brief"s:** Coralie into the oven (1), Larrère at the garden gate with three minutes and no bell, "Nothing came" (24), and Coralie bent to the oven again and unable to go on (25). The whole book's handling of comedy and its withdrawal is in those three.
