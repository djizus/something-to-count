# Critic's report, round 3: Prose

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `25.md`, in order, in full (59,300 words). Nothing else. Counts below are from the text of those 25 files.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** Clear and restated without fuss, but they belong to the village long before they belong to Maylis. "Behind it were forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." (ch. 2)
- **Tension: 6.** Chapter 22 is a 9 by itself; chapters 5 to 18 report fear in a clause and go back to inventory. "She was more frightened than at any time since the night of the flood, and she wanted the card in her hand." (ch. 4)
- **Progression: 7.** What crosses grows in a straight line: letters, a signature, a weal, a body, a day. "On Monday morning the other one left the chair first, and Lesca went with her." (ch. 14)
- **Comedy: 7.** The procedural jokes are exact. They are also one joke register. "There's nothing here that lets me un-receive it. There's no column." (ch. 11)
- **Characters: 7.** Distinct in what they want, alike in how they talk. "I'd have taken it without one." (Coralie, ch. 4)
- **Switch between dark and comic: 8.** As good as the comparison. "He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." (Kévin counting five fingers in the flood, ch. 22)
- **Prose: 7.** Good sentences at one unvarying pace, with a faulty clock. "Out on the meadows the dark began to shine." (ch. 19)
- **The ending: 8.** It costs a named person the reader has laughed with, and it leaves Coralie unsettled. "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (ch. 25)
- **Clarity: 5.** The rules of the device are given in the narrator's most compressed sentences. "She took it for a thing she had. It was a thing Inguma kept, on her side, under its weight." (ch. 6)

**Finished unpaid?** Yes, but chapters 6 to 8 were pushed through, not pulled.
**Pressed on a friend?** On a friend who reads dry English comic novels, yes. On the Crouch reader the bar names, not as it stands.

## 2. Against the comparisons (prose)

**Below the bar, narrowly, and for reasons that can be fixed.**

- **Below Crouch on legibility.** A Crouch sentence never has to be read twice to learn when it happened or who is acting. His device is stated in a line a reader can repeat to someone else by the end of the first act. Here the reader assembles the rules from numbered "findings" written in ledger language (ch. 4, 6, 14, 18), and the plainest statement of the premise, Kévin's "it's the loto before the priest has called a number" (ch. 15), arrives at the 60% mark.
- **Above Crouch on the sentence.** He writes serviceable prose in one-line paragraphs. This book chooses nouns better than he does ("the sound of a sack let down off a trailer", ch. 1) and its dialogue is better than his throughout.
- **Level with The Good Place on the running gag, below it on voice.** "Either", "I'll be brief", "took note", "put it in forty-two" and Monsieur Daguerre's "Here" are planted and paid like that show's best runners. But that show's people are each wrong in their own idiom. Here ten people share one dry wit (fault 3).

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The central choice is not on the page when it happens. (Structural; ch. 21 to 24.)**
Lesca's motion is to take Maylis's body, bed and Coralie (ch. 14). At 02:52 she gives all of that up. The book renders it from Maylis's side only: "The hand was hers. It lay on the wheel with the fingers open and nobody in it. Something went out of the cab as the warmth had gone out of a chair" (ch. 22). The renunciation itself is four words about a voice: "It asked for nothing." The plain version comes two chapters later, as a report on a landing: "I was in her right hand. I let go of it, and I told her with her own mouth to go to the tonne first" (ch. 24). A reader who has to decode the moral centre of the book afterwards has not felt it. The same compression hides the rules that make the choice mean something: "It would be an addition. She meant to have it as one" (ch. 18).
*Do:* write Lesca's side of that minute, 200 to 300 words, either closing chapter 21 or between "Go to the tonne first" and the answered call. She has the hand and the ignition, she sees what Maylis is reaching for and why, and she lets go, and the text says what she leaves behind. Separately, state the three rules once, in plain words, by chapter 8: what she dwells on by day crosses at night; if she is in the chair when the other one gets up, she goes with her; Inguma fetches her back. Move Kévin's loto image forward to help.

**2. The book's clock is broken: backstory is told in the simple past. (Book-wide.)**
In a story with two timelines and a twelve-year gap, the verb tense is the reader's only clock, and it is unreliable. Chapter 1, in a scene set at 02:51 on Tuesday: "On Monday she cracked a rib on the right side." Chapter 2: "She tried them on the night after the flood, when the thing first came, and learned what came of it." Chapter 12: "She was right to the minute, that night, with a telephone in her hand." Chapter 22 puts April and the present in adjacent sentences with the same tense: "She put that patch in herself in April, a barrow at a time. She gave it ten minutes." Chapter 7 drops a scrap into the loto with "That came in the morning after the medal", and the reader cannot tell which morning. Elsewhere the past perfect is used normally ("She had patched that bank each spring for six years", ch. 19), so it reads as error, not as style. The year itself, 2026, is first stated in chapter 9.
*Do:* one pass over all 25 chapters. The first verb of every step back in time takes "had"; after that the passage may relax. Put the year in the first sentence of chapter 1.

**3. Ten people have the same wit. (Structural for dialogue; throughout.)**
Almost every exchange ends on a dry last word, and the last word sounds the same whoever has it. The priest: "I have preached to people who came for less" (ch. 7). The gendarme: "I have stood longer for less" (ch. 12). The narrator: "She had sent Guy Larrère's motions down the table again for less" (ch. 14). Darrigrand: "Then the commune can sweep it" (ch. 6). The clerk at Hontaube: "We have a river as well" (ch. 20). Coralie: "It holds twelve when somebody's counting" (ch. 20). Sarthou and Père Honoré are also marked by the same device, speech without contractions: "I do not say yes on a doorstep" and "I do not ask because I have forgotten anything."
*Do:* ration the last word. Coralie and Jeannot earn it; Larrère has his own refrain. Take the aphorisms from Sarthou and leave him his report language ("persons unknown", "attended at the brigade"), which is funnier. Let Maïté and Arlette be plain. Maïté's best line in the book has no wit in it: "There's a tile gone over the back bedroom."

**4. One pace for everything. (Book-wide.)**
Average sentence length sits between 8.9 and 12.4 words in every chapter. Sentences of 30 words or more are under 3% in 24 chapters of 25. There is no semicolon in the book and there are three dashes. The join is always the same: ", and" 1,112 times, ", with" 272 times. One sentence in five opens on She, He, It or They, and 125 open on "It was". The budget meeting reads: "They gave her four minutes. The radiator ticked." The flood reads: "She got out and ran. She had not run since the rib. It let her have a third of each breath." Same step. The switch from comic to dark is therefore carried by content alone, and the middle chapters blur into each other. A check for stock formulas comes back almost clean; the risk here is the opposite one, prose scrubbed so even that it stops registering.
*Do:* keep the clipped step for committees. Let the run in chapter 22, the flood night in chapter 19 and the square in chapter 23 breathe in long sentences that do not stop where the reader expects. Chapter 23's "She watched her sentence go back through six thousand people at a walk, row by row and on down the road to the Port" shows the book can.

**5. The narrator keeps score, and recaps before a payoff. (ch. 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 18, 19, 22, 23.)**
About 25 sentences tell the reader that something is the first, the only or the last. "It was the first question the association had ever put to Maylis" (ch. 5). "It was the one order given in the square that week that was hers" (ch. 3). "It was the first thing in Peyrebarthe that was done because she said so. She liked it" (ch. 9). "She sat with the form in front of her and liked it more than she had liked anything since the medal" (ch. 11). Each follows a scene that had shown exactly that. Worse are the recaps placed in front of the strongest moments: "She had kept one unanswered call for twelve years, and on Thursday she said that she would know which Lesca did with the line. She knew now" (ch. 23); "He had come out of a dry cellar half an hour before, by the neck, and had been told that the gate was to be opened on him" (ch. 22).
*Do:* cut all but two or three of the score sentences. Cut every recap. In chapter 9 keep only "She would have done it again that afternoon, to anybody."

**6. A second narrator explains France. (ch. 1, 2, 11, 17.)**
"the mairie, the village's town hall"; "the commune, which was what the village was called when it had a council and a budget"; "Saturday's loto, which was bingo, played for a ham"; "The prefecture, which was the State's office at Pau." This is a guidebook voice, and the narrator who never explains anything else has no business with it. The policy is also inconsistent. Bingo is explained; "pawl", on which the plot turns, never is (first use ch. 2, eleven uses). "Tonne" is used a section before it is explained in chapter 1. Madame Ducasse is introduced again as "the secretary" in chapter 11, nine chapters after her first entrance, and Tastet again as "the first deputy" in chapter 17.
*Do:* explain through someone who needs telling, with a joke on it, as chapter 2 does for the bull: "For anyone who has managed not to see one, it's a frame in the shape of a bull with fireworks tied along it." The postman, the courier and the young man from Pau are already in the book for this. Show the pawl once, in chapter 5, by what it does.

**7. Habit clauses and year counts. (Throughout; worst in ch. 2, 3, 14.)**
"As she did", "as he read everything", "as she said the rosary" (twice, ch. 4 and 21): 47 of these. Chapter 3 has "in a wet week" four times. Durations appear 72 times: eleven years, twelve years, sixteen years, eighteen years, twelve winters, four winters. The numbers that tell the two women apart (eleven against sixteen, twelve against eighteen) are drowned by repetition.
*Do:* halve both. Keep a duration only where it is the point of the sentence.

**8. Stock gestures. (Throughout.)**
Kévin shrugs 14 times, three of them in chapter 8. "For some time" nine times. "Watched her do it" six times. People speak to objects at least eleven times: "to the ladder", "to the rails" (ch. 2), "to the water" (ch. 6), "to his hat" (ch. 17), "to the wall" (ch. 16). They look at radiators, wipers, shoes and stoves 18 times. Distances come in hands and forearms about 30 times. The ponies arrive in identical words in chapters 3 and 11 ("The ponies had heard the van").
*Do:* give each gesture one owner. The radiator and the averted address are Maïté's. The wall is Marie-Jo's. Take them from everyone else.

**9. Feeling is announced, flat. (ch. 2, 4, 5, 16, 17.)**
"That frightened her as the thing on her chest had not for years" (ch. 2). "She wanted one more thing to sign, and she wanted it a great deal" (ch. 17). "He was pleased to be asked, and it showed" (ch. 5). "He enjoyed that" (ch. 16). A narrator this withheld cannot also issue bulletins; the words are weaker than the scenes. The book knows how to do it properly: "the hand she held them with began to draw in on itself, from inside, as a fist closes on a thing it means to keep" (ch. 14).
*Do:* in the steel room (ch. 4) and under the leaves (ch. 2), put the fear in the body as chapter 14 does. Delete the bulletins.

**10. Sentences that defeat a first reading, and loose ends of usage.**
"It was no village in which Guy Larrère wore it" (ch. 17). "She knew what was on the stone, and she wanted it with the stone in" (ch. 17). "None of it had the wrong furniture any longer" (ch. 18). "Ten days were left, and each night's alarm took the room when she had been given one thing" (ch. 8). "as a fortnight of going in let her" (ch. 16). Usage: "He smelt of the river" (ch. 16) against "smelled" nine times; the waterproof's lettering is "across the back" in chapters 1, 2 and 7 and "across the shoulders" in chapter 16. Spelling is otherwise consistent British throughout.
*Do:* rewrite the five; one spelling of smelled; one place for the lettering.
