# Round 3 — Reader

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `25.md`, in order, in full (about 59,000 words). Nothing else opened but the brief.

Lens: an ordinary reader with no knowledge of French communes, sluice gates, Basque folklore or the two-lamp experiment.

Verdict against the bar: **below it as a whole, at about 7.** Chapters 14 to 22 are at the bar or over it. Chapters 1 to 13 are not, and a reader who loved Dark Matter reaches chapter 14 only on goodwill.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes, but I would have put it down twice first, after chapter 8 and after chapter 10. From chapter 14 I could not have stopped.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who likes quiet village comedy, yes, with the instruction to get to chapter 14. On a friend who came to me for another Dark Matter, no, not as it stands.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch on pull, above him on the marriage.**

- Crouch has his premise in play inside the first chapter and tells the reader in plain words what the hero is trying to do next. Here the premise lands at the last line of chapter 4 ("It had not had it yet"), 11,000 words in, and the clock at the end of chapter 6. After that I could not say what either woman was trying to do. Lesca's aim changes four times (stop it, send a date, send five words, stay). Maylis has no aim about the flood until chapter 19.
- A Crouch chapter ends on a turn. Of the first thirteen chapters here, eight end on a quiet beat: a door shut (10), a register read (8), a telephone call overheard (13), "I told her there would" (7). Of the last twelve, ten end on a turn.
- Crouch makes you afraid for a marriage by threatening it from outside. This book does it by neglect, and does it better. Coralie carrying her pillow across the landing "for the sleep", the envelope behind the fruit bowl for nine months, "That's what I thought you'd say" as it drops in the post box: I was more afraid for these two than I ever was for Jason and Daniela, because Coralie is a person with a rota, a secret and her own guilt, and Daniela is a prize.

**Level with The Good Place on making the problem funny; below it on reversal and on variety.**

- The moral problem is funny without being made small. The stone cannot take a verb. Darrigrand on thirty-six houses against four: "It's four when you're the one counting. When it's your house it's one, and they knock it down." That is the trolley problem done as The Good Place does it.
- The Good Place has a reversal that makes you re-read everything. This book has one that does so (02:51 is the minute of the call, which re-reads the first sentence of the book) and three that only confirm what I had guessed (she knew he was out, from chapter 2; Larrère and Maïté, from chapter 9; Sarthou's notebook, from chapter 12).
- The Good Place's people are each wrong in their own way. Here six people are funny in one way (see fault 8).

**What I will remember in a month:** the thing of leaves and rivets with its back turned; eggs for Either; "Staff to wait. Staff not to join in"; "Nine minutes to three. I've had that for twelve years"; "And six. Bugger off"; Monsieur Daguerre's "Here"; Kévin wanting six fingers; "Forty-one"; the dead man of one village climbing down into a grave in the other. That is more than I kept from Recursion.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Each woman's decisive act happens in a clause, and Lesca's happens offstage. (21, 22, 24)**

The book builds two wants for eight chapters. Lesca's motion: "That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it." Maylis's: "I mean to stay in the chair. That is where the sash is." Both are given up, and I was not shown either being given up.

Maylis's renunciation of the sash is: "Lesca was sitting, and holding, when the other one turned to a telephone." (21)

Lesca's renunciation of Coralie, of a living Jeannot, of the whole other life, is seen from outside, by the other woman: "The hand was hers. It lay on the wheel with the fingers open and nobody in it. Something went out of the cab as the warmth had gone out of a chair." (22) Two chapters later it is reported: "I was in her right hand. I let go of it." (24)

Why did she let go? She had the ignition. She could have driven to the tonne herself, saved him with her own hands and kept the bed. I do not know what opened her fingers. This is the choice the book is about for her, and it is missing.

What I would do: write it from inside the hand. Open chapter 23 there, before she wakes, or break the alternation for one page in 22. She has the key turned; she feels Maylis going for the winch because Coralie is behind the bank, which is her own reason of twelve years ago; something makes her say the words and go. Whatever the reason is, I need to be in her when she has it. Give Maylis one beat of the same in 21: let her look at the chair she came for before she turns to the telephone.

**2. Chapters 6 to 13 mark time, and one heroine does not know there is a flood coming until the night before. (6–13, 19)**

The date is fixed at the end of chapter 4. Lesca resolves to act at the end of 6. Then: a rake (8), a nap (10), a doorstep (10), a walk with a watch (12), a visit to Bayonne (12). Maylis meanwhile is given "a Friday the eleventh with nothing attached to it" (9) and "*Friday. Not a flood. A lake.*" (11). She learns what Friday is in chapter 19, on Thursday night, with one day left.

So for the whole middle the only person in danger does not know it, and the only person who knows it is rehearsing sentences. The gauge ("a metre sixty", "a metre forty", "a forearm", "a hand") is the one clock I could hear. This is the stretch where a Crouch reader stops.

What I would do: give Maylis the flood by chapter 9. Move the night in three pieces (now the opening of 13) up to there. Then her wanting the medal and the sash is wanting them while knowing what they cost, which is a worse and better want. And she gets four days, not one, to try the Hontaube telephone, the sand and Larrère, and fail at each. Fold 8 into 6 or 10, and 12 into 10. The takeover in chapter 14 is where the book becomes impossible to put down; it should arrive at 40 per cent, not 54.

**3. The two rules the plot runs on are asserted in passing, and one has a hole. (6)**

The first: "What she carried in her head by day went over at night, and for a week it had gone over unsorted, so she took the prefect's medal out of the mairie safe". This is the mechanism of the whole middle, and it arrives as a subordinate clause in a summary paragraph. I skated over it and understood it only in chapter 7. Lesca never sees proof that it works, either. She sends the medal, the date and the five words blind, and I could not tell how she knew.

The second: "*3. It has not stopped raining there since the night Inguma first sat on both of us. I kept that flood on my side for twelve years, and I have let it across. That flood is mine.*" The two villages split over a lamp in June 2014. Why did it rain for nineteen days that December in one and not in the other? The only word on it is Larrère's "The river hasn't been over the line since 2009" (5). And who let the flood across: Lesca, by going in to meet the thing, or Maylis, by cracking a rib and lying on her back? The rain began on the night of the 24th, before Lesca had done anything.

What I would do: make the first rule a numbered finding with evidence, since she already numbers them. Let her receive Maylis's "*I want that*" on the pad the morning after the medal. For the second, let somebody ask the reader's question in the reader's words. Kévin is the book's explainer and would ask it. One line on what December 2014 was in Maylis's village.

**4. Earlier events are told in the simple past, with no "had". (throughout)**

"On Monday she cracked a rib on the right side" (1, on Tuesday night). "On Monday afternoon he telephoned to have the gate raised" (1, on Tuesday morning). "She tried them on the night after the flood" (2). "He asked her for it in the hall on Saturday, and she gave it to him." (25, on Sunday).

In a book with two villages, two Decembers twelve years apart and one woman's memories arriving in another woman's head, the reader's first question at every sentence is: when is this, and whose is it? The prose withholds the one small word that answers it. This is the largest single tax on an ordinary reader and it is in every chapter.

What I would do: put "had" on the first verb each time the story steps back. It needs no more than that.

**5. The hall of doubles and its empty chair are set up as a third way out and never used. (13, 15)**

"'That's yours, with your card on it. Sit down in that and you're not hers and you're not yours. You're nobody's. There's nobody to fetch a person back out of there.'" (15)

After that I waited for somebody to sit in it, or nearly. Chapter 21 goes back to the steel room and the hall is not there. The chair returns only as the empty chair in the real hall after the flood, which is a different chair with a different meaning.

What I would do: decide. Either the hall is where the loser of chapter 21 can end up, and one of them looks at it, or Kévin's theory goes and the hall stays as an image only. If the dream chair was Madame Etcheto's all along, one line has to say so.

**6. Chapter 1 asks me to carry too much before it pays. (1)**

The first page is as good an opening as I have read. Then come 2,500 words that introduce Coralie, Larrère, the postman, Jeannot, Marie-Jo, Dr Kessler and Sarthou, and the mairie, the commune, the association, the gate, the tide, the tonne, the forty-one letters, the lights, the loto, the Hontaube merger and the box. Chapter 2 then introduces most of them again in a second village.

And I did not follow the quarrel the chapter is about: "to have the gate raised before the tide turned, since the Adour was tidal this far up and the meadows drained only on the ebb". Raising the gate drains the meadows on Monday and floods them in a flood. I understood that in chapter 5. Until then I did not know what Jeannot was complaining of or what Larrère wanted.

What I would do: hold Hontaube, the lights and Dr Kessler's speech for chapter 3. Say once, plainly, in chapter 1, what the gate does in each direction.

**7. Maylis does not choose between the gate and the man. A rut does. (22)**

"She meant to do both, and the folder on his passenger seat could go to Pau on Monday." Then the van sinks, and: "She got out and ran."

Lesca chose in 2014: she rang once and turned the handle. Maylis's version of the same night is a plan to have both that fails at a dip she was warned about ("softer than where I went down in seventeen. You'd sit there till Easter"). It reads as a mistake. The question the book has asked for 21 chapters is never put to her as a question.

What I would do: the dip is the place. At 02:59 she is six hundred metres from the winch and four hundred from the hatch, on foot, with the bank at four minutes. Give her the paragraph in which she looks both ways, has the whole sum, and goes on to the hatch knowing what is behind her. Then Madame Etcheto is hers by choice, and chapter 25 has something to weigh.

**8. The comedy has one engine. (throughout)**

"'There's nothing here that lets me un-receive it. There's no column.'" (Ducasse, 11) "'That is a file. It is mine. It has a number.'" (Sarthou, 7) "'I've a tray for each, and a book.'" (Hontaube, 20) Add Larrère's minutes, Tastet's diary and Jeannot's numbered points.

Each is funny. They are the same joke: a person who can only meet the world through a procedure. By the fifth official I had the shape of the line before it came. "Minuted" appears twelve times, "They tell me" eleven, "I'll be brief" seven; Kévin shrugs fourteen times.

The people I will remember are the ones outside that joke: Madame Etcheto giving a demon its workload, Kévin and his forum, Coralie doing Larrère into the oven, Monsieur Daguerre.

What I would do: thin the minuting. Chapter 8 fires "I ask that that be minuted" three times in one scene; once does it. Spend the room on the four above.

**9. Chapter 25 settles every account in Maylis's favour. (25)**

Forty houses were flooded and a woman drowned because the keyholder left her gate. In one morning Larrère is brought down on foot, Kévin tows the van for nothing, Jeannot writes letter 42 on her behalf, the old joke is retired, and Lafitte blames himself: "I'd the pencil in my hand, and I wrote down Madame le maire." The verdict of the village is given second-hand and gently: "They told him he'd had the key and gone for a walk." "Nobody laughed."

In the other village one drowned man got Lesca a walk-out of seven councillors, a first deputy's sash in a carrier bag and a widow who wipes off her kiss for twelve years. Here nobody whose kitchen has a tide mark at table height says a hard word to her. Only Coralie's door is left open, and that one line carries the whole cost.

What I would do: let one person in the Port hold it against her to her face. Darrigrand's wife with the pram, or Sarthou before he turns to Larrère. And let somebody laugh at "gone for a walk".

**10. Larrère's secret is known eleven chapters before it is asked, and what he is guilty of stays vague. (9, 13, 16, 24, 25)**

His car is in the back lane in chapter 9. His folder is under Maïté's bread in 13. He all but says it himself in 16. So "'From whose telephone did you ring me, Guy?'" (24) asks what I already knew, and "Nothing came" is the answer to a different question from the one that matters.

What matters is in Maïté's mouth: "'He said if the gate stayed shut it made no odds where he was.'" He knew the man was out and said nothing to the keyholder, because saying it would have said where he was standing. That is never put to him. In the other village the charge is weaker still: "'An order to keep a gate shut, with the bank going, given from a kitchen you had no business in. That is the file.'" (25) I could not tell what the file was for. Being in the wrong kitchen is not an offence.

What I would do: change Lesca's question at the garden gate to the one Maïté has been asking for twelve years. Have Sarthou say in one sentence what the order did.

Smaller things that stopped me, not ranked:

- "She had each of them past the edge of the leaves, which lifted when she breathed in." (14) I read it three times.
- The site manager: "'Does Monsieur le maire want the weighbridge opened? He's had my tickets, and he's been on the telephone twice.'" (12) I did not know who had telephoned or why.
- The year is stated once, in chapter 9 ("It was December 2026"). Before that it is a sum: thirty-one in 2014, forty-three now.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The landing scene in chapter 18.** "'Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one.'" "'Nine minutes to three. I've had that for twelve years.'" And why she never asked: "'Because you'd have told me.'"
2. **Chapter 22 from the first timestamp to "Forty-one."** The clock times, "'Here.' 'I know you are.'", Kévin's five fingers, the walk past rooms nine, ten and eleven, Coralie taking the pulse.
3. **"Either", from the eggs to the grave.** The shopping-pad boxes, Tastet writing it down as consent, the woman at Hontaube who cannot put it in a tray, and the one time the question gets an answer.
4. **Madame Etcheto.** The care plan, "It hasn't come for you, girl. It comes to that bed", the nineteen sandbags counted aloud, and the handbag under her hand.
5. **Jeannot Castagnet alive.** The six numbered points down the telephone at five in the morning, and the last sentence of the book.
