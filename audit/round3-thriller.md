# Critic's report: Thriller

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `25.md`, in order, in full. 59,315 words.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Turned then, the gate keeps every bed in the Port dry, and hers with them. And it drowns Jeannot." (25) The sum is exact and every figure in it has a face. For thirteen chapters before it the stake is a reputation and a staff file.
- **Tension: 6.** "He asked for the register before she had blotted it, and read all three entries through, twice, with his finger under the lines." (8, last line) Chapters 19 to 22 are a 9. Chapters 7 to 10 are a 4.
- **Progression: 6.** "On Monday morning the other one left the chair first, and Lesca went with her." (14, first line) The first time either woman crosses is word 30,279 of 59,315. The book's premise goes into play at 51 per cent.
- **Comedy: 8.** "There's nothing here that lets me un-receive it. There's no column." (11)
- **Characters: 8.** "And six. Bugger off." (19) Jeannot alive is why the reader fears the gate.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." (22) The finger count, a joke since chapter 3, lands in flood water as the worst news in the book.
- **Prose: 8.** "It was the sound of a lorry tipping gravel, a long way to her right, and it did not stop." (22)
- **The ending: 8.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (25)
- **Clarity: 6.** "If it had not, there would be one of us, with both in her. The hand would go to whichever held on longer." (14) The rule the whole third act runs on is given once, as item 7 of a numbered note.

Finished it unpaid: yes, and I would have stopped twice on the way, in 7 and in 10.
Press it on a friend: yes. Not on the friend who lent me Dark Matter, as it stands.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch through chapter 13, level from 19, above him on the last page.** Overall below the bar as a thriller: a 7 with a 9 inside it.

- **Crouch has the double take the life by page thirty and keeps him in it.** Here the takeover is declared at the halfway mark ("and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it", 14) and exercised for one Tuesday of counting road signs. The two women share thirty lines in one room (21) and Maylis says three words to her. Crouch would have built the middle on that room.
- **Crouch ends a chapter on the turn.** Nine of these twenty-four endings are a door shutting, a page being read, a pillow staying put. The four that turn (4, 11, 14, 21) show the book can do it.
- **Above him on cost.** Crouch gives the family back. This book gives back nothing: the rescue kills the old woman the first flood spared, the wife kept to her own list and owns the death, and neither Coralie is seen to cross a landing. And the clock is better than his: a painted line on a pier, read fourteen times from "a metre sixty under" to "ninety-two over".

Where the reader is afraid, and of what: end of 4 (the flood has not happened there yet); 13, at the stop line; 14, of a wife's hand held by the wrong woman; 19 to 22, of everything. From 5 to 12 the reader is curious and not afraid.

Where a Crouch reader puts it down: at the postman in chapter 1; at the interval of the loto in 7; at Marie-Jo's doorstep in 10.

The reversal test: 02:51 is the minute of one unanswered ring, and that re-reads the first sentence of the book and twelve years of a wardrobe. It works. "The floor was dry. It was as dry as a kitchen" re-reads the five words. It works. Neither re-reads the whole, as the comparison's best reversal does.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. Nobody crosses until the book is half over (5 to 13).**
"On her left the rubber seal of the door gave. She kept her eyes on the lamp." (8) That door gives at word 20,000 and nobody goes through anything for another 10,000. Nine chapters run on leakage: a signature, a habit with a rake, a weal. Chapter 10 stops the story to compare the two lives by interview: "Say it had still been me in 2022. What would you have done?" Chapters 7 to 10 are 8,600 words in which the only event that changes anyone's position is the blackmail in 9.
*Do:* move the six o'clock crossing of 14 to where 8 now is. Fold what survives of 8 into 6. Keep the budget sleep from 10 and attach it elsewhere; put Marie-Jo's doorstep after the laugh in the minute, where it has something to cost. Cut the loto by a third (the 2020 list and the second half of the complaint).

**2. The takeover is announced and never exercised (16).**
"She took up the pencil in the left hand, ticked *soup*, and wrote *seven o'clock* beside it." Seven o'clock never comes. Lesca, who moved in writing to take a bed and a woman, spends her one day in the stolen life on "the signs of Peyrebarthe in the rain, on a board, with a pencil" and is fetched home at two in the afternoon. Her one try at Coralie is undercut for a laugh: "'Either,' the mouth said." So the reader is never afraid that Coralie might prefer the one who asks, though 15 says she nearly does ("This morning I'm asked, out loud, with my hand held").
*Do:* make the gravel last until night. Give Lesca the soup at seven, across the oilcloth from a Coralie who gets answers and likes them, with Maylis in the right hand having to watch. That is the scene that makes the marriage a stake. The signs can go to four lines.

**3. Lesca's letting go happens off the page (21 to 23).**
"The hand was hers. It lay on the wheel with the fingers open and nobody in it." (22) Then, after the fact: "She knew what put a woman on a track at three in the morning. She had said it with that woman's mouth." (23) Three chapters build her resolve to stay; its reversal is seen from outside in two sentences. Each woman's renunciation is shown through the other's eyes, and for Maylis that works, since a head turning to a telephone is the whole of her choice. Lesca's is the reversal of a written motion and needs its reason. Worse, Arlette has set the shortest count in the book against that minute ("Then you'll go where my daughter has sat down, and you'll fetch her back", 21), so the reader cannot tell whether Lesca chose or was fetched.
*Do:* run 21 on past the telephone, ninety seconds in the cab from inside the hand: what she reaches for, what the stall costs on the clock, what she is giving up named as she gives it. The book tells the medal twice and Monday at six twice; this is the one moment that needs it. Then either pay Arlette's count on the page or cut it.

**4. The message plot resolves without resistance, then goes stale (12, 13, 20, 22).**
Chapter 12 is spent getting five words right. They arrive as "five words that had come with nothing attached to them" at the foot of a note (13). The question the reader has carried since 6, whether a warning can cross, is answered in a subordinate clause. By 19 Maylis has the whole night; by dusk in 20 she has already been to the tonne. So when her mouth says the words at 02:53 they tell her nothing.
*Do:* let them fail to cross. Strike the line from the note in 13. Maylis walks to the hatch at dusk on what 19 gave her. The first time she hears the words is in her own mouth in the cab.

**5. The hall is the book's most frightening idea and nobody is ever in danger from it (13, 15).**
"Sit down in that and you're not hers and you're not yours. You're nobody's. There's nobody to fetch a person back out of there." (15) That is a third door, planted twice, with a warning on it. At the climax the steel room has its wall back and the kept chair is never offered to either woman. Its payoff as the forty-second chair (22) is good and is a different thing.
*Do:* when Lesca lets go of the hand, do not send her straight to a dry bed. Put her on the waxed boards with Inguma pinned under nobody and the chair four metres off, and make getting home cost her something. This is also where Arlette's count could be heard to finish.

**6. Chapter endings that close (3, 7, 8, 10, 24).**
"'I told her there would.'" (7) "Marie-Jo took it in against her chest with the cloth, stepped back, and shut the door." (10) Each is a good last line for a story and a bad one for a chapter with seventeen more behind it.
*Do:* end 7 on "He's dead there. She drove into him" and move the minibus before it. End 8 on the seal giving. End 3 on Maylis saying the words to the wardrobe and the clock reaching 02:51. Chapter 10's ending goes with the move in fault 1.

**7. The confession has no consequence on the page, and the medal is dropped (23, 24).**
"Lesca stood where she had been put and waited to be told what she was now." The book never tells her. A woman says into a television camera that she knowingly drowned a man, and eight hours later she is up his widow's ladder with no deputy, gendarme, prefect or reporter having spoken to her. Larrère's punishment in this village is a silence at a gate. The medal, which Maylis answered with "*I want that*" (7), was last seen in a safe in 17.
*Do:* one scene between the stone and the kitchen. The young man from Pau has a clipboard and no line for this; let him ask for the case back, or let her take it to the wreath. Let Tastet say what the council does on Tuesday.

**8. The rule that makes the second flood hers is asserted against the book's own dates (6, 18).**
"I kept that flood on my side for twelve years, and I have let it across. That flood is mine." By the same page the rain over there began on the night of the 24th, when a rib put Maylis on her back; Lesca did nothing on purpose until the 26th. Either the flood crossed because of a winch the council took note of, or because of her. The answer decides what "Last night I sent it to her" (24) weighs, and nobody in either village tests it.
*Do:* let her catch the date order herself and amend finding 3 in the notebook. She would. It is a turn, and it leaves her with the five words as the one thing that is certainly hers.

**9. Larrère's threat is built for a page and spent in a clause (20, 22).**
"If that gate moves tonight before I ring, this goes to Pau on Monday, registered." Then: "She meant to do both, and the folder on his passenger seat could go to Pau on Monday." The pension never weighs a gram at the decision, and since the gate never moves it is never tested. The antagonist's whole third act is one call.
*Do:* use what is already there. "I ring once more tonight. Be in that van." Let him ring while the van is axle-deep in the dip and the telephone is on the seat.

**10. Chapter 1 sets the hook in fifteen lines and leaves it for 2,800 words.**
The thing on the wardrobe and the stone are on page one. The box, which is the premise, arrives as a café joke two-thirds through: "the cat that is alive and dead until somebody lifts the lid, with Maylis for the cat."
*Do:* move the 2014 paragraph up into the bedroom, under the weight, where the smell of steel and tobacco can be the barn's. The reader then carries "they looked for two of her" across the square, and the first line of 2 lands as an answer.
