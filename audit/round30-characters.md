# Round 30 — critic's report, lens: Characters

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** "The trapdoor was twenty-five minutes away on foot. The bank had ten." (9) One man against forty-two beds, in one sum, and both sides have faces.
- **Tension: 7.** "She pushed until 4 again, flat on the kitchen floor with her forearm across her side." (7) The second night repeats the first. Chapters 6 to 8 go slack in the same way before 11 to 13 pull tight.
- **Progression: 6.** "'I asked where you'd be,' Coralie said. She went up to the spare room." (9) This is the sixth scene since chapter 4 in which Coralie asks, Maylis deflects and Coralie leaves on a line.
- **Comedy: 8.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (6) The joke comes out of what the man is.
- **Characters: 8.** "'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much.'" (16) The minor cast is at this level throughout. The two leads together are a 7.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 9.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" (16) The running joke stops mid-word, and that is how the reader learns what the flood has done to this house.
- **Prose: 7.** "This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel." (12) The narrator explains what the handbrake had just shown.
- **The ending: 8.** "Then he stepped over the string in his rubber boots and began to dig." (16) It costs her Coralie, the job and the village's good opinion, and the one man who helps is the one she chose.
- **Clarity: 6.** "'Your river made up two days in an afternoon, so only the last night counts.'" (8) I followed the rule, but I had to stop and work it out. It is the sixth rule the chair has been given.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes.

**Would I press it on a friend?** Yes, with a warning to get past chapter 3.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Above Crouch.** Three things put it there.

- Both versions are on the page and nobody could mistake one for the other. Crouch keeps his second Jason offstage. Here the low voice and the high voice, the motion and the job sheet, and the right hand against the left do the work in every shared scene.
- Crouch's secondary people are functions. Here Jeannot, Kévin, Sarthou, Maïté, Madame Etcheto and Larrère would each be missed.
- Neither woman is let off. Lesca says "I wouldn't have stopped" (16). Maylis says "I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me" (16).

**Below The Good Place.** Three things put it there.

- Its leads change each other by talking. These two hold meetings. The only exchange between them as two people is eight lines on the last page: "'Is yours in the house?'"
- Its people are all wrong, including the ones who judge. Coralie is the judge here in both villages, and the only person who ever finds her wrong is Coralie.
- Its central relationship moves every episode. Maylis and Coralie stand still from chapter 4 to chapter 9.

**Verdict on this lens:** better than Crouch, not yet as good as The Good Place. The villagers clear the bar; the central pair does not.

**The brief's questions, answered directly.**

- *Distinct, wanted-for, alive?* Distinct, yes. Alive, yes. Lesca's want is written out and is the best sentence of her: "*That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*" (3) Maylis's want is delivered mostly by reflex (fault 5).
- *Softened or excused?* Lesca is, by omission, at the moment she decides to cross (fault 2). Maylis is not excused by anyone in the book, but the death she causes is the gentlest one available (fault 6).
- *Described from outside as a woman?* No. I found no instance. Neither lead is physically described at all beyond a voice, a rib and a scar. Josiane's "She lives with a nurse, and that's to throw us off" (5) is the only line that looks at the couple from outside, and the joke is on Josiane.
- *Who would be missed?* Jeannot first, then Kévin, Madame Etcheto, Sarthou, Maïté. Not Arlette on Maylis's side, not Nadine, not Bixente.
- *Which relationship carries the book, and does it pay?* Maylis and Coralie carry it by page count, and it pays: "the car door shutting on a pillow" (16). Maylis and Jeannot is the one that is alive in every scene, and it pays better.
- *Does the reader care who lives?* Yes for Jeannot. Yes for Coralie, for about thirty lines. Madame Etcheto's death is felt but expected.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Coralie and Maylis play one scene six times (chapters 4 to 9).**
"She ticked both." (4) "'For a minute there I had somebody.'" (5) "'At work.'" (7) "'It's twenty minutes to Bayonne.'" (8) "'And then there's me, as a distance.'" (8) "'I asked where you'd be.'" (9)
Each time Coralie asks, Maylis gives a measurement or a silence, and Coralie delivers a line and leaves the room. By chapter 8 the reader has the pattern and is waiting for it to end. Chapter 8 alone holds three of them. The relationship that carries the book has no movement for a third of its length, so when Maylis does ask in chapter 11 it reads as the plot's timing and not as hers.
*Do:* cut to three. Keep the two boxes in 4, the pillow in 7 and the pad in 8. Drop the Bayonne test in 8 and the exit in 9: end that scene on "'I'll have to ask you everything like that'" and the hand on the rib, which is the only time in the middle of the book these two are happy.

**2. Lesca's decision to cross, knowing she is the water, is never made on the page.**
"'I've worked out what I'd be giving up, and I've written it down. Nil.'" (8) "In her own office Lesca could tell herself anything." (10)
From chapter 8 she knows that her arrival breaks the other bank. She is a woman who recites "forty houses and a care home" every December. In chapter 10 she never once thinks of the other forty-two, whom she ordered out of their beds herself in chapter 5. The narration notes the self-deception and moves on, which lets her off. Her reversal in the van then comes from a calculation the reader was not inside. In the climax I was afraid of her and not for her.
*Do:* the place is already built. In chapter 10 Coralie presses down eight strips of tape and says the other thirty-four died "in those beds, of the things people die of". The next line is "It took her less than a minute." Put the other Port there: tonight those beds are full, and she is what is coming for them. Let her count them and still go.

**3. The two women never speak as two people until the last page.**
"'A meeting needs somebody in the chair. Since neither of us is in it, I'll chair from here.'" (4)
They meet face to face five times. Four are business: rules, dates, the gate, a refusal. Neither asks the other one personal question until "'Is yours in the house?'" (16). The premise promises one woman looking at what she would have been. The book gives her an agenda.
*Do:* in the second chair scene of chapter 8, where nothing false can be said standing, let Maylis ask what Lesca's Coralie is like, and let Lesca find out what will and will not come out of her mouth. It would also show why the open door in chapter 10 is not enough for her, which at present is one clause: "in a house where nobody had ever picked up that phone".

**4. Coralie is never caught. She only confesses.**
"'I've had the form since March. I posted it today, and I posted it for me.'" (7)
She kept an envelope behind the fruit bowl for nine months while drawing boxes for a woman who would not say what she wanted. She tells the intruder first. Maylis's only thought on it is kind: "She had waited since March to tell anybody." (8) In both villages Coralie gets the last line of nearly every scene she is in, and the only charges against her are the ones she brings herself: "'That part is mine.'" (16) A person who is always right about everyone, herself included, is a verdict.
*Do:* give Maylis the envelope, once, said flat, in chapter 8 or 16. Give Coralie no answer to it. She should leave the house as someone who also kept quiet.

**5. Everything good Maylis does, she does before she can decide to.**
"It was out before she had time to think about it." (1) "She had gone back in and paid for them before she knew what she was doing." (4) "It had come out before she had a reason to give for it." (4) "Her right hand went out before she had decided anything." (5) "It was out before she could stop the mouth." (6) "Maylis hadn't meant to say it on a doorstep" (11)
Six times the narrator tells us she did not choose it. The effect is to excuse her from wanting. It also wears as a construction. When she finally chooses, at the dip, the book has trained the reader to think her hands do her choosing for her.
*Do:* keep two, "You" in 2010 and the hand on the falling gate. Make the chrysanthemums, the apology to Maïté and the timer things she sees herself decide.

**6. The night kills the one person the book has prepared us to lose, and gives Coralie back too soon.**
"'I won't sleep in a chair. I've slept in room twelve since I came here, and I'll make them put me back at 10.'" (11)
She is 94. She chose the room. The lore said in chapter 2 that such a woman "dies in her own bed". Coralie takes a share. Each cushion is good on its own; together they make Maylis's dead cost less than Lesca's, a man of 58 locked in a cellar. And the one fear that would cost more is spent in a page: Coralie goes missing and "came up the ramp on the rail, hand over hand" about thirty lines later.
*Do:* do not change who dies. Move Coralie's return to after the dark interval, when the one van has gone and the woman is holding Maylis's sleeve. Let Maylis push chairs through that dark not knowing.

**7. The chair hands over what a scene should earn.**
"Maylis knew why. She had been given the reason in the chair." (6) "Lesca wasn't in it yet, but the night of the flood came to Maylis anyway" (9)
Kévin's prison, Marie-Jo's sash and the whole night of 2014 arrive as transfers. The night in chapter 9 is the central fact about Lesca and it reaches Maylis with nobody in the room. Lesca does not give it, and Maylis does not take it. It is nobody's act.
*Do:* put Lesca in the chair for chapter 9, seated and silent while it goes across. She said "'That's not a no'" was unanswerable two nights before; this is her answering it by not getting up.

**8. Too many people end on the same kind of line.**
"'It's four when you're the one counting.'" (10) "'Nobody emptied hers.'" (15) "'I know what you picked. I was the other one.'" (16) "'That's what you'd keep.'" (16)
Darrigrand, Madame Etcheto, Coralie, Arlette and Maïté all close a scene with a short turned sentence in the same cadence. The men are told apart by syntax: Jeannot numbers, Larrère announces he will be brief, Sarthou files a report, Kévin asks questions. The women other than Maïté, who breaks off, share one voice for the last word.
*Do:* take the closing line away from one scene in three. Start with Coralie's.

**9. Lesca's first chapter is spent on a file and a cast list.**
"Marie-Jo Labat turned a white kitchen timer to three and stood it in front of him." (3)
In 2,500 words the reader meets Lesca, a second Coralie, a second Larrère, Marie-Jo, Maïté as a widow, Arlette, the stone, the timer, the verb, five lines of river levels and a motion. Lesca's feeling for the dead man gets six lines at the stone. A reader sorting who is whom in the second village is not meeting her.
*Do:* cut the Arlette kitchen scene from this chapter. It repeats lore from chapter 2, and its one essential line, that her mother never called her anything but Maylis, can open chapter 10.

**10. Maylis's mother has one scene and no ending.**
"'My ponies are on that grass, Maylis.'" (8)
Lesca's mother gets three scenes and the right last word. Maylis's gets a tap and a bucket, and is absent from chapter 16, the morning after her daughter lost the Port.
*Do:* cut her from chapter 8, or give her three lines in 16. Cutting is cheaper and the chapter is the longest of the slack ones.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Jeannot never thanks her.** "'It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you.'" He votes Shut because she threatened him, he will not come up for a number, and on the last page he calls her "Lesca", without "little", for the first time.
2. **Kévin's hand in the flood, and what he says about it afterwards.** "'Five,' he said." (13) "'I wanted six, Maylis.'" (16)
3. **The square in chapter 14, from "'I knew that he was'" to the chairs.** "A child asked what the lady had done. Somebody told the child." Then Marie-Jo says her name and gives her work to do.
4. **The owner keeps her right hand.** The tipped spoon, the shut tap, the stair post, the handbrake. This is where Maylis is a person while someone else is using her mouth.
5. **The two Coralies' opposite silences.** One asks for sixteen years and gets "Either". The other never asked for twelve: "'Because you'd have told me.'" (10) And the landing in chapter 7: "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'"
