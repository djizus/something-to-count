# Critic's report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "If she turned that handle, a man drowned and her hand had done it. If she went on, whatever the river did behind her was the river's doing." (13)
- **Tension: 7.** "One breath to a ring was all the rib would give her." (11)
- **Progression: 6.** "'So that's a fourth point, and I didn't have it on my list.'" (4) The middle keeps adding rules and meetings where it should add events.
- **Comedy: 7.** "'It's in the plan now. Staff to wait. Staff not to join in.'" (2) Good, and sustained through chapter 8. Not yet level with The Good Place.
- **Characters: 7.** "'It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you.'" (16) Jeannot and Coralie are 8s. Lesca is a 6.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" (16)
- **Prose: 7.** "Twenty-five minutes was a trailer of leaves to the dump and back. Ten was the time it took to hook the trailer up." (7) Exact and plain. The tics are "a hand of water", the few centimetres, and two fingers on the inside of the wrist.
- **The ending: 8.** "Then he stepped over the string in his rubber boots and began to dig." (16)
- **Clarity: 6.** "'Your river made up two days in an afternoon, so only the last night counts.'" (8) I had to stop and do that sum. A reader doing sums is not smiling.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes.
**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who loved The Good Place, yes. On a Crouch reader, not until chapters 4 and 8 carry fewer rules.

## 2. Against the comparisons

Crouch does not attempt comedy, so on this lens the book is above him by default. The real comparison is The Good Place. Verdict: **below overall, level in one thing, above in one thing.**

- **Level: how the running jokes end.** The Good Place builds a gag until a reversal makes it mean something else. This book does the same and does it by silence. Coralie's oven-door Larrère plays twice and breaks off the third time at "The—". Larrère's own "I'll be brief" ends at a garden gate with "He said nothing." The café's "Still in the box, were you?" comes back as "Had the key and went for a walk, did you?" Each joke is retired by the plot. That is as good as the comparison.
- **Below: the range of wrongness.** The Good Place has four people wrong in four different ways. Peyrebarthe is wrong in one way. Jeannot numbers his grievances, Lesca moves motions, Larrère consults, Sarthou files, Bordenave asks what to put, Marie-Jo counts chairs, Coralie rules columns, the secretary minutes, Hontaube wants a form, Pau has a sheet. It is one joke, procedure applied to the wrong thing, worn by ten people. Only Josiane, Kévin, Arlette and Monsieur Daguerre are funny by other means.
- **Below: the rules.** The Good Place explains its afterlife at speed, as jokes, and never asks the viewer to audit it. Here the reader must hold who drives, who keeps the right hand, sitting against standing, truth when standing, what Inguma counts, and that the visitor brings the rain. From chapter 5 to chapter 8 I was tracking hands as often as I was smiling.
- **Above: the cost.** The Good Place can reset. This book lets its comic word kill its funniest old woman and does not take it back. "'Forty-one,' Coralie said." Then, a chapter later, "Twelve. One, two. Somebody pulled a chair in to a table."

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Chapter 15 throws away the book's biggest comic reversal in one sentence.**
"On Tuesday the council gave Lesca three minutes to speak against her own removal, and she turned the timer back to nothing herself. Eleven hands went up, and when the secretary's pen stopped at 'Madame le—', Lesca said, 'Lesca, Maylis. Surname first.'"
The kitchen timer has been built since chapter 3 and paid once in chapter 6, when Larrère runs eleven minutes ("'Eleven years I've been timed like an egg'"). The woman who made the rule being timed by it is the Good Place reversal. It is given as a summary, with a callback to the bingo card that a reader has to decode. The whole chapter is 768 words holding five payoffs, each a punchline with no room around it.
*Do:* play the council as a scene. Marie-Jo stands the timer in front of Lesca and, for the first time, speaks to her face and not the wall. Larrère has three minutes and uses none. Lesca winds the dial to nothing. Six hundred words.

**2. The cast shares one comic engine.** (throughout; worst in 8)
"She ruled two lines down it, as she did for a resident's chart, and wrote a heading over each of the three columns." This comes four chapters after Lesca's "three points, and I'll say them once" and between Jeannot's "One… Two… Three" and Sarthou's "Then this file contains one name". By chapter 8 I could predict how each new person would be funny.
*Do:* ration it. Numbered points belong to Jeannot alone. Motions belong to Lesca alone. Take the columns away from Coralie, whose own device is the tick-box, and it is better. Let Sarthou be pompous without a file. Give the Hontaube woman and the young man from Pau something other than a form each.

**3. The mid-book reveal and its joke land on top of each other.** (7, then 8)
"'You're the water.'" / "'She wants water,' one of the Lassalle sisters said."
The hall's misreading is only funny if the reader already knows what the three words mean. The clue is given that afternoon ("She had nobody to say it to and no mouth to say it with") but not the conclusion, so at the bar I was working out the reveal while the Lassalle sisters were being funny about it. Chapter 8 then opens with 500 words of Coralie's chart explaining what the joke has already spent.
*Do:* let Maylis reach the conclusion in plain words on the platform on Saturday afternoon. Then the reader is ahead of the hall and every misreading pays. Cut the chart scene to its third column and Coralie's "A tide comes in twice a day. It doesn't take Thursday off."

**4. The stuck-word device is used until it is machinery, and the steel-room meetings stop being scenes.** (4, 8)
"'It's the ti—' Lesca said. She started again, and it stuck on the same word." Then "'I w—'" and "'This time I'm coming to get—' It stuck there."
In chapter 4 the meeting is a comic scene with its own build: "'Then it's carried, or it's a tie, and in a tie the chair decides.' 'There's nobody in the chair.'" The truth rule arrives as a gag ("'Tell me it's Tuesday'"). By chapter 8 the same stop-mid-word has fired five times and is doing Lesca's confessing for her. The two chapter 8 meetings have no agenda joke at all.
*Do:* two uses. Tuesday in chapter 4, "To stay" in chapter 8. Cut "ti—" and "I w—". Merge the two chapter 8 meetings into one and give it the chairing business back, so that "She didn't call anything to order" in chapter 16 has something recent to answer.

**5. Lesca stops being good company at the foot of the stairs and the book goes on using her for jokes.** (5, 7)
"By now Lesca had the voice nearly right, higher, the way Maylis used it."
She tries to walk another woman's body up to Coralie's bed, then imitates that woman's voice to ask Coralie into it. After that her minutes-voice is still played for smiles. "You can put that in the minutes as apologies received" (12) is the best line she has, and I smiled at it less than I should have, because she had not yet faced the stairs. She owns it only in chapter 16, in two sentences.
*Do:* move the owning forward. In chapter 10, alone with the speech, she goes over the man and the phone. Give her the stair post there, in her own procedural terms, as a motion she would have sent back down the table.

**6. Coralie, the funniest person in the book, keeps stopping to explain herself.** (4, 7, 10)
"'I've held hands for a living since I was nineteen, so I know when a hand is holding on, and I know when it wants to be let go.'" / "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'"
She is funny when she acts: the towel, the oven, "'Eggs is what Either gets in this house.'" In these speeches she supplies her own credentials and her own moral. The Bayonne and Ithurbide history is delivered on a landing in the middle of a three-way standoff over a wrist. In chapter 10 the other Coralie speaks almost unbroken for 600 words.
*Do:* cut every clause in which she cites her job as authority for a feeling. Move Ithurbide to the Sunday kitchen in chapter 8. Break the chapter 10 speech with Lesca's coat, the wardrobe, the four sheets.

**7. The fight in the van reuses the body comedy straight, at the climax.** (12)
"The right hand let go of the wheel and covered the eyes. Lesca kept the foot down, blind." / "The stop threw the body forward onto the steering wheel, and the horn sounded once across the fields."
One body fighting itself has been played for laughs twice, with the tipped spoon and the overflowing coffee. A hand over the driver's eyes and a honk are clown beats. I smiled, at the one moment the book least wants it.
*Do:* cut the eyes and the horn. Wheel, then handbrake, then "You're the water."

**8. Forty-one and forty-two each mean two things.** (1, 5, 13, 16)
"'Put it in forty-two.'" / "forty-two people in their beds" / "'Forty-one,' Coralie said."
Jeannot's letters stand at forty-one sent and forty-two pending. The care home holds forty-two and loses one. The darkest line in the book carries the number of the book's most-repeated joke. At the end of chapter 13 I thought of registered letters for half a second.
*Do:* if the rhyme is meant, nothing on the page says so and it costs the line. Move the letters to another number.

**9. The narrator and the cast point at jokes that have already landed.** (5, 6)
"Nobody in Peyrebarthe had ever heard Maylis's mouth forbid anything." / "'It took his first name to get the glass out of the way.'" Josiane's maxim, "because she's after the husband or because she's after the wife", is given in chapter 5 and repeated nearly word for word by Larrère in chapter 6.
*Do:* cut the first two. In chapter 6 let Larrère get the maxim slightly wrong; the topper, "Josiane had still got there first", survives.

**10. Lesca leaves the hall of the dead by walking out.** (12)
"A hall with no walls had no door, so she took the aisle between the second row and the third."
The bingo of the dead is the book's best comic invention and gets about 500 words on stage. The refusal is funny. The exit costs nothing in the scene, and the cost only appears two chapters later.
*Do:* let the caller answer the apology in the scene, in the caller's own terms: a place laid is a place filled. The reader then carries that into chapter 13.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Larrère's window and Coralie's oven door.** The gap, the window going "all the way down, into the door", "I'm told I wave", the oven impression twice, then "The—" and "He said nothing."
2. **Jeannot's numbered points, through to letter 42.** "'One. Those are my boots.'" "'And six. Bugger off.'" "A man had just sworn at her in six points, and he was alive." "'It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you.'"
3. **Bingo night and its double.** The tablecloths lifting, "'Tooth,' Marie-Jo said into the microphone", Madame Etcheto finishing the words over her duck, "'How's that spelled?'", then the same hall with the dead in it and "Twelve. One, two."
4. **Either.** From "'I don't cook Either. There's no recipe for it'" to "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station'" to "'I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it.'"
5. **Kévin's fingers.** "'I want to fly. As high as the church. Is that stupid?'" to the count in the flooded yard to "'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much.'" "'It was five.'"
