# Critic's report: Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. About 42,000 words, 4,344 sentences, mean sentence length 9.7 words.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Behind the bank there are forty houses, and the care home. And out on the fields, in his duck hut, there is one man—" (ch. 5). The sum is stated early and never blurs. It is a village's stakes, not a world's, and the book does not pretend otherwise.
- **Tension: 7.** "The gate was six hundred metres behind her. The red reflector was four hundred metres in front. The bank had six minutes left." (ch. 13). Chapters 9 to 13 hold. Chapters 6 to 8 do not: three rounds of who has the jaw.
- **Progression: 6.** "So that's a fourth point, and I didn't have it on my list." (ch. 4). The rules are still being amended in ch. 8 ("Now I've got both"), so the middle spends its pages changing the terms and not raising the price.
- **Comedy: 7.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station." (ch. 6). Every speaker has a machine of their own and the machines collide. The possession farce in ch. 7 is the one place I stopped smiling, because I was counting hands.
- **Characters: 8.** "I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it." (ch. 16). Coralie is the best thing in the book and is never described from outside.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'It's Saturday.' 'Then they'll want the bins out,' he said." (ch. 13), said in a flooded corridor four lines before Coralie comes up without Madame Etcheto. The jokes are left running during the worst hour and none of them is at its expense.
- **Prose: 7.** "She wanted it not to be her." (ch. 13) is the book at full strength: seven plain words carrying the whole moral. Against it: "She took the right wrist in the left hand and dragged it off the face." (ch. 12), which is a wiring diagram. Chapters 9 to 16 are an 8. Chapters 5 to 8 are a 6.
- **The ending: 8.** "He went and got it. Then he stepped over the string in his rubber boots and began to dig." (ch. 16). It costs a life, a job and a marriage, and nobody is thanked.
- **Clarity: 5.** "She put up the left hand. Lesca's right let go of the timer and went up beside it." (ch. 6). To read that sentence I must hold which village, whose body, who is driving, which hand the owner keeps and which Coralie is in the house. The book asks for that ledger on most pages between ch. 5 and ch. 12.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. From ch. 9 I did not stop. Chapters 6 to 8 I read on credit earned by ch. 3.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who reads slowly, yes. On the Crouch reader the bar names, no: that reader leaves in ch. 6 or ch. 7.

## 2. Against the comparisons, for prose

**Against Crouch: above on the sentence, below on ease. Net level.**

- Crouch writes transparent prose that costs nothing to read, and he names the feeling outright. This book names a feeling twice ("She was scared.", prologue and ch. 13) and otherwise makes objects do it: a pillow going down on a single bed, a tick left on a pad, a hand staying on a bandage "a little longer than a bandage needs". That is better writing than Crouch's.
- Crouch never makes the reader keep accounts. His premise rules are said once and then only dramatised. Here the rules arrive as "points" in ch. 4, gain a fourth in the same scene, a weather rule in ch. 8 and a hall of the dead in ch. 8 and ch. 12, and each one changes how the sentences have to be read. A Crouch chapter can be read at speed. Chapters 5, 7 and 12 here cannot.
- Crouch ends a chapter on a threat or a reveal. This book does it as well as he does in ch. 2 ("'Come back across,' her mouth said. Maylis hadn't said it."), ch. 8 ("LESCA, MAYLIS.") and ch. 13 ("'Forty-one,' Coralie said."). It ends ch. 6, ch. 9 and ch. 15 on a settling-down.

**Against The Good Place: level on voices, below on carrying the rules.**

- The Good Place gives each person one way of being wrong and lets it speak. So does this: Jeannot numbers his grievances, Larrère promises to be brief, Sarthou speaks like his own report ("I do not record that I would have stayed"), Lesca moves motions, Coralie draws boxes. No line of dialogue could be given to a different speaker. That is level with the comparison.
- The Good Place explains a rule as a joke and then never asks you to remember it under pressure. This book's best rule joke ("Then it's carried, or it's a tie, and in a tie the chair decides." "There's nobody in the chair.") is as good, but the reader is then examined on the rules for eight chapters.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. The driven-body chapters are written as a parts list (ch. 5, 7, 8, 12). Structural.

> "Lesca pulled at the jaw and Maylis held it, so all Josiane got was a noise through the nose." (ch. 7)
>
> "The right hand let go of the wheel and covered the eyes. Lesca kept the foot down, blind. She took the right wrist in the left hand and dragged it off the face." (ch. 12)

Whenever one woman is inside the other, the narrator stops saying "her hand" and says "the hand", and stops saying "she" and says the name. Counts: ch. 5 has "Lesca" 41 times and 37 article-nouns (the mouth, the eyes, the left hand, the legs, the chest) in 2,869 words. Ch. 12 has 25 and 34 in 1,902 words. In the track walk in ch. 7, "Lesca" is the subject of twelve consecutive sentences. The device is right in principle: "the mouth" tells me it is nobody's. In bulk it turns the book's most frightening and funniest idea into bookkeeping, and the reader's attention goes to the ledger and off Maylis. This is where the clarity score is lost, and where a Crouch reader stops.

What I would do: decide per scene, in one sentence, who has what ("Maylis had her right hand and nothing else" does it in ch. 5), then write the driver as "she" and the body as "her" until something is contested. Keep "the jaw", "the mouth", "the wheel" only at the moment two wills are on them. Tell these scenes from what Maylis feels being walked, and stop narrating Lesca's actions limb by limb. The article-nouns should fall by more than half and the van fight in ch. 12 should fit in half its present length.

### 2. The narrator recaps what a scene has just shown, and the worst cases sit at the book's three turns (ch. 9, 10, 12, 16). Structural.

> "Maylis had been given the whole night, but nothing in it told her which of the two Lesca had turned the handle for." (ch. 9)
>
> "But on Wednesday, standing in the steel room, she hadn't been able to finish that sentence. What had come out was that she was going over to stay, in a house where nobody had ever picked up that phone." (ch. 10)
>
> "She had told Maylis there was no both. She had wanted both herself, the bed and the man. But the motion she had written at her own kitchen table was for a seat and a bed. There was no man in it." (ch. 12)

Ch. 9 states the forty-houses-or-Coralie question in narration, one chapter before Coralie asks it aloud in the best speech in the book. Ch. 10 then closes on five paragraphs that summarise ch. 5 and ch. 8 ("She had seen him, eleven days ago, on a stool in a café..."). Ch. 12 gives Lesca's surrender as a proof in three steps, in the middle of a fight in a van. Ch. 16 says the book's finding three times: Maylis to Kévin ("I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me"), Coralie ("That was Either, with the river in it"), Maylis to Lesca ("I drove past the handle to be the one who did").

What I would do: cut the two sentences in ch. 9. End ch. 10 at "nothing was sitting on it", then Coralie's line from the landing. In ch. 12 keep "This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel" and cut the paragraph before it. In ch. 16 keep Coralie's version, which is the only one that is also a joke, and cut the line to Kévin.

### 3. One explanatory capper after a shown beat, as a habit (throughout).

> "For a week she had been afraid of the handle on that gate. Now she was more afraid of her own stairs." (ch. 5)
>
> "It had always come when she needed it. It had never been true, and it wasn't true now. She did want one of them, badly." (ch. 4)
>
> "For one breath, Maylis had won." … "Inguma was gone. Maylis had lost." (ch. 7)
>
> "Nobody had given her this night. It was her own, and it was happening." (ch. 11)
>
> "She had put Lesca out, and it had come too late: the river was through the bank already." (ch. 13)

Each follows an action that already said it: the hand locked on the stair post, the word sticking in the truth room, the tablecloths lifting and lying down, the red reflector, the hand on moving clay. The bingo pair keeps score for a reader who has just watched the point being played. The ch. 13 line carries the only explanatory colon in the narration.

What I would do: delete all five. Where a capper is a joke, keep it ("For sixteen years Maylis had sat down and said nothing, in the wrong kitchen" in ch. 6 earns its place).

### 4. Every scene opens on the clock (throughout, worst in ch. 5).

> "At 8 Maylis was standing on the platform… At 9 Maylis was standing beside the village van… At noon Maylis felt her legs turn… At 7 there was soup… At 9:05 Coralie turned off the light" (ch. 5, five sections running)

Of 78 scene openings, 46 begin with an hour, a day or "That night". Add the weather report in the second clause ("with rain on the kitchen window", "it was raining", "there was frost on the window") and the book reads in its first half like the flood file it describes. The countdown needs dates. It does not need one at the head of every scene. The six scenes that open on speech are the best openings in the book: "'Hand,' Coralie said." (ch. 9), "'I'm heating the whole square,' Josiane said" (ch. 8).

What I would do: keep the stamp where the day changes or the hour matters to the sum (all of ch. 11 and ch. 13). Elsewhere open on the line or the object and let the time fall in the second sentence, or nowhere.

### 5. One rhythm for 42,000 words.

Mean sentence 9.7 words. 892 sentences of four words or fewer. 97 of twenty-five or more, most of them in speech. "She" opens 354 sentences and "The" 264. The trailing "with" phrase closes a sentence 148 times: "with her back turned", "with her bag on her arm", "with his back to them", "with rain falling on the roof". The plainness is the voice and it is the right voice. The sameness is something else: by ch. 8 I could feel each paragraph preparing to end on a short object sentence. Ch. 13 is the only chapter that changes gear, and it does it with verbless fragments ("Torch up. Right arm across her side.") that belong to a more ordinary thriller.

What I would do: let a sentence run when the action runs. The café scene in ch. 5 and the council in ch. 6 want one or two long, breathless sentences each. Halve the trailing "with" phrases by giving the detail its own verb. In ch. 13 give the fragments back their verbs: the chapter is fast enough without them.

### 6. The narrator tells the story by what does not happen.

> "But the man behind the glass paid her wages, so she didn't knock on it." (ch. 1)
>
> "Nobody came up the steps. Nobody shouted." (ch. 14)

426 negatives, one in every hundred words: 62 "didn't", 35 "hadn't", 51 sentences on "Nobody", 10 "never once". In ch. 1 this is characterisation, three times over in one shape: she wanted, but, so she didn't (the wrist, the duck, the window). It is exact for a woman whose word is Either. The narrator then uses the same construction for Lesca, Coralie, Larrère, Jeannot and the square, so it stops belonging to Maylis, and her first positive act in ch. 13 has less to push against.

What I would do: keep the negative for Maylis and for the village's silence about her. Rewrite the rest as what the person did do. "He left the kiss on it" (ch. 10) is the model.

### 7. Each person is compared only to her own job, and the reader sees the rule.

> Coralie: "in the voice she used for reading a dose off a chart" (ch. 4), "a chart of the kind she kept for a resident" (ch. 7), "for as long as she would have given a pulse" (ch. 7), "as she did for a resident's chart" (ch. 8), "the way she counted a pulse against the clock" (ch. 8), "the way she read from a patient's chart" (ch. 10).
>
> Maylis: "the way she read a job sheet" (ch. 6), "the way she wrote out any job" (ch. 8), "She worked it out the way she worked out a job" (ch. 13).

Six charts and pulses for Coralie, three job sheets for Maylis. The principle is good and it produces the best measures in the book ("Twenty-five minutes was a trailer of leaves to the dump and back"). Repeated as "the way she" plus the trade, it becomes a visible method.

What I would do: two for Coralie, one for Maylis, and let the trade show in what they do. Coralie taking a dead woman's pulse for the full half minute needs no simile and has none.

### 8. The stuck word, used seven times, and the tally of years, used thirty-five.

> "'It's—'", "got as far as 'Because'" (ch. 4), "'Ei—'" (ch. 5), "'It's the ti—'", "'I w—'", "'I'm coming to get—'" (ch. 8)
>
> "Maylis had stood beside that neck at this counter for eighteen years without once looking at it." (ch. 5)

The truth room's trick is a fine one and "To stay… That's the one that comes out" is its payoff. By the fourth use I knew the sentence would break before it did. Separately, the narrator dates every feeling: eleven, twelve, sixteen or eighteen years appear 35 times, most often as "for sixteen years she had…". In Coralie's mouth the number is a reproach and it works ("I've had sixteen and a shopping pad"). In the narrator's it is a filing habit.

What I would do: four stuck words, each on a lie of a different size, the last one "to get". Leave the year counts to the speakers.

### 9. One thing, two names; and three American words in a British book.

- The red line is on "the pillar" of "the old gate" (ch. 3, 8, 14) and the river is read off "the marker post" (ch. 7, 9, 13). I could not tell whether these are one object.
- "long white lights" (ch. 7, 8) become "strip lights" (ch. 12, 13, 14). "polished wooden floor" (ch. 8) becomes "waxed wooden floor" (ch. 12). The hall of the dead depends on the reader recognising the village hall at once, so the words should match exactly.
- "resident" everywhere, then "a patient's chart" (ch. 10) and "getting a patient out of it" (ch. 13).
- "the steel room" in narration, "the door of the box" in ch. 4 and ch. 8, in a book where "box" is also the handle box, the tick boxes, the cool boxes, the confession box and the first-aid box.
- "Jeannot Castagnet's duck hut" is one hut on the fields until ch. 6 ("the track to the duck huts") and ch. 11 ("the hunters' huts"). The sum of one man against forty houses needs it to be one.
- Lesca's closed hand is the left in ch. 3 ("Lesca's left hand went up against it still closed") and the right, with the scar, from ch. 4 on. Both are meant, but one chapter apart they read as a slip.
- The spelling is British (colour, metres, pavement, torch, rubbish bin, mum). "truck" (6), "dish towel" (7), "baby carriage" (2) and "dump" (2) are not.
- "the way you carry a child" and "the kind you turn by hand" (ch. 13): the only two places the narrator says "you".

What I would do: one name per object, chosen once. Lorry, tea towel, pram, tip.

### 10. Three places where the last chapters break the book's own terms.

> "The door opened behind her. A man came in out of the rain." (ch. 16)
>
> "Down in the Port a rocket went up from the bank and lit him red, then white." (ch. 15)
>
> "On Tuesday the council gave Lesca three minutes to speak against her own removal, and she turned the timer back to nothing herself." (ch. 15)

Ch. 7 and ch. 8 establish, in the room where only true things can be said, that the rain is Lesca: two nights without her and "the boards of the platform were dry". She leaves at 2:53 on Saturday. On Sunday a man still comes in "out of the rain", and on Monday, after "Don't come again" and "I won't", the priest stands "under his umbrella". The café line is there to echo ch. 1 and pays for the echo with the rule. In ch. 15 a firework goes up at the reception hours after the mayor has told the square she drowned the man it commemorates, and nothing prepares it. And the council that removes her, which eleven chapters of timers and votes have been building to, is given in two sentences of synopsis, in a chapter of 768 words made of four scraps. It is the only place the narrator summarises a scene the reader has been waiting for.

What I would do: a dry Sunday and a dry Monday, with the mud. Cut the rocket or plant the fireworks on the young man's sheet in ch. 10. Write the removal vote as a scene, a short one: Larrère, the timer, eleven hands, "Surname first."

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **"She wanted it not to be her."** and the three steps back towards the lamp before it (ch. 13). The moral of the book in seven words of one syllable or two.
2. **Coralie at the oven door, the third time** (ch. 16): "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'". A joke set up twice so that it can fail once. Nothing is said about why it fails.
3. **Room twelve and the count** (ch. 13): two fingers on the inside of the wrist "for the full half minute", the line through the last name, the hatbox on the wardrobe, then "'Forty-one,' Coralie said." Every gesture has been used before as comedy or tenderness, and none is explained here.
4. **Jeannot's phone call** (ch. 9): six numbered points ending "And six. Bugger off.", then "A man had just sworn at her in six points, and he was alive." The comic machine and the reason to save him are the same paragraph.
5. **Kévin's hand** (ch. 13 and ch. 16): "'Five,' he said." in the water, and "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much." The slightest strand in the book carries the heaviest line, and the answer is three words: "It was five."

Also leave alone: the sentence travelling "back through the square and down the road, row by row, at walking pace" (ch. 14), and "That village hadn't been spared its flood. It hadn't had it yet." (ch. 3).
