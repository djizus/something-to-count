# Round 31: Characters

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** 'The trapdoor was twenty-five minutes away on foot. The bank had ten.' The sum is clear and it is a person against a street. The second stake, the bed, is weaker because the reader has seen the couple happy only once.
- **Tension: 6.5.** Chapters 9 to 13 are an 8 or better. Chapters 7 and 8 run on a jaw: 'All Maylis had to do was keep the mouth shut until it came.'
- **Progression: 6.** 'You're the water' is said at the bar in chapter 7 and again in the van in chapter 12, and the kitchen fight in chapter 8 replays the bar fight of the night before. The middle contests one thing three times.
- **Comedy: 7.5.** 'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'
- **Characters: 7.** 'I've asked you one question for sixteen years, and drawn you boxes for it.' That is Coralie describing herself accurately, and it is why this is not an 8: for half the book she is that question.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** Kévin in the flooded yard at 4 a.m., a wheelchair half on his van, counting his fingers: ''Five,' he said.'
- **Prose: 7.** 'It wasn't much. She took more than that to the dump in a morning.' The plain voice is consistent and belongs to her. It also keeps every feeling at arm's length.
- **The ending: 7.5.** 'I know what you picked. I was the other one.' It costs a death, a job and a marriage. It is short of 8 because the marriage is understood more than mourned.
- **Clarity: 6.** 'A minute ago she had been asleep in a steel chair, with somebody warm in it beside her.' I had to read the opening of chapter 3 twice to know whose body, which village and which Coralie. The rule that a lie sticks in the steel room is never said, only tested.

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes, and from chapter 9 without putting it down.
Would I press it on a friend? No. I would describe it to a friend, and the friend would ask me to explain it.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Verdict for this lens: one point below the bar on the couple, above it on the double and on the village.**

- **Above Crouch on the double.** In Dark Matter the second Jason is a thief with the hero's face. Lesca has a claim. She is funnier than Maylis, she answers when asked, she is partly right, and the book lets the lover prefer her out loud: 'I'd have this one again.' Crouch never risks that.
- **Above Crouch on everybody else.** His secondary people are functions. Here the reader would miss Jeannot ('And six. Bugger off.'), Madame Etcheto ('That's my door'), Kévin ('I wanted six, Maylis'), Sarthou ('I do not record that I would have stayed'), the Arlette of the other village ('Don't put me in that'), and Marie-Jo speaking to the wall. Nobody would miss Josiane, Bordenave, Bixente or Nadine, and nobody needs to.
- **Below Crouch on fear for the marriage.** Crouch spends his first chapter making the reader want that kitchen. This book gives one evening of eggs, and by the second scene Coralie is already at the end of sixteen years of patience. The reader is told the marriage is worn before being shown what it is worth.
- **Level with The Good Place on people wrong in their own way.** Jeannot will not be told, so he votes his own hut dry and goes to it because he was forbidden. Coralie would not ask, because 'you'd have told me', and would not wake a woman of 94 for orange. Lesca let it ring once. Maylis said Either with her weight on the handle. Each fault causes something.
- **Below The Good Place on the leads changing each other.** Eleanor and Chidi alter each other by arguing. Maylis and Lesca meet five times and hold a meeting. The change is delivered by a mechanism, the sentence that will not come out, and not by one woman getting through to the other.

**The lens questions, answered.**
Distinct: yes, by speech alone. One thinks in wheelbarrows and trailers, the other in items and motions.
Wanted-for: Lesca yes, from 'and Coralie Mounet in it'. Maylis wants only to stop things until chapter 11.
Softened: Lesca is, by placement (fault 4). Maylis is not.
Described as a woman and not a person: neither lead. Maïté is (fault 9).
Which relationship carries it: the book says Maylis and Coralie. The one that pays is Maylis and Jeannot.
Do I care who lives: Jeannot and Madame Etcheto, very much. Either woman, no. The card with her name on it never frightened me.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The marriage is shown working once, and Coralie's own want arrives in chapter 7 in one speech.**
Chapter 7: 'I've had the form since March. I posted it today, and I posted it for me.' Chapter 4 plants it blind: 'an envelope that had been standing there since March'.
For six chapters Coralie has no wish that is not an answer from Maylis. So when she packs, the reader agrees with her and does not grieve. The book's first stake is a bed and the reader has not been made to want it.
Do: put Bayonne in chapter 1. The form is behind the fruit bowl, Maylis has seen it, and she has said nothing about it for eight months. Her silence then has a price with a name on it from the first evening. Put Ithurbide, the torch and the list into the room twelve scene in chapter 2, where Coralie is already running a corridor. And give the couple a second good scene before the mayor arrives. The bandage in the teeth that opens chapter 9 is that scene, and it comes eight chapters late.

**2. Maylis is a brake for the whole middle, and she never once reaches for Coralie.**
Chapter 5: 'Maylis had her right hand and nothing else.'
Count what that hand does: it pulls away from the pulse, holds the stair post, tips the spoon, shuts the tap, covers the mouth, pulls the handbrake. Every act is a refusal. Meanwhile the café, the bingo and the kitchen (chapters 5, 7, 8) stage the same contest, and in all three the woman worth watching is Lesca. By chapter 8 the reader has gone over to her, as Coralie has. The only hand that closes over Coralie's fingers in this book is Lesca's.
Do: cut the kitchen fight in chapter 8 to the chart and the answer, since the bar already did the wrestling. Give Maylis one thing in the middle that she does and Lesca cannot. And let the right hand do one thing that is not stopping: on the doorstep in chapter 11, when she asks, she should touch her.

**3. Coralie says she would have the other one, and the couple never speak of it.**
Chapter 7, to Lesca, with Maylis in the wrist: 'I'd have the one who answers. I'd have you tomorrow.'
This is the wound of the triangle. Maylis hears it and the book gives her fingers closing on a cut. In chapter 16 Coralie names what is hers about room twelve and names what Maylis picked at the dip. Neither of them names this. The relationship that is meant to carry the book ends without its hardest sentence.
Do: one exchange in the last kitchen scene. Maylis says it. Coralie does not take it back.

**4. Lesca's hardness is reported and her charm is staged.**
Chapter 6: 'She had been given the reason in the chair. Lesca had put cameras on the gates and sent the film to the gendarmes, and Kévin had done six months in prison, for eels. After that she had given him the job. And the coat.'
The most interesting facts about her come as a download: she jailed a boy and then hired him, signed her mother's field into a lake, lost her deputy over a plastic bag. On the page she is the one with the timer and the small m. The reader's Lesca is therefore kinder than the file's. That is softening.
Do: stage one of them in her own village before chapter 10. Darrigrand's step ('It's four when you're the one counting') shows how. The plastic bag is the scene to write.

**5. The most important word in the book is not lit from inside.**
Chapter 11: 'The river went by under the boards. Her weight was on the handle. 'Either,' Maylis said.'
Three days earlier she said 'Open' twice to Larrère. An hour earlier she asked Coralie back. A reader can take this Either as the plot needing the gate shut. The real cause is in chapter 1: the duck costs Coralie an hour at the stove, so she will not want it. She cannot want a thing in the face of the person who pays for it, and here the man who pays is on the platform. The book has built that and does not use it.
Do: one beat of hers between his question and her answer. And in chapter 8 make it plain she says Open to a car window, not to Jeannot's face.

**6. The last two chapters give the reader a man to blame.**
Chapter 15: 'You knew he was out there, Guy. You stood in my kitchen that night and rang her from my phone.' Chapter 16: 'It says who had rung her, and from whose kitchen.'
In chapter 4 the book knew better. Lesca tries to blame Larrère and the sentence sticks: 'It gives me somebody to blame, and it won't come out.' Then both endings hand him part of the bill, through an affair that is the same coincidence in two worlds. In this village he is a window, a folder and a catchphrase, and his reason for keeping the gate shut is never his own.
Do: keep the garden gate and his silence. Cut the kitchen from Jeannot's letter. Give the Larrère of Maylis's village the one finished sentence the other one gets ('Eleven years I've been timed like an egg'), so that he is wrong for a reason.

**7. The stammer judges Lesca so the reader does not have to.**
Chapter 8: 'This time I'm coming to get—' It stuck there.' Then: 'To stay.'
The device is used nine times across chapters 4, 8, 12 and 16. Each time it sorts a motive into true or false. Then chapter 12 shows her driving for the man in earnest, which the device had ruled out.
Do: keep three. The first Either, the blame, and the last 'I won't.' Let her say 'to stay' unforced.

**8. The landing has two reasons that cannot both be true.**
Chapter 6: 'Do you know what I'd give for a woman at this table who sat down when she was told? I'd carry my pillow back across that landing tonight.' Chapter 10: 'I thought I could have you without it, if I kept a landing between us.'
If the landing is a call log at 2:51, sitting down for soup does not close it. The first line is there for the mirror and it undercuts the best scene in the book.
Do: in chapter 6 she offers something smaller than the pillow.

**9. Maïté is seen only from outside, as wife, widow and mistress.**
Chapter 4: ''That's winter things,' she said. 'In the— It's blankets.''
She is a black coat, a dressing gown, a handbag and broken sentences. In the other village she gets 'Left', the best single word in the book. Here she gets a suitcase, and when the bus goes a prop leaves.
Do: one finished sentence of what she wants, before the bus.

**10. Three people introduce themselves by handing over a rule.**
Chapter 2: ''That's the door,' Kévin said. 'Don't push at it. Lie still under it and you're in.'' Madame Etcheto and Arlette do the same within twenty pages.
They survive it because each is funny. Kévin's is the one that reads as instruction.
Do: let him get it slightly wrong.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Jeannot's voice, start to finish.** The numbered grievances, 'And six. Bugger off', and a last letter that 'doesn't thank you'.
2. **The landing in chapter 7.** Coralie finds Maylis by the pulse and says: 'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'
3. **The bedroom in chapter 10.** 'Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. 02:51.' and 'Because you'd have told me.'
4. **Madame Etcheto, from room twelve to the hatbox.** Bingo with the dead, 'That's my door', 'Twelve. One, two. Somebody pulled a chair in to a table', 'Forty-one', and 'Nobody emptied hers.'
5. **The catchphrases that break.** Coralie at the oven door getting as far as 'The—'. Larrère saying 'I'll be brief' and then nothing. The timer ringing on Lesca's own silence. Marie-Jo saying 'Somebody has to stack them.'
