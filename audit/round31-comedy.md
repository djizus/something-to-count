# Critic's report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** "The gate, or Castagnet? Say which, Maylis. For once." (13) Forty houses, forty-two beds, one man and one marriage hang on a single word, and the word is the book's running joke.
- **Tension: 8.** "She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it broke." (13) Chapters 11 to 13 are as tight as the comparison. Nearly every chapter ends on a hook ("Are you coming up?", "LESCA, MAYLIS.", "The arms of the chair were warm.").
- **Progression: 7.** "That village hadn't been spared its flood. It hadn't had it yet." (3) The turns are good. The stretches between them, the start of 6 and the start of 7, move by summary.
- **Comedy: 8.** "It's in the plan now. Staff to wait. Staff not to join in." (2) I smiled without a break from the mayor's window in 1 to the end of 5, and again through the back half of 6, the bingo in 7 and the vote in 8.
- **Characters: 8.** "It's four when you're the one counting. When it's your house it's one, and they knock it down." (10) A man with one scene, and he is a whole person and a whole argument.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 9.** "She stayed bent over for a moment with her hand on the door. Then she shut it and sat down at the kitchen table in her coat." (16) The same oven-door impression that was the first laugh in 1 fails to come out, and that is how the book tells you what Friday cost.
- **Prose: 7.** "Nobody at that table had heard him finish a sentence since 2015." (6) The deadpan is right for comedy. The narrator too often follows a joke with the sentence that explains it.
- **The ending: 8.** "He went and got it. Then he stepped over the string in his rubber boots and began to dig." (16) Letter 42, brought by hand because he will not pay to register it, is the running gag turned into the one decent act left.
- **Clarity: 6.** "Your river made up two days in an afternoon, so only the last night counts." (8) The rule the whole second half runs on is given in one line of dialogue. Several jokes need the mechanism held firmly, and it is not.

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. Would I press it on a friend? Yes.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Level with The Good Place. Above Crouch, who has no comedy to compare.**

What puts it level:

1. **Every running gag comes back as something else.** The Good Place's best trick is the joke that turns out to have been the plot. Here: "I'll be brief" ends as a man with nothing to say at a garden gate (15). "Still in the box, were you?" ends as "Had the key and went for a walk, did you?" (16). "Either" ends as "Both", and Coralie names it: "That was Either, with the river in it." (16). The kitchen timer rings on its owner. Jeannot's numbered complaints end as the only testimony in her favour. I count nine such gags and not one is dropped.
2. **Everyone is wrong in their own way.** Larrère talks through a gap. Jeannot numbers his grievances and registers them. Sarthou cannot use a contraction ("Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station."). Josiane reasons flawlessly from the wrong premise ("It's adultery whichever she's after, and it's in that house."). Marie-Jo speaks to the wall. Lesca can only decide by motion. That is an ensemble.
3. **The moral problem is funny and not small in the village scenes.** The committee cannot give the stone a verb because any honest verb says what she did: "Opened what? There's a name over it. It reads that she opened him." (3). The landowners vote on the river through a car window with the café door open (8). "It has enough seats when nobody's counting." (11). These are jokes that are also the argument.

What keeps it from going above:

1. **The two women are a double-act for one scene.** The Good Place makes ethics funny by having two people argue it. Here the two versions of Maylis argue once, in 4, and it is the best two-hander in the book. After that they fight with hands and jaws, which is good physical comedy, but the argument that matters ("both" against "no both") gets six lines in 8 and none of them is funny.
2. **Comic scenes sometimes stop at the first beat.** Larrère untimed after eleven years is a premise with three escalations in it. It gets one.
3. **The mayor's village is less good company than the roadmender's.** Its Coralie has one joke ("It's soup. It doesn't need a vote.") and then explains the dilemma in a paragraph.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The central argument is not played. (8, and so 12 and 16.)**
"'There's no both. You walked it with me, and you did the sum twice on the way back. You turn the handle first.' 'Both,' Maylis said."
This is the trolley problem of the book and the one exchange that decides who dies. It goes by in six lines between an explanation and a vision. Chapter 4 showed what these two can do with an empty chair and a vote of two ("Then it's carried, or it's a tie, and in a tie the chair decides." "There's nobody in the chair."). They never do it again. The Sunday meeting earlier in 8 is a paragraph of mechanism and the Wednesday one is a negotiation.
*Do:* make the Wednesday meeting the second sitting of the meeting in 4. Lesca tables "the handle first" as an item. Maylis has by now sat in Lesca's council chair and pinned her timer hand to the table, so she knows the rules and can use them: she asks for her three minutes, she moves "both" as an amendment, Lesca rules it out of order on the arithmetic. Use the room's own rule. It stops a lie, and it lets "There's no both" and "Both" through, one after the other, because each woman believes hers. That is the terrible joke the scene is missing, and it would make the catastrophe in 13 the payoff of a comic scene, as the best reversals in the comparison are.

**2. Larrère's eleven minutes stop where they should build. (6.)**
"'I have sentences in that register with no ends on them. The secretary will find one under the cameras on the gates. "A man can no longer." It rang there.'"
A man cut off mid-sentence for eleven years finally gets to finish them. We hear one ending ("I'm told I wave."), then the chapter cuts to Coralie at the bedside, then comes back at "eleven minutes" for the question. The cutaway sits in the middle of the build and the build never happens.
*Do:* give him three endings from the register, each less petty than the last, so the third unfinished sentence is the one about the platform, and the bell that cuts it off is the same joke with the laugh taken out. Move the bedside cutaway to before he stands or after the bell.

**3. The farce is started by an action the heroine does not choose. (4.)**
"She had gone back in and paid for them before she knew what she was doing."
Carrying funeral chrysanthemums past the café window to the wife of a living man is the best piece of comic staging in the book, and everything Josiane and Larrère do afterwards comes from it. But Maylis does it in a trance, and finds out what she has "come to say" only on the doorstep. Farce needs a person doing the wrong thing for a reason that seems good to her.
*Do:* let it come from her flaw. She wants to take the wife something, since she cannot warn the husband with a dream. The only pots at the door are the ones left from All Saints. Josiane asks which, and Maylis says the word she always says. Then the pot is hers, the mistake is in character, and the village's misreading is earned.

**4. Coralie explains the dilemma. (10.)**
"'So for twelve years I've had two answers. Either you opened it for forty houses, and I live with the mayor. Or you opened it because I was in that building.'"
The scene has already landed on "'Why not?' 'Because you'd have told me.'", which is the best line this Coralie has. The paragraph that follows lays the moral problem out as a tidy pair of options, in the same grave cadence as Lesca's replies, for sixty lines with no relief. This is where the people stopped being company and became a case.
*Do:* cut the "two answers" paragraph to "I wanted you in the house, and I thought I could have you without it, if I kept a landing between us." Keep the call log read like a chart. Give this Coralie one dry line in the middle of it, in the register of "It's soup. It doesn't need a vote."

**5. "You're the water" is misheard by the hall before the reader is sure what it means. (7, 8.)**
"She had nobody to say it to and no mouth to say it with."
The bingo joke depends on the reader holding the true meaning while the hall takes three wrong ones ("She wants water." "She's got a bar full of coffee."). But Maylis's discovery, that the river stood still on the two nights she kept Lesca out, happens at the marker post in three oblique sentences. A reader can miss it and be told next morning by a chart.
*Do:* one plain sentence at the post. Two nights she kept her out, two dry days. One night in, and the river is back on the page of Lesca's file. Then the hall's mishearing is funny from the first line and the chart in 8 can be shorter.

**6. "Ei—" in the square blurs the rule the last line of the book depends on. (5.)**
"'Ei—' Lesca said. 'You have three minutes.'"
Chapter 4 taught that an untrue word sticks in the steel room. Chapter 8 confirms that it sticks only there ("'It came out in my kitchen.' 'This isn't your kitchen.'"). Here a word sticks in front of the town hall. If words can stick anywhere, "'I won't.' It came out whole. They both heard it." (16) is worth less.
*Do:* keep the pivot to "You have three minutes", which is very funny, but have Lesca refuse the word, not fail at it. She has it ready and will not spend it on that man.

**7. The first sixty lines of 6 are three threats in a row. (6.)**
"'Take the complaint back, and I won't ring it.'"
Larrère blackmails Maylis, Sarthou files her, Maylis blackmails Jeannot. Each is a transaction at summary speed, and it is the first place in the front half where I stopped smiling for more than a page. Sarthou's two lines are the only light.
*Do:* keep the blank sheet in the folder, which is good menace, and Jeannot's "That's how Larrère asks for a thing." Play her threat as a woman who is bad at it: she has never asked for anything, and it should show in how she asks for this.

**8. The narrator explains jokes that have landed.**
- 6: "Nobody at that table had heard him finish a sentence since 2015."
- 15: "She had rung it on the ends of Larrère's sentences for eleven years. Now it had rung on hers before she began one."
- 5: "Nobody in Peyrebarthe had ever heard Maylis's mouth forbid anything."
- 16: "It was the smallest thing that had happened to her since Friday."
- 7: "For one breath, Maylis had won." and "Maylis had lost."

*Do:* cut all six. In each case the line before did the work.

**9. A callback slows the worst minute of the night. (13.)**
"'I put that handle in number six.' 'I wrote to them too.' 'I know you did. They took note.'"
"They took note" is a good line. But it comes at 3:31, with the water rising, room twelve unreachable and "There was nobody to carry him" two lines earlier. Two people stand in a doorway and compare correspondence. It is the one place a joke loosens a dark moment.
*Do:* go from the key on his palm to "It's no use to anybody now. Have it back." to Hourcade. The handle and who was written to are in letter 42 in 16, which is where they belong.

**10. The book keeps its voice back for a page and a half. (0, 1.)**
"She was scared." (0) "She wanted to scream." (1)
Two cold openings in the thriller's register before anything says this is a comedy. The first sentence in the book's real voice is "They opened the door, counted one of her, paid her, gave her a sandwich and went back to their university." It is excellent and it goes by fast.
*Do:* leave the prologue. Let Coralie be herself in the first exchange at 2:54, so the reader meets her humour before meeting her patience. One line would do it.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Room twelve and the care plan (2).** Bixente joining in to be friendly, the old woman reporting that "it had been told twice and given two lots of stars, and it got through them early and came at 1", and "With cards?" This is the funniest scene in the book and it plants the death.
2. **Jeannot's six points down the phone (9).** From "Who's dead?" to "And six. Bugger off." to "A man had just sworn at her in six points, and he was alive." The cleanest switch from dark to comic I have read in a long time. It is also the moment the reader starts to love him.
3. **The oven door, three times (1, 8, 16).** "'In my tray,' she said, and shut the door on him." Then the soup that "will be consulted". Then "The—", and nothing.
4. **The stone's verb (3) and Maïté's answer (15).** "It reads that she opened him." Then twelve chapters later, "*Left*. He was left out there." and "It would have been right for both lines."
5. **The square after the sentence (14) and the last page (16).** The deputy turning the sheet over to see if there is more on the back. The man beyond the last loudspeaker who starts to clap. "Somebody has to stack them." And letter 42, four pages, which "doesn't thank you."

Also leave alone: "The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies" (12), "It was a short count" (12), "It takes a small m" (5), "I wanted six, Maylis." (16).
