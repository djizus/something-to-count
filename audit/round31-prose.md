# Round 31 — Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else. Counts below are mine, taken from the text; sentence counts are rough.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** 'I'd have had you. You answer. But what I'd have had is a river over forty-two beds.' (ch. 8)
- **Tension: 7.** "She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it broke." (ch. 13) Nothing between the stairs in ch. 5 and the handle in ch. 11 comes near it.
- **Progression: 6.** 'I've tried for three nights to keep you out, and I can't. So I'm asking. Don't come on Friday.' (ch. 8) is 'Then stop coming' (ch. 4) asked again four chapters later.
- **Comedy: 7.** 'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.' (ch. 6)
- **Characters: 8.** 'One. It's Jeannot. Two. Jean's for the stone.' (ch. 7)
- **Switch between dark and comic: 8.** 'It's Saturday.' / 'Then they'll want the bins out,' he said. (ch. 13, in a flooded corridor)
- **Prose: 7.** "Under the water the torch found the sole of a rubber boot." (ch. 9) is what it can do. "Maylis knew what that meant by now." (ch. 5) is what it does too often.
- **The ending: 7.** 'I know what you picked. I was the other one.' (ch. 16) It costs enough. It is reached through ch. 15, the thinnest chapter in the book.
- **Clarity: 6.** 'Your river made up two days in an afternoon, so only the last night counts.' (ch. 8) The climax runs on this rule. It is given once, in a sentence that has to be read twice.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. From ch. 9 to 13 I could not have stopped. In ch. 6 to 8 I would have put it down once.
**Press it on a friend?** On a friend who reads Claire Keegan or Magnus Mills, yes. On the Crouch reader the bar names, not yet.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, by one point: a 7 where the bar is 8.**

1. **Above Crouch at the sentence.** Crouch names the feeling and sets it on its own line. This book gives an object: two fingers on a wrist, a reflector screwed on at an angle, a car door shutting on a pillow. He has nothing as good as the boot sole and does not need it.
2. **Below Crouch at the page.** Crouch changes gear: long paragraphs while the trap is laid, one-line paragraphs when it shuts. This book has one gear. The mean sentence is 9.6 words, and no chapter leaves the range 8.7 to 11.5. There are 78 scenes averaging 540 words, and 49 of them end on a sentence of ten words or fewer. There are 528 one-sentence paragraphs. By ch. 11 the short line is spent, so it cannot mark the moment the book has been building to. Ch. 13 works because one scene runs 3,000 words without a cut.
3. **Below The Good Place on rules.** That show says each rule out loud, as a joke, then breaks it. This book makes the reader infer the rules (a false sentence will not come out in the steel room; a count holds the thing off for as long as there is something left to count; the river travels with Lesca) and then explains the feelings, which the scenes had already shown. It is the wrong way round.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. One tempo, cut like a logbook (whole book; worst in ch. 5 to 8).**
Ch. 5 opens its six scenes "It was 6:10 a.m.", "At 8", "At 9", "At noon", "At 7", "At 9:05". Ch. 6 has eleven scenes in 2,970 words, and three of them end on a man walking out: "He went out." / "He put the cap back on and went." / "Larrère put his hat on and went out after her."
Every scene is a time, an incident and a closing line. The reader learns the beat by ch. 4 and starts hearing it. The two places where a scene is allowed to run, the corridor in ch. 13 and the square in ch. 14 (mean sentence 11.5 words, the book's longest), are the best prose in it.
*Do:* take ch. 5 to 8 from 30 scenes to about 15. In ch. 6 keep the minute of silence, the file and the council, and play each at length. Drop the clock from scene openings unless the hour matters. End half the scenes mid-movement.

**2. The two-in-one-body scenes are written as a ledger of limbs (ch. 5, 7, 8, 12).**
"The right hand let go of the wheel and covered the eyes. Lesca kept the foot down, blind. She took the right wrist in the left hand and dragged it off the face. By then the front of the van had swung away from the track, towards the drop, so she let go of the wrist to catch the wheel." (ch. 12)
And in ch. 7, one walk: "Lesca had taken the watch", "Lesca waited", "Lesca kept to the grass", "Lesca didn't slow down", "Lesca walked her up", "Lesca didn't step off."
The two names come 27 times per thousand words in ch. 5, 25 in ch. 7 and 22 in ch. 12, against 6 or 7 in ch. 11 and 13. "The left hand" appears 28 times. The reader is doing bookkeeping at the book's hinge.
*Do:* fix one convention and hold it. In a Maylis chapter, Maylis is "she" and the body is hers; what Lesca does is told as what happens to her, as the book already does at its best: "Her legs swung out of the bed and carried her down to the kitchen. She hadn't told them to." One contested part per scene. In ch. 12 cut the eye-covering and the wrist; keep wheel against handbrake.

**3. Lesca's turn is argued, not done (ch. 12).**
"She had told Maylis there was no both. She had wanted both herself, the bed and the man. But the motion she had written at her own kitchen table was for a seat and a bed. There was no man in it." Then: "This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel."
The second woman's whole arc resolves in four paragraphs of the narrator reasoning on her behalf. The image that does the work is already there and is buried under them: "At the end of the headlights the reflector was red, and no nearer than it had been twelve years ago."
*Do:* go from that sentence straight to 'I'm letting go.' Cut the reasoning. The motion can come back in her mouth in ch. 16, where she says the same thing better: 'It was my own night I was putting right.' Do the same to the closing paragraphs of ch. 10, from "She had the sum" to Coralie's last line.

**4. The rules arrive by inference, and several lines have to be decoded (ch. 2, 4, 8, 15, 16).**
'Tell me it's Tuesday.' / 'It's—' (ch. 4) is the only statement of the rule that pays off in the book's last exchange: "It came out whole. They both heard it." A reader who took ch. 4 for a stammer loses that.
Others: "She said it had been told twice and given two lots of stars, and it got through them early and came at 1." (ch. 2; two lots should make it later.) 'That one's yours,' Maylis said. 'At 3 I never tried it.' (ch. 16.) "Down in the Port a rocket went up from the bank and lit him red, then white." (ch. 15; nobody has mentioned fireworks.) The file has the gate raised at 02:55 and the watch stopped at 03:11, while every character says four minutes; a book that trains its reader to do sums should make this one come out.
*Do:* one plain sentence in ch. 4 saying that nothing untrue would come out in that room. One in ch. 8 for the river. Rewrite the three lines.

**5. Chapter 15 is a list of outcomes.**
"She had rung it on the ends of Larrère's sentences for eleven years. Now it had rung on hers before she began one."
Four scenes in 1,160 words, each a single exchange and a closing line. The confession's consequences are minuted. It sits between two endings and lowers both.
*Do:* move Madame Etcheto's exchange into the square in ch. 14. Make the gutter scene the chapter and let it run; 'Left' is the best single word in the book and gets ten lines. Give the vote one paragraph that ends on 'Surname first.'

**6. Coralie speaks in finished paragraphs that say what the scene means (ch. 4, 5, 7, 10, 16).**
'I've held hands for a living since I was nineteen, so I know when a hand is holding on, and I know when it wants to be let go.' (ch. 4) 'I asked five things and I got five answers.' (ch. 5) 'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.' (ch. 7) 'I'm trained. I know what a coincidence is.' (ch. 10) 'I know that word. I've cooked it.' (ch. 16)
Each speech has the same build: the fact, a tally of years, a maxim from the job. She is funny in two lines at the oven door and becomes the book's analyst at 150 words. Both Coralies do it, so the two are one voice.
*Do:* halve every speech of hers over 80 words. Keep one trade maxim in the book, the one on the landing.

**7. Significance certified by tally (whole book).**
"Maylis had stood over that pad every morning for sixteen years and had never once got as far as a tick." (ch. 5) "Maylis had never smiled at Jeannot Castagnet in her life, and the whole café was watching her do it." (ch. 5) "Nobody in Peyrebarthe had ever heard Maylis's mouth forbid anything." (ch. 5)
"Eleven", "twelve", "sixteen" or "eighteen years" appears 39 times; "had never" or "never once" 23; "every night", "every December" and the like 25. There is a figure or number-word every thirty-odd words, forty per thousand in ch. 7. With everything measured, the three numbers that matter (ten minutes, twenty-five minutes, 2:51) are harder to hold. And when the narrator, Coralie, Lesca, Jeannot, Marie-Jo and Sarthou all count, counting stops being Maylis's.
*Do:* one tally per scene, and only where the number is the joke ('I've had sixteen and a shopping pad'). Cut the certificate wherever the action is already odd: the window going down needs no "Maylis hadn't known it could."

**8. A closing sentence that explains what was just shown.**
"The cold reached the bar and came over it. For one breath, Maylis had won." and ten lines on, "Inguma was gone. Maylis had lost." (ch. 7) "That made twice he had asked her about a kitchen. He hadn't asked once about a river." (ch. 6) "She had never had a time of her own." (ch. 9) "She had put Lesca out, and it had come too late: the river was through the bank already." (ch. 13) "It was the smallest thing that had happened to her since Friday." (ch. 16) And one stock line on page 2: "She wanted to scream."
*Do:* delete each. None carries information the paragraph has not given.

**9. Stock moves.**
The job simile, ten times: "She read it the way she read a job sheet" (ch. 6), "So she wrote it out the way she wrote out any job" (ch. 8), "She worked it out the way she worked out a job" (ch. 13); Coralie does a thing the way she does it at work four times (ch. 4, 8 twice, 10).
The reaction beat is always an object set down: "Arlette put the pan down." "Coralie put the pot down." "He put the glass down." "She put her spoon down." "The cards went down." Nine of these.
"Coralie stood in her work uniform with the coffee pot in her hand." (ch. 4) / "Coralie had stopped with the coffee pot in her hand." (ch. 5)
Also: ", with" as a sentence tail 149 times; "Then" opening a sentence 87 times; "looked at" 52; "still" 66; "with her back turned" 4.
*Do:* one job simile per character. Vary the reaction beat or drop it. Search the rest and cut by half.

**10. Usage.**
- British spelling and vocabulary throughout (metres, windscreen, torch, pavement, rubbish bin, mum) with American items mixed in: "truck" (ch. 10, 13), "dish towel" (seven times), "baby carriage" (ch. 16).
- "marker post" (ch. 7, 9, 11, 13) and "the pillar" with "her father's line" (ch. 3, 8, 14): one object or two?
- The village hall floor is "polished" (ch. 7, 8) and "waxed" (ch. 12, 13).
- "resident's chart" (ch. 7, 8) but "patient's chart" (ch. 10) and "a patient" (ch. 13).
- "the duck huts" (ch. 6) and "the hunters' huts" (ch. 11); everywhere else there is one hut.
- "she did not fit it to the gate" (prologue) is the only uncontracted negative in the narration.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. The reflector, ch. 9: "At the end of the track the reflector went on shining in her headlights for four more minutes. Then the water rose up the door and covered it, and the red light went out."
2. The square after the confession, ch. 14: "She watched her sentence travel back through the square and down the road, row by row, at walking pace." and "A child asked what the lady had done. Somebody told the child."
3. Room twelve to the end of ch. 13, from the torch on the pillow to "There was a hatbox on it. Nothing else." and 'Forty-one.'
4. The villagers' signatures: 'I'll be brief'; Jeannot's numbered points, up to 'And six. Bugger off.'; 'It reads that she opened him.'; "They opened the door, counted one of her, paid her, gave her a sandwich and went back to their university."
5. The punctuation: no semicolon, no ellipsis and no exclamation mark in 42,000 words, a dash only where speech breaks off, almost no adverbs. And the oven door failing in ch. 16: 'I'll be brief,' she said. 'The—'
