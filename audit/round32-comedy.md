# Critic's report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "'I'd have had you. You answer. But what I'd have had is a river over forty-two beds.'" (8) The stakes are people the reader has laughed with, which is why they hold.
- **Tension: 7.** "On the sixth ring the rib caught and held. She had no breath to push with." (11) High from 11 to 13; slack in the first third of 7 and in the chair scenes of 8.
- **Progression: 6.** "'Your river made up two days in an afternoon, so only the last night counts.'" (8) One line writes off the week the reader has just spent watching her push.
- **Comedy: 7.** "'Eleven years I've been timed like an egg,' he said. 'I have sentences in that register with no ends on them.'" (6) Five passages at 8 or above. The two leads face to face, and a farce that never collides, keep the book at 7.
- **Characters: 8.** "'There's a key, and a handle, and Larrère, and a van. And then there's me, as a distance.'" (8) Coralie is the funniest person in the book and the one with most to lose.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" (16) The third oven door, which she cannot finish.
- **Prose: 7.** "There were five of them, locked on the brake. It was a short count." (12) Against that: "the way she read a job sheet" (6), "the way she wrote out any job" (8), "the way she worked out a job" (13).
- **The ending: 8.** "'It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you.'" (16) It costs a death, a marriage and a job, and the last voice is still Jeannot's.
- **Clarity: 6.** "'The one it cannot take home dies in her own bed.'" (2) In 12 Lesca refuses to be taken, sits down among the dead, walks out and wakes on a hospital stretcher. The reader has to work out alone why she was let go.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. From 5 onward I was not going to stop.

**Would I press it on a friend?** Yes, on the friend who watched The Good Place twice. Not on the one who reads only Crouch: chapters 7 to 9 walk where he runs.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below The Good Place, narrowly. Above Crouch, who is not funny and does not try to be.**

Level with The Good Place:

- **The flaw is the joke, then the wound.** Chidi's indecision is a stomach ache and a carton of almond milk. Maylis's "Either" gets her eggs in chapter 1, loses her the gate in 11 ("'Either,' Maylis said. 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said.") and is named for what it did in 16: "'That was Either, with the river in it.'" The Good Place never let Chidi's flaw kill somebody the audience liked. This book does, and it kills the funniest old woman in it.
- **Everyone is wrong in their own way, and you could tell them apart with the names covered.** Larrère evades by procedure ("It isn't settled, and you're not to worry about it"), Jeannot by numbered grievance, Josiane by syllogism ("She lives with a nurse, and that's to throw us off"), Sarthou by the file ("Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station"), Lesca by the motion.
- **The moral problem is funny and not small.** A committee debating the grammar of a memorial: "'Opened what? There's a name over it. It reads that she opened him.'" (3) And Darrigrand with his broom: "'It's four when you're the one counting. When it's your house it's one, and they knock it down.'" (10)

Below it:

- **The central pair is not funny together.** The Good Place runs on Eleanor and Chidi, or Eleanor and Michael, in one room. Maylis and Lesca in one room is the least funny recurring scene in the book. Their comedy is all in the shared body: the spoon, the coffee tap, the one nose.
- **The comic reversal is thrown away.** The Good Place's turn makes every earlier joke mean something else. This book has one: the village's wrong theory about the Castagnet house was the right house. It is spent on a line at a café door: "'I only had the one name wrong.'" The farce and the fact never meet in public.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The two women stop being funny face to face after chapter 4. (8, 9, 12, 16)**
The first steel-room scene is the best premise scene in the book because it is a meeting: "'Then it's carried, or it's a tie, and in a tie the chair decides.' 'There's nobody in the chair.'" Every steel-room scene after it drops the meeting and becomes two identical voices exchanging rules: "'Now I've got both. Your river made up two days in an afternoon, so only the last night counts. If I'm in your village at 2:51 on Friday night, the water comes through your bank as it came through mine, and your bank goes. If I'm not, it holds.'" Then 16 pays off a gag the book stopped running: "She didn't call anything to order."
*Do:* Lesca chairs every one of them: an agenda, items, a vote. Maylis, who has never run a meeting, learns the form and beats her with it. "Both" is moved as an amendment and ruled out of order. The rule about the last night arrives as an item she tables and cannot get seconded. Then the silence in 16 lands, and the pair at the centre of the book is its comic engine and not only its plot.

**2. The farce never collides at home. (5, 7, 8, 16)**
Josiane's theory is set up perfectly in 5 and runs well through the bingo in 7. Then it stops making anybody do anything. The scene where the false theory meets the true fact is given to the other village's council ("'I've never had to leave my car in the back lane with its lights off.'", 6), where it is delicious and costs the main story nothing. At home Larrère only bats it away in private from his car (11), and the payoff is one line to nobody: "'I said that house. I only had the one name wrong.'"
*Do:* give it a public scene at home. The association vote in 8 is built for it: Larrère is parked at the open café door with Josiane behind the counter, "heating the whole square". Let her run her syllogism about that house within reach of the gap, and let the window go up on it. The vote scene gets funnier, Larrère's "shut" gets a second motive the café can smell, and the line in 16 becomes the reversal it should be.

**3. The middle says its sums twice. (6 to 9)**
Three nights of pushing, three chair meetings in a row (8, 8, 9), and the arithmetic restated until the reader can recite it as Coralie recites the speech: "Twenty-five minutes was a trailer of leaves to the dump and back. Ten was the time it took to hook the trailer up. Maylis did the sum at the water and again at the fence." The night of 2014 is told in 00, 3, 6, 9, 10 and 14. I stopped smiling in the first third of 7 and in both chair scenes of 8, and this is why: no villager is on the page and nothing is new.
*Do:* cut the Sunday-night chair scene in 8 and fold its one fact into Wednesday's. Reduce the file page in 6 to its one new line (trapdoor locked on the inside). Give the two pushed nights in 7 one paragraph. Keep the timed walk; it is the only version of the sum the reader watches.

**4. The stuck word is used seven times and is funny once. (4, 5, 8)**
"Standing up in this room, a woman can only say what's true" is the best comic rule in the book, and "'That's not a no.'" (8) shows what it can do: Lesca's official sentence is true, so it passes. But mostly it is a lie detector that stammers: "It's—", "Because", "Ei—", "'It's the ti—' Lesca said. She started again, and it stuck on the same word.", "'I w—'", "to get—".
*Do:* keep the three in 4 and the last in 8 ("'To stay,' she said. 'That's the one that comes out.'"). Cut "It's the ti—" (the kitchen has already shown that lie fail) and "I w—". Spend the rule on sentences that are true and evasive; that is where it bites.

**5. Payoffs are followed by a sentence that explains them. (4, 12, 13, 15)**
"She had written that word on the shopping pad on Tuesday, when she wanted the duck. It had always come when she needed it. It had never been true, and it wasn't true now." (4) "Twelve years ago she had let his phone ring once and then done what she had come to do. This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel." (12) "Lesca had let his phone ring once and then turned the handle. Maylis had let it ring nine times, and the man was alive." (13) "She had rung it on the ends of Larrère's sentences for eleven years. Now it had rung on hers before she began one." (15) The plastic bag is explained in 6, 14 and 15. Each time the reader had it already, and the gloss turns a callback into a reminder.
*Do:* cut all four. In 15 keep "It was louder from here" and nothing else.

**6. A joke slows the darkest scene. (13)**
Coralie has just read "Monsieur Hourcade. Carried." and there is nobody to carry him. Jeannot arrives with the key, and they stop for a callback: "'I put that handle in number six.' 'I wrote to them too.' 'I know you did. They took note.'" Forty people are sitting in water. Letter forty-two in 16 says the same thing better ("who had been written to about it").
*Do:* cut the three lines. He holds out the key, she hooks it on, she says Hourcade.

**7. The dark interrupts the best comic climb. (6)**
Larrère has found the timer held down and is finishing eleven years of sentences. The book cuts away: "In the other village it was raining. The clock radio said 18:40. The pad standing against it said *Back at 2*." When it cuts back the scene has to start again from cold.
*Do:* move the bedside lines to before the council sits, after the soup. Let the council run unbroken to the two raised hands, and let the bill arrive where it already does: "She was five hours late."

**8. Kévin explains the rules. (2)**
"'It's a thing you can learn, waking up inside your dreams. The phone goes off on the hour and you count. Five, and you're awake. In a dream the hand comes out wrong.'" Then: "'That's the door. Don't push at it. Lie still under it and you're in, and you've still got your head.'" It is the first flat patch, in the second chapter, and a boy up a tree happens to know how the plot works.
*Do:* three lines of the counting, and make him wrong or unsure about the rest. Madame Etcheto gives the real rule forty lines later and she is funnier.

**9. A promised form never arrives. (11 → 16)**
"'I'll post it today anyway, so you have it.'" The Hontaube call is the funniest new thing in the last third and it sets a clock: a loan-of-staff form, needing two signatures, due Monday. On Monday in 16 she is cutting turf one-armed and "the village has no outdoor worker this morning". The form is not there.
*Do:* it arrives. One line, at the cemetery gate or in the mayor's tray.

**10. One joke needs reading twice. (8)**
"'And I'm standing there with my purse out, thinking, twice. I'd have liked to hear the second one. I'd have missed the first, looking for my purse.'" Arlette is good company everywhere else. Here I had to go back to find what "twice" was.
*Do:* put "open" in her mouth: she'd have liked to hear her daughter say open the second time.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **"Either", from start to finish.** "'I don't cook Either. There's no recipe for it.'" (1), said to the demon in 2, to the gendarme in 6, on the platform in 11, and "'I know that word. I've cooked it.'" (16). The best thing in the book.
2. **Coralie at the oven door, three times.** Chapters 1, 8 and 16. The third stops at "The—". Do not add a fourth and do not finish the third.
3. **Bingo night, all of it. (7)** "'Tooth,' Marie-Jo said into the microphone." "'How's that spelled?' Bordenave said." The demon comes under the tablecloths while the hall plays for a duck, and the kind old woman finishes the prayer and loses Maylis the night.
4. **Jeannot's numbered points.** They run to "'And six. Bugger off.'" and "A man had just sworn at her in six points, and he was alive." (9), then to a letter that "doesn't thank you" (16).
5. **Larrère's sentence.** The gap in the window, the window going "all the way down, into the door" (5), "timed like an egg" (6), and at the garden gate: "'I'll be brief,' he said." Then: "He said nothing." (15) With it, Lesca among the dead: "'You can put that in the minutes as apologies received.'" (12)
