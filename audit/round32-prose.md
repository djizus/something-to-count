# Critic report: Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. About 42,600 words. Counts below are my own, taken across all seventeen files.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.5.** Lives, a marriage and whose hand does it, all in one sentence: "If she turned that handle, a man drowned and her hand had done it. If she went on, whatever the river did behind her was the river's doing."
- **Tension: 6.5.** Chapters 11 to 13 are an 8 ("Then the screen said 01:12. She hadn't seen it change."). Chapters 4 to 8 bank the date and spend the pages on set pieces, and the prose walks at the same pace in both.
- **Progression: 7.** The best turn of the first half is two sentences: "That village hadn't been spared its flood. It hadn't had it yet." Chapter 7 then loops (push, push, the rib gives) before it moves again.
- **Comedy: 7.5.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" The people are funny in themselves. The wit has one rhythm, shared by everybody (fault 7).
- **Characters: 7.5.** Coralie is the most alive person in the book: "'There's a key, and a handle, and Larrère, and a van. And then there's me, as a distance.'"
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** In the flooded corridor: "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'" The running joke goes into the dark and is not made to wink.
- **Prose: 7.** No filler vocabulary at all: no "suddenly", no "seemed", no "somehow", one "really", no semicolon in 42,000 words. Against that, one gait in every chapter, 114 trailing "with" clauses, and a narrator who explains what the scene has just shown: "She had never had a time of her own."
- **The ending: 7.5.** "'I know what you picked. I was the other one.'" It costs Maylis the woman, the job and her name in the café. It costs Lesca less than the rules promised (fault 5).
- **Clarity: 6.** The rule that makes the river rise is worded three ways, and the last piece of it is patched in by a line of dialogue: "Your river made up two days in an afternoon, so only the last night counts."

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. From the end of chapter 2 I was not going to stop, and from chapter 11 I could not.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who likes a village comedy with a hard centre, yes, today. On a friend who reads Crouch for speed, not yet: I would have to tell them to wait for chapter 11, and a book that needs that sentence is under the bar.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Against Crouch: above on the sentence, below on the gear.**

1. Above. A Crouch sentence carries plot and nothing else. These carry plot, voice and a joke in the same ten words: "They opened the door, counted one of her, paid her, gave her a sandwich and went back to their university." He has no line as good as that, and no minor character who talks like Jeannot or Sarthou.
2. Below. Crouch changes gear. When his chapter runs, the paragraphs go to one line, the sentences break, the white space does the breathing. This book has one gait. The mean sentence is 9 to 10 words in every chapter: 9.9 in the council farce of chapter 6, 9.6 in the flood of chapter 13. About three sentences in ten are five words or fewer everywhere, and almost none is over 25. The night of the flood is read at the pace of the vote on the stone.
3. Below. Crouch explains his physics in lumps and leaves feeling alone. This book does the reverse and it is the worse trade: the mechanism is rationed well, and then a sentence arrives to tell me what Maylis has understood from a scene I have just watched.

**Against The Good Place: level on the idea, below on the voices.**

1. Level. The show's best trick is the afterlife as administration. This book has it and owns it: a care plan that says "Staff to wait. Staff not to join in", a demon given clerical work, and a dead woman's seat refused with "You can put that in the minutes as apologies received."
2. Level. The switch (score 8). The show cuts from a joke to a wound inside one line and so does this: Coralie bends to the oven door for her Larrère impression, gets as far as "'I'll be brief,' she said ... 'The—'" and cannot finish it.
3. Below. On the show each of the four is wrong in a different sentence shape. Here the syntax differs by speaker (that is a strength, see section 4) but the punchline does not: everybody lands a short capper on the end of the speech, and by chapter 10 I could hear it coming.

**Overall for this lens: just under the bar.** The sentences are good enough to clear it. What holds the prose at 7 is not a word list. It is three habits that can be cut out: explaining, one pace, and one staging shape.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. Lesca's turn is argued by the narrator (chapter 12)

> "She had told Maylis there was no both. She had wanted both herself, the bed and the man. But the motion she had written at her own kitchen table was for a seat and a bed. There was no man in it."

> "Twelve years ago she had let his phone ring once and then done what she had come to do. This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel."

This is the moment the second woman gives up the book's central want, and it is the one place in a ring-by-ring physical chapter where nothing physical happens. For the length of one ring the van fight stops and the narrator makes the case for her: here is the parallel, here is what her own motion left out, here is the lesson. Then she says "I'm letting go." The reader has been handed the reading before the act. It is also the paragraph where the chapter's tension drops to nothing, between ring eight and the hall of the dead.

What I would do: cut the argument and keep what she sees, which is already on the page and is enough. "It was the wheel of a van with a dead engine and its brake on." "At the end of the headlights the reflector was red, and no nearer than it had been twelve years ago." Then the line. If the motion must be there, one sentence of fact: the motion said a seat and a bed. The steering-wheel parallel goes; chapter 16 says it better and in her mouth: "It was my own night I was putting right." This is a rewrite of some 150 words and it is the most valuable one in the book.

### 2. One gait for the farce and the flood (whole book; worst in chapter 13)

> "She got out into water that came over her boots. The gate was six hundred metres behind her. The red reflector was four hundred metres in front. The bank had six minutes left."

> "Thirteen people stood behind their chairs in the council room and waited for the mayor to sit. It was 6:30."

Subject, verb, place, full stop. Nearly a quarter of all sentences open with "She" or "The"; 113 open with "Then". The plain declarative is right for this narrator and I would not make her fancy. But pace in prose is change of pace, and there is none: the 400-step run to the hut, the walk through the Port and the forty chairs are all told in the measured tread of the leaf-raking. Chapter 13 tries fragments ("Torch up. Right arm across her side.") and they are the only fragments in the book, so they read as another hand.

What I would do: leave chapters 1 to 10 alone. In 11 (from 23:34), 12 and 13, let the run be one long sentence that does not stop until she does, and let the stillnesses (the three breaths at the handle, the corridor in the dark, room twelve) be the short ones. Join with commas and "and" what is continuous; break only where she stops. Cut "Then" as an opener in those three chapters (31 of them). This is restructuring sentences, not adding words.

### 3. The sentence that explains the beat (every chapter)

> "Maylis dug the graves in Peyrebarthe. She knew what a name on a stone meant." (1)

> "It had always come when she needed it. It had never been true, and it wasn't true now. She did want one of them, badly." (4)

> "She felt the breath stop in the chest and start again, and she knew why. Where Lesca came from, this man was a name cut in a stone." (5)

> "She had been in room twelve when the old woman told them the rest. She had made a joke of it then." (6)

> "She knew now why Inguma always came at 2:51, first to Lesca and then to her. She had never had a time of her own." (9)

> "Lesca had let his phone ring once and then turned the handle. Maylis had let it ring nine times, and the man was alive." (13)

> "She had rung it on the ends of Larrère's sentences for eleven years. Now it had rung on hers before she began one." (15)

In each case the scene has done the work one line earlier: the word sticking in her mouth, the eyes on the back of a living man's neck, the stopped call at 02:51, the bell. The gloss then says it again in the narrator's voice. I marked several in every chapter and did not find a chapter without one. Singly they are harmless. Together they make a narrator who does not trust the two things the book is best at, the object and the line of dialogue. The same habit names feelings after the image has delivered them: "She was scared" follows "felt the clay move under her palm" (prologue and 13); "She wanted to scream" follows the thing sitting down on her chest (1).

What I would do: one pass with one test. If the sentence tells me what a character knows, wants or has understood, and the previous five lines showed it, cut it. Keep the ones that carry a fact I could not have had ("Maylis got that laugh three or four times a year") and the ones that are jokes ("For sixteen years Maylis had sat down and said nothing, in the wrong kitchen").

### 4. Recaps of the plan at the head of scenes (chapters 6, 8, 9, 10, 11)

> "The vote had left the gate shut for Friday afternoon. If the rib let Lesca in on Friday night, the gate would have to come up in the dark, and that job would be Maylis's." (8)

> "Maylis stood at the counter. If she couldn't keep Lesca out on Friday, she wanted the river let onto the fields in daylight, by order. Jeannot stayed on his stool. He held the hunters' seat in the association. An open gate would fill his duck hut with river." (8)

> "She needed a second pair of hands. One woman couldn't raise the floodgate and also get Jeannot up out of his duck hut." (11)

> "But on Wednesday, in the steel room, she hadn't been able to finish that sentence. What had come out was that she was going over to stay" (10)

A different fault from 3, with a different cure. These are position reports: where the plot stands, who wants what, in case I have forgotten since the last chapter. I had not. The countdown is clear from chapter 3 and each of these paragraphs stands between me and a scene that would have told me the same thing by playing. The list on the back of the pad in chapter 8 is the plan; the paragraph in front of it is the plan again.

What I would do: delete them whole and start on the first line of action or speech. In chapter 8 the vote scene should open on "'I'm heating the whole square'" and let Jeannot's seat come out when Larrère says "Then it's up to the hunters."

### 5. The rules of crossing are stated, then restated differently (chapters 4, 8, 12)

> "Since the night of the 23rd. That was my first night on my back. It was her first night in, and it started raining." (8)

> "When she comes over into me she brings her river with her." (8)

> "If I'm in your village at 2:51 on Friday night, the water comes through your bank as it came through mine" (8)

> "She had held the wheel against it for a whole ring, and this was where that put her." (12)

Three wordings of what brings the river: her being in the chair, her being in the body, her being in the village at one minute. They are not the same rule. On Wednesday 2 December Lesca is not in the other village at all (Maylis is driving her), and "In the other village it was raining." The dry Thursday and Friday then get explained away in one line so that the date can stand. And the price of resisting the creature, which the old woman set in chapter 2 ("The one it cannot take home dies in her own bed"), is waived in chapter 12: Lesca resists, is put in the hall of the dead, declines the seat and walks. A reader who has been taught the rules as carefully as chapter 4 teaches them ("There are three points, and I'll say them once") notices when the fourth and fifth are improvised.

What I would do: pick one wording (the meeting in the chair at 2:51 is the one the events support) and use it every time. Give the hall a stated way out before she needs it, or make her leaving cost something visible on the page in chapter 14 besides the "Twelve. One, two." that falls on somebody else.

### 6. Two women in one body: the name does the work of the pronoun (chapters 5, 7, 12)

> "Lesca waited for the second hand to reach the top, then walked her down off the bank and onto the track. ... Lesca kept to the grass between them. ... Lesca didn't slow down for it ... Lesca walked through it ... Lesca walked her up onto it ... Lesca didn't step off." (7)

> "By then Maylis's left hand had served eleven coffees and her mouth hadn't said another word." (7)

The convention is the cleverest thing in the prose: the driven parts take "the" (the mouth, the legs, the left hand) and the part she keeps takes "her". Where it holds it is funny and exact. But "she" is unusable in these scenes, so "Lesca" opens 20 sentences in chapter 5, 15 in chapter 7 and 11 in chapter 12, and the name is the subject of a verb 114 times across the book. And the convention slips: "Maylis's left hand" and "her mouth" in the line above are Lesca's to drive at that moment; in chapter 5 it is "her legs" and "her own face" while they are being driven.

What I would do: make the rule absolute, and then use it to rest the name. Let the driven parts be the subjects: the legs went down off the bank, the left hand held the watch out of the rain. That halves the "Lesca" count and is stranger and better.

### 7. Everybody caps (dialogue, whole book; densest in chapters 8, 10, 16)

> "'It's soup. It doesn't need a vote.'" (Coralie, 3)

> "'A tide comes in twice a day. It doesn't take Thursday off.'" (Coralie, 8)

> "'It's four when you're the one counting. When it's your house it's one, and they knock it down.'" (Darrigrand, 10)

> "'The emergency services can stand. Mine can't.'" (Coralie, 10)

> "'I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it.'" (Coralie, 16)

Each is a good line. The fault is that nearly every speech of weight ends the same way, on a short flat sentence that turns the one before it, whoever is speaking. The speakers are distinct in their syntax and identical in their timing. In chapter 16 three of Coralie's speeches in a row close on a capper, and the best line in the book has to follow two of them.

What I would do: take the capper off one speech in three and let the speech end where the thought ends. In chapter 16, thin the two before "I was the other one" so that it lands alone.

### 8. One staging shape: a person in a doorway, in a garment, with an object (whole book)

> "Arlette stood in the office doorway in her coat, with her bag on her arm." (10)

> "Coralie stopped at the bedroom door in her uniform, with her keys in her hand." (8)

> "Coralie stood in her work uniform with the coffee pot in her hand." (4)

> "Coralie was standing in the bedroom doorway with the speech in her hand" (10)

There are 114 clauses of the form ", with [a body part or a prop] [somewhere]", one in every forty sentences, and they bunch at entrances. Coralie is "in her uniform" seven times. Before a line of dialogue the default stage direction is an inventory of three things looked at: "He looked at the string, at the cut grass, at the way she held the shovel"; "She looked at Maylis's side, at the arm held across it, at the water running out of her raincoat." "Looked at" appears 54 times. Speech is given "with her back turned" three times and "to the wall" six.

What I would do: keep the prop when it is the scene (the potato and the knife, the pillow, the ham under the arm). Where it is furniture, cut the clause. Keep "to the wall" for Marie-Jo, who owns it and whose last use of it pays ("She said it to Lesca this time, not to the wall").

### 9. Three phrases the book leans on

> "She read it the way she read a job sheet" (6); "she wrote it out the way she wrote out any job" (8); "She worked it out the way she worked out a job" (13)

> "the way she counted a pulse against the clock" (8); "the way she read from a patient's chart" (10); "in the voice she used for reading a dose off a chart" (4); "as she did for a resident's chart" (8); "for as long as she would have given a pulse" (7)

About twenty-five comparisons are built "the way she [does her job]". It is the book's only simile and by the third use for each woman it is a reflex: Maylis is like a job, Coralie is like a chart. Two more: "never once" seven times, in three different mouths and the narrator's; and the involuntary action, "before she knew what she was doing", "before she had decided anything", "before she had a reason to give for it", "before she could stop the mouth", five times for Maylis. The last is thematically right for a woman who will not choose, and that is why it must not be spent: by chapter 13 I have read it so often that her hand opening on the wheelchair gets no charge from it.

What I would do: one job simile per woman per act, and none in chapter 13. "Never once" for Coralie only. The involuntary action twice in the whole book, the second time at the handle.

### 10. Slips and usage

- **A dropped beat (11).** Two speeches by Maylis in a row with nothing between: "'Nobody. She was by herself.'" then "'How many seats has your van got?' Maylis said." It reads as a missing line of Kévin's.
- **Continued speech (10).** Coralie's long speech closes its quotation mark after "what had been done.'" and the next paragraph, still hers, opens a new one. Chapter 4 does it correctly (no closing mark after "I'll say them once."). As it stands, the reader gives "So for twelve years I've had two answers" to Lesca for a moment.
- **Clock readings (13).** The book's rule is that a reading with a leading zero is read off a screen ("The screen said 02:55"). "03:03." and "03:06." are given while her phone is on the seat of the stuck van; she picks it up after. Either give her the phone or put those two in the narrator's form.
- **"a.m."** appears three times ("2:51 a.m.", "6:10 a.m.", "4 a.m."). Everywhere else the hour is bare ("At 5", "at 9"). Pick one.
- **American words in British prose.** The spelling and idiom are British (metre, grey, colour, programme, towards, torch, windscreen, pavement, rubbish bin, "My mum", "Bugger off"). Against that: "dish towel" seven times, "truck" six, "baby carriage" twice, "the dump" twice. Tea towel, lorry, pram, the tip.
- **Coralie's job.** Josiane says "She lives with a nurse"; Coralie says "I'm a care assistant"; she "trained" under "a night nurse" and has applied for a hospital floor "to run". If Josiane's word is a villager's imprecision, fine; if not, settle it, because the last chapter turns on what the Bayonne job is.
- **"the kind you turn by hand", "the way you carry a child" (13).** The only two uses of "you" in the narration.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The night that comes back word for word.** Prologue, chapter 9, chapters 11 and 13: "The headlights lit up the rain." "Low on its left-hand door the round reflector, screwed on at an angle, shone red." "the clay move under her palm". The exact repetition is the plot. A reviser who varies it for elegance will break the book.
2. **How each person talks.** Jeannot's numbered points, down to "And six. Bugger off." Sarthou's uncontracted officialese ("I shall require it"; "I do not record that I would have stayed"). Maïté's sentences that break before the noun. Larrère's "I'll be brief" and what becomes of it: at the garden gate he says it and then "He said nothing", and at the oven door Coralie cannot get past "The—".
3. **Feeling told as a measurement, and Coralie refusing it.** "It was fourteen metres to the back of the van. She counted them." "'And then there's me, as a distance.'" "'Don't give me the measurement.'" "It was closer to a kilometre and two hundred metres. She didn't correct her." This is the book's own way of writing emotion and nobody else has it.
4. **The corridor and room twelve (13).** "'It's Saturday.' / 'Then they'll want the bins out,' he said." The pulse held "for the full half minute". The line through the last name. "There was a hatbox on it. Nothing else." "'Forty-one,' Coralie said." Not a word.
5. **The running jokes at the moment they turn.** "Either" said with her weight on the handle, and Jeannot's "'Then it's shut.'" The café's greeting changed from "Still in the box, were you?" to "'Had the key and went for a walk, did you?' Then he looked up." "'I know what you picked. I was the other one.'"
