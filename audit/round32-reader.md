# Round 32: Reader

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

Verdict against the bar: just under it, at 7.5. From chapter 9 to the end it is level with Crouch and costs more than he does. Before chapter 9 an ordinary reader works harder than Crouch or The Good Place ever ask.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** "The trapdoor was twenty-five minutes away on foot. The bank had ten." One sum, and the whole book hangs from it.
- **Tension: 7.** Chapters 11 to 13 are a 9. Chapter 6 stops the clock for a whole day: "Nothing came at 2:05. Nothing came at 2:30."
- **Progression: 7.** "Your river made up two days in an afternoon, so only the last night counts." One sentence cancels the two nights of pushing I had just read through in chapter 7.
- **Comedy: 8.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station."
- **Characters: 8.** "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them."
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** In the flooded corridor: "'Here,' he said. 'I know you are.'"
- **Prose: 7.** It never gets in the way, but in the shared-body scenes I had to count hands: "Lesca's wrist went out from under the left hand. Maylis couldn't have held it with two. The right hand took the dial, wound it right around and back to nothing, and the bell rang."
- **The ending: 8.** "She heard the passage, then the front door, then the car door shutting on a pillow."
- **Clarity: 6.** On the fourth page I am expected to follow "On Monday you put the gate up with the river low, and my water ran out through it" before anyone has said that the same gate empties the fields at low river and fills them at high.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. I put it down once, at the end of chapter 6, and picked it up the next day. From chapter 9 I read to the end without getting up.

**Would I press it on a friend?** Yes, on a friend who reads novels. Not on the friend who reads only Crouch: that one stops in chapter 3.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Level from chapter 9, below before it.**

1. **Below, on rules.** After three chapters of Dark Matter I could explain the box to a stranger. The Good Place tells each rule as a joke at the moment it matters, and nobody rewinds. Here I count about twelve rules (the steel room, the words, the counting, who sits, who stands, the right hand, the truth when standing, what crosses in the chair, who brings the river, the last night, the hall, the empty seat). I went back four times: to sort Maïté from Marie-Jo in chapter 3, to follow the tied vote in chapter 6, to work out why "Maylis had won" turns into "Maylis had lost" in chapter 7, and to check "only the last night counts" in chapter 8.
2. **Above, on the marriage.** Crouch makes the reader afraid by putting the wrong Jason in the house with Daniela. This book puts the wrong woman in the right body at the right table, and it is worse: Coralie at the sink, "I'd have this one again", and under the dish towel Maylis closing her fingers on the cut. Nobody in Crouch is as good company as Coralie, Jeannot or Kévin.
3. **Level, on the reversals.** "You're the water" re-reads nineteen days of rain. "2:51. I've known that for twelve years" re-reads the landing. "The screen said Castagnet, house" re-reads the order to keep the gate shut. Those are the size of The Good Place's turns. The moral problem stays funny and stays large: "That was Either, with the river in it."

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Chapter 6. The day as mayor is an errand the book's own rules make pointless, and it is where I stopped reading.**
"Maylis wanted to read the page for that night with her own eyes: the hour, the place where the river came through, how much time there had been, and what was found at the hut."
Chapter 4 has told me that in the chair "what one of us knows, the other gets", and in this same chapter she is "given the reason in the chair" for Kévin's cap. Three chapters later "the night of the flood came to Maylis anyway", whole, for nothing. What the file gives her is three typed lines. Meanwhile the river, the man and the bed are all in the other village, and the chapter runs eleven sections with nothing in them to be afraid of except six lines of Coralie at the bedside.
What I would do: make the second reason the only one. She goes because she wants "one day as the woman who signs things". Cut the file to the one fact she needs (the trapdoor locked on the inside). Cut the wait on the office floor to the tractor joke. Go back to Coralie and the open-eyed body three times, not once, so that each laugh in the council room has that bed under it.

**2. Chapter 11. At the handle, in daylight, with the man standing behind her, the page goes blank.**
"Her weight was on the handle. 'Either,' Maylis said."
This is the choice the book turns on, and I did not believe it when it came. An hour before, she asked Coralie back across the landing. On Tuesday she said "Open" twice to Larrère's face. At 5:10 that morning she moved the trapdoor to the top of her list, "before dark", and now it is before dark and he is out of his hut. She has already defied the car on the bank by fitting the handle. Then she not only says the word, she unships the handle herself while his van is still on the bank beside her: "She lifted the handle off." The reason arrives five chapters late: "I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me."
What I would do: keep the failure and keep the word. Put that reason in this scene, in her head, in two or three plain sentences before she speaks. And do not let her take the handle off. Leave it on with one tooth caught, as she leaves it at 11. Then Madame Darrigrand's "You had the key on your belt then" lands on a reader who agrees with her, not one still arguing with the plot.

**3. Chapter 8. The rule the last night depends on is announced, not found.**
"Your river made up two days in an afternoon, so only the last night counts. If I'm in your village at 2:51 on Friday night, the water comes through your bank as it came through mine, and your bank goes. If I'm not, it holds."
Everything in chapters 11 to 13 rests on this. I could not have worked it out, and I cannot check it. It also cancels the two nights Maylis won in chapter 7. The same looseness is in the rule about what crosses in the chair: she gets a stone, a sash, an afternoon on a ladder and Kévin's prison, but not the one night that matters until chapter 9 needs it.
What I would do: let Coralie's chart do it. The dry Thursday, the cross on Bordenave's pad and the river catching up by Saturday dusk are already in her three columns. Let Coralie run the pencil down and say what follows. In the steel room Lesca then only has to fail to deny it.

**4. Chapter 15. The best pair in the book gets seven lines.**
"'Good night, Maylis,' she said, and went into the spare room."
In chapter 10 this Coralie says she has carried 2:51 for twelve years, will not be told which answer is true, and will be in the second row to see whether the line is read. Lesca does more than read it. Then the book gives the two of them one exchange on a landing. The chapter is four short scenes, each closing an account on a neat last line, and it is the one place after chapter 9 where I did not need the next page.
What I would do: give the landing a full scene. Coralie has had the first half of her answer in front of the whole square. Show what she does with it at her own kitchen table. Keep "Good night, Maylis" as its last line. Shorten the council to pay for it.

**5. Chapter 10. On the day of the flood, two pages of rehearsal.**
"'A point of order,' Lesca said. 'You have the flowers as one item and the kiss as the next. They're one item.'"
I skimmed from here to the moment Coralie takes the tape. The river is due that night in the other village and I am being told which side a photographer stands on.
What I would do: open the square on Coralie pressing down the strips. Keep Darrigrand's swept step and "The others have died since then, in those beds, of the things people die of." The kiss needs no notice: chapter 14 shows the cheek turned away, and that is enough.

**6. Chapters 12, 14 and 15. I do not know what "Twelve" means.**
"'Twelve. One, two.' Somebody pulled a chair in to a table."
Lesca refuses the empty seat and walks out. A number is called and someone sits. In chapter 15 the old woman says "Then it wasn't Inguma that came for her. It was you." Did Lesca's refusal put Madame Etcheto in that chair, or did the river simply drown her and the hall take note? If it is a trade, it is the worst thing Lesca does in the book and nobody says so. If it is not, the number is decoration.
What I would do: decide, and give Lesca one sentence in the square in chapter 15 that says which.

**7. Chapters 6 and 10. What Lesca's eleven years cost her village comes in half-sentences.**
"His mother had been Lesca's deputy mayor until a night in 2018, when she handed her sash back across the council table in a plastic bag." "Lesca had signed the gravel company's contract at this desk, and the village field was a lake now."
The book's argument is that the woman who decides is not the better one. The evidence is a jailed boy, a mother who will not enter the town hall, four houses and a lake, and each reaches me as an aside. In chapter 3, where I first meet her, I see only the stone, the roof she mends and the timer. So for half the book I took her for the one to envy, as Maylis does, and nothing on the page argued.
What I would do: put Kévin and his cap in chapter 3, on her walk across the frosted square. One of the costs seen at first meeting does the work of the four told later.

**8. Chapters 1 and 6. The order that starts the book is never explained.**
"On Monday afternoon the president of the association had rung and told her to raise it."
That order drains Jeannot's water, cracks her rib, puts her on her back and lets the other one in. Why Larrère gave it, with the river low, I never learn. Chapter 6 raises it out loud ("Was the order the mayor's, or the president's?") and then drops it when Jeannot withdraws his complaint. Tied to this: chapter 1 never says in one plain sentence that the gate works both ways.
What I would do: one sentence in chapter 1 on the gate. One line, from Jeannot or from Maïté, on what the Monday order was for.

**9. Chapter 8. The same machinery comes round again.**
"At 2:51 on Wednesday the 9th she had no push to give it at all. She was in the chair."
That is the second meeting under the lamps in one chapter, and the fourth of five in the book. Both in this chapter end with Inguma coming in over the step. Add four votes and three scenes with the timer. By here I could see the pattern: cross, argue standing, the door opens.
What I would do: one meeting in chapter 8, the Wednesday one. The Sunday one exists to deliver the rule in fault 3, and the chart can carry that.

**10. Chapter 3 onward. Names that look alike.**
"Marie-Jo Labat turned a white kitchen timer to three" and, eight lines on, "Maïté Castagnet, Jean's widow, held her handbag in front of her".
Two older women with M names meet me at the same table in the same scene, alongside Maylis. Later Darrigrand (the swept step, the baby) and Daguerre (the baker who says "Here") sit a few pages apart, and Lesca, Larrère, Labat and Lassalle share a letter.
What I would do: rename one of each pair.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 9, all of it.** The reflector that "went on shining in her headlights for four more minutes", then the nine rings and the six points ending "Bugger off."
2. **Coralie and the oven door.** Chapter 1, chapter 8, and the third time in chapter 16, when she gets as far as "The—" and shuts it.
3. **Coralie on the landing in chapter 7.** "Which of you is that?", the two fingers on the wrist, "That's her. In this hand."
4. **Kévin's fingers.** Counted in his own headlights at 4 in the flood, "Five", and then on the step of his van: "I wanted six, Maylis."
5. **The village's two lines.** The window that goes "all the way down, into the door", and the café finding its new greeting in chapter 16: "Had the key and went for a walk, did you?"
