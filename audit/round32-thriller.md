# Critic's report: Thriller

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. 42,600 words. Nothing else opened.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the care home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the fields there was one man, at his duck hut, and no one came to tell him." (3) Countable, and the reader can do the sum. The private stake, a woman losing her body and her bed to her other self, peaks in chapter 7 and is gone by chapter 8.
- **Tension: 6.5.** At its best a 9: "Beyond the last wheelchair the fire door stood open. The old wing behind it was dark. Coralie was somewhere behind that door." (13) At its worst the book flags its own hole: "There was still time to go down the track and knock. She didn't go." (11)
- **Progression: 7.** Every warning tightens the trap: "Two. I hadn't given the 11th a thought until you handed it to me." (5) Chapters 6 and 10 stop the clock to visit a village where nothing can happen.
- **Comedy: 8.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station." (6)
- **Characters: 8.** "I've held hands for a living since I was nineteen, so I know when a hand is holding on, and I know when it wants to be let go." (4)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies,' she said, in the voice she used for the prefecture. 'She has a ceremony at 11, and she will not be taking this seat.'" (12)
- **Prose: 7.** Fast and concrete: "The street lamps of the Port went out one after another, starting at the bank end." (13) One construction is worn out: "It was out before she had time to think about it" (1), "It had come out before she had a reason to give for it" (4), "It was out before she could stop the mouth" (6).
- **The ending: 8.** "She heard the passage, then the front door, then the car door shutting on a pillow." (16) It costs the marriage and gives nothing back.
- **Clarity: 6.** "Your river made up two days in an afternoon, so only the last night counts." (8) A rule that arrives on the page where the plot needs it.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. From the stairs in chapter 5 I would not have stopped; before that the prologue and the file in chapter 3 carried me.

**Pressed on a friend?** Yes, but not on the friend who reads Crouch for the pace. That friend gets it with the words "give it until chapter 3", and a thriller that needs those words is below the bar.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**As a thriller: below Crouch, a 7 against his 8. Chapters 11 to 13 are at his level. Two things in the book are above him.**

Below:

- **Rule economy.** Dark Matter has a box. Recursion has a chair. This book has a steel room, a thing that sits on sleepers, a counting charm that sets the length of a visit, a right hand the owner keeps, a room where lies will not come out, a hall of the dead, and weather that follows a person. Each is staged well. Together they mean the reader cannot work out, on the last night, what winning would look like. Crouch's reader always can.
- **Who is driving.** Jason Dessen wants one thing from page 30. Maylis wants nothing until chapter 6, by design, and the book covers that by handing the drive to Lesca. It works until chapter 8, when Coralie refuses Lesca and Lesca's prize is gone. From there to the end of chapter 10 neither woman is pushing: one plans to sit in a van, the other plans to turn up.
- **Dread.** The first page is a horror page. By chapter 6 the heroine is lying on an office floor to give the monster somewhere to sit, and it is five hours late. Funny, and paid for with the fear.

Level:

- **Speed of premise.** Two selves confirmed at 10% ("'Come back across,' her mouth said. Maylis hadn't said it."), the date of the flood at 16% ("That village hadn't been spared its flood. It hadn't had it yet."). That is Dark Matter's pace.
- **The reversal.** "You're the water" re-reads the first half as The Good Place's first-season turn does: the rain began the night Lesca first came, and the rescuer is the flood.

Above:

- **Fear for the marriage.** In Dark Matter the wife cannot tell the husbands apart, and the fear is that she won't. Here she can, by the pulse in one wrist, and she is tempted anyway: "I'd have the one who answers. I'd have you tomorrow." (7) That is worse, and better.
- **The cost.** Crouch gives the family back. This book runs the trolley problem twice, once with the lever pulled and once without, and both women lose the same wife. The Good Place played that problem for a splattered tram. Here it has a body in room twelve.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Chapter 11. The heroine spends the nine hours before the flood in a parked van, and the book does not say why.**
"There was still time to go down the track and knock. She didn't go."
She knows the minute, the place and the man. At 3:03 in chapter 13 she gets him out in one sentence: "She grabbed the collars of both his jackets in her left hand and pulled." Nothing stops her doing that at 1:12 and being back at the handle by 2. Her one plan is to stay awake, and Kévin kills it at dusk: "The thing that sits on you doesn't need you asleep." She hears him and sits down anyway. A thriller reader sees the author holding her in the seat so that the 3 a.m. dilemma can exist.
*Do:* make the waiting her choice and put it on the page. At 1:12 she starts down the track and turns back, for the phone on the dashboard, the dated letter and the name under it; or she stays because staying awake is the only plan in which she never has to choose. That is "She wanted it not to be her", two hours early, and it makes the vigil part of the fault the village names in 16. Cut Kévin's line in 11. His line in chapter 2 ("I've had that twice, wide awake") is plant enough, and without the reminder the roof bending in at 2:51 is a shock, not an appointment.

**2. Chapters 8 to 12. After chapter 8 Lesca has nothing to win, so the fight in the van is over a steering wheel.**
"I'd have had you. You answer. But what I'd have had is a river over forty-two beds." (8)
Coralie has refused her on the landing and pinned her hand to the kitchen table. Yet 8, 10 and 12 keep saying "I've come to stay", and 12 says she crossed "for that sound, and for a bed with two pillows". No such bed is on offer. The book's Dark Matter fear, the other one in your bed, is spent 30% before the climax.
*Do:* use what chapter 11 already built. At 4 p.m. Maylis asks and Coralie answers: "I finish at 7. I'll carry the pillow across myself. Be in the house." What one knows in the chair, the other gets. Let Lesca know it at 2:52: whoever holds this body at 7 gets the pillow Maylis asked for. One sentence, and the handbrake is being pulled for the marriage.

**3. Prologue, 3, 6, 8. The question the book asks three times was answered on page one.**
"It rang once." (00) Then: "Did you know he was out?" (3), "When you stood on that platform, did you—" (6), "That's not a no." (8).
The prologue shows her turn the headlights down the track, look, and ring a number. By chapter 3 the reader knows whose. The three cut-off questions are staged as cliffhangers and are waits: the reader is ahead of every character for nine chapters, and Coralie's "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. 02:51." (10) loses half its force.
*Do:* end the prologue four lines earlier, on "She looked down that track while the wipers crossed the windscreen twice." What she saw and what she did then become live questions, the phone arrives in 9, and the call log in 10 is a turn. If "It rang once" is too good to lose as an opening, keep it and cut two of the three teases; the timer in 6 is the one to keep.

**4. Chapters 4 and 8. The room where only truth comes out is an oracle, and nobody consults it.**
"Standing up in this room, a woman can only say what's true." (4) "Nothing had stopped a word of that." (8)
As worded, the room tests facts: "Because nobody has looked after that bank" will not come out. So any reader who knows Recursion asks why neither woman stands up and tries "the bank holds if the gate goes up at 5". And chapter 8 uses the room to certify a new rule about the river, which is the book certifying its own patch.
*Do:* make it a rule about the speaker. A woman cannot say there what she knows is not so. Every use in the book already fits (Tuesday, the tide, "to get—", "I won't"). Then "only the last night counts" is Lesca's belief, and the reader is allowed to doubt it, which is tension the last night could use.

**5. Chapter 6. The longest chapter of the middle is a trip the book's own rules make unnecessary.**
"Maylis wanted to read the page for that night with her own eyes".
Rule one of the chair: "In this chair, what one of us knows, the other gets." And three chapters later "the night of the flood came to Maylis anyway", free and in full. She risks thirteen hours and Coralie's place in the bed for three typed lines.
*Do:* cut the file as the motive and keep the other one, which is true and is hers: "She wanted one day as the woman who signed things." A woman who wants nothing going over because she wants something is a stronger chapter. Then cut 600 words of the untimed speech; the back lane, the hand on the dial and the two raised hands are what the plot needs.

**6. Chapters 12 and 13. The handbrake costs nothing the reader can see.**
"I know where the dip is. Let me drive." (12) "The front of the van went down and stayed down, and the back wheels dug in." (13)
Maylis throws Lesca out to save the bank, and it is too late. She loses three minutes, then does Lesca's errand herself and sinks the van in the dip Lesca could have driven. Chapter 16 knows this ("I drove past the handle to be the one who did"). Chapter 13 does not let the reader feel it when it happens.
*Do:* one line at the dip, as the wheels dig in: the five words she was left with, or the offer she refused. The climax then has a price paid on the page and not in a later conversation.

**7. Chapters 2, 12, 14. The one mortal rule is waived for a joke, and the trade that pays for it is too quiet.**
"The one it cannot take home dies in her own bed." (2) "'Twelve. One, two.' Somebody pulled a chair in to a table." (14)
Lesca holds the wheel against Inguma, lands in the hall, sits, and talks her way out with a memo. I would not cut the memo. But a Crouch reader expects the announced price to be collected. If the hall stops calling her name because room twelve has taken a chair, that is the blackest thing in the book and half its readers will miss it.
*Do:* say it. After the chair is pulled in, the voice does not call "Lesca, Maylis" again. Six words.

**8. Chapter 10. On the day the bank goes, 800 words of stage rehearsal.**
"'A point of order,' Lesca said. 'You have the flowers as one item and the kiss as the next. They're one item.'"
The chapter sits between the card with her name on it and the best run in the book, and its middle is tape, a clipboard and seating. This is where the Crouch reader puts the book down for the night.
*Do:* move the rehearsal to the Saturday of chapter 3 or into Maylis's day in 6, where the cross of tape and the eight survivors would also set up 14 sooner. Chapter 10 becomes Arlette and the call log, and runs straight into 11.

**9. Chapter 1. For 16% of the book there is nobody to be afraid for.**
"Above hers was a man's name, which she couldn't read."
The first page is fear. The 1,100 words in the square that follow plant nearly everything (the window, letter forty-one, the handle, Either), and the reader has no way to know it. Jeannot rolls his window down and is a comic turn.
*Do:* let her read the first name on the stone, and let the receipt card she signs carry the sender as the files have him. Then the man laughing in the white van is under a stone from page five, and the whole comic scene has a shadow over it.

**10. Chapters 7, 9, 15. Three chapters end on a settling, not a turn.**
"Maylis heard the pillow go down on the single bed." (7) "She crossed out the line about the trapdoor and wrote it again at the top, above the key, with *before dark* after it." (9)
Eleven of the sixteen endings make the next page compulsory. These three let the reader stop. In 7 the turn is twelve lines earlier ("What is he to you?"). In 9 it is one paragraph earlier.
*Do:* end 9 on "A man had just sworn at her in six points, and he was alive." End 7 on the question and the shut mouth. Chapter 15 is short enough to carry.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **"Either" at the handle (11).** "'Either,' Maylis said. 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said." The running joke becomes the fatal word, in daylight, with the man standing on the bank. And its bill in 16: "That was Either, with the river in it."
2. **The care home, from the gatepost to "Forty-one" (13).** Maylis with a wheelchair in her hand and Coralie behind the fire door. "'Here,' he said. 'I know you are.'" Kévin counting five fingers in his own headlights. The empty chair.
3. **The wrist (5 and 7).** The post at the bottom of the stairs, and Coralie telling the two apart by a pulse: "That's her. In this hand." This is the book's own invention and its best fear.
4. **The cross on Bordenave's pad (7 and 8).** Maylis keeps Lesca out for two nights, the forecast for the 4th comes out wrong, and that cross is why Jeannot votes shut. Also Josiane stopping the flood at "One man" (5). The warning is drowned by a farce, and that is why nobody listens.
5. **The confession and what follows it (14).** The sentence going back through the square "row by row, at walking pace", the man who claps, and "Somebody has to stack them."

Also leave alone: the reflector that "went on shining in her headlights for four more minutes" (9), and "'I won't.' It came out whole." (16)
