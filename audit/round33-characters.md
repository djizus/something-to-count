# Round 33 — Characters

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "That village hadn't been spared its flood. It hadn't had it yet." Forty houses, forty-two beds, one man and a marriage are all concrete. But one of the two leads says she stakes nothing ("I've written it down. Nil."), and the reader believes her.
- **Tension: 7.** "She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it broke." Chapters 11 to 13 hold. Chapters 7 and 8 are the same contest over a jaw, three times.
- **Progression: 6.** "I've tried for three nights to keep you out, and I can't. So I'm asking. Don't come on Friday." The steel room meets four times before the flood and the last two meetings restate the first two.
- **Comedy: 8.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station."
- **Characters: 7.5.** "That's her. In this hand. I've had sixteen years of her saying nothing, and I know it when I've got hold of it." The village is a 9, Coralie an 8, each lead a 7, and the two leads as a pair a 6.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'It's Saturday.' 'Then they'll want the bins out,' he said." Said in a flooded corridor, and the scene loses nothing.
- **Prose: 7.** "The mayor's vote had made the tie. Then it had broken it." The narrator explains a joke the scene had already made, and does it often.
- **The ending: 8.** "He went and got it. Then he stepped over the string in his rubber boots and began to dig."
- **Clarity: 6.** "She had held the wheel against it for a whole ring, and that ring had laid a place for her here. But she had let go before she was sitting in it." A rule arrives in the paragraph that needs it.

Would I have finished it unpaid: yes. Would I press it on a friend: yes, on the friend who reads for people. Not on the friend who wants Recursion.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Above Crouch. Short of The Good Place.**

- **Above Crouch on the double.** In Dark Matter the other Jason is a thief with one motive, and Daniela is fooled for a month. Here the thief writes her theft down as a council motion ("and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it"), has a widow's gutter to clear and a speech she can't finish, and the wife is not fooled for one minute: "Who is that?" Then the wife refuses her on a principle from her own trade: "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." Crouch has nothing like that sentence.
- **Level with The Good Place on people each wrong in their own way.** Maylis won't choose, Lesca chooses by sum, Coralie won't ask (one village) or won't wake a woman for orange (the other), Jeannot votes out of spite, Larrère orders by phone, Kévin wants six fingers. Nobody is wrong the same way twice. Neither woman is ever described from outside: neither has a face, hair or a figure, only a voice, a hand and a height.
- **Below The Good Place on the central pair.** Eleanor and Chidi change each other and the show gives them room to say so. Maylis and Lesca trade facts, rules and blame. Their last scene is twenty lines. What each envies in the other is never said to her face, in a room built so that it would have to be true.
- **Below Crouch on fear for the marriage.** Crouch shows the marriage happy from inside before he takes it. This one is met in its sixteenth year of complaint ("Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you, and that was me"), and by chapter 4 the wife has an envelope in her bag. The reader fears for Maylis, not for the marriage, and at the end thinks Coralie is right to go. That is more honest than Crouch and it grips less.

The relationship that carries the book is Maylis and Coralie. It pays twice: on the doorstep in chapter 11 ("Come back across the landing") and at "the car door shutting on a pillow". Maylis and Jeannot close the book and that pays too. Maylis and Lesca do not pay.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. Lesca's choice to cross on Friday is skipped. (Ch 10, 12)**
"This Coralie knew. She was four steps away with her door open, but Lesca still meant to go. She had told her it was for the man."
By then Lesca has said, standing, "If I'm in your village at 2:51 on Friday night, your bank goes, as mine did. If I'm not, it holds." Her own Coralie has just opened twelve years of silence and left her door open. Lesca has no cracked rib and her mother has told her how to keep the thing off. So crossing is the one deliberate wrong in the book: a river onto forty houses, for a bed. The narrator sums it up in three paragraphs and the chapter cuts. She is never seen to lie down. The book spares her the scene, and the reader can't tell self-deception from compulsion.
*Do:* cut the narrator's summary at the end of chapter 10. Write the night. She lies on her side with her knees up, as told. The light is on across the landing. Then she turns onto her back. No explanation. Let her Coralie hear the bed.

**2. The two women never become a relationship. (Ch 4, 8, 16)**
"'Don't come again.' 'I won't.' It came out whole."
Four meetings and each is an agenda. Maylis spends a day in Lesca's life and wants it ("'I want that,' Maylis said. It was out before she could stop the mouth"). Lesca has one bowl of soup in Maylis's and wants that. Neither says so to the other. The truth rule is used on plot ("It's the ti—") and almost never on the person.
*Do:* use the rule on them. In the Wednesday meeting, Maylis says she liked the square and can't say she didn't. Lesca tries to say she doesn't miss the yellow coat and it sticks. Give the last meeting one more exchange: what each would take from the other if she could. The book has the device and uses it for exposition.

**3. Maylis is a passenger in chapters 5 to 8.**
"Maylis had her right hand and nothing else." "She had nobody to say it to and no mouth to say it with."
For four chapters the lead's acts are: grab a handle, tip a spoon, hold a post, shut a tap, clench her teeth, cover her mouth. Each is a refusal. Her one day of agency is in the other village, among people the reader met a chapter ago. So at the dip the reader knows what she refuses and little of what she wants.
*Do:* one more un-ridden scene in her own village where she does something she wants and it costs. The model is already there and is her best beat before chapter 11: "Take the complaint back, and I won't ring it." Pay for it by cutting one of the three jaw contests (the Sunday kitchen and the bingo bar are the same scene).

**4. Jeannot is never told what he is to them. (Ch 7, 16)**
"It says why she thought she had to."
The man both women act on, and the one the reader likes most, learns offstage that he drowned in another village under this woman's hand. Coralie asks "What is he to you?" twice and gets an answer. He asks once ("What's left on me to look at?"), gets "You're breathing", and drops it. His line "Jean's for the stone" is funny because he doesn't know. The scene where he does know is the one the book owes, in numbered points, and it happens in a letter we don't read.
*Do:* play it. The cemetery will take it: three lines before he fetches the shovel.

**5. Coralie exists to examine Maylis until chapter 7. (Ch 1 to 7)**
"I want the geriatric floor at the hospital in Bayonne, on nights. I held my first hand on that floor, at nineteen, under a night nurse called Ithurbide."
This is the first time she wants anything that isn't an answer from Maylis, and it comes as a paragraph. Before it there is one envelope behind a fruit bowl. She is also nearly always right: she reads two people in a wrist, charts the river, refuses the bed. Her one error is a good nurse's error. Her temptation ("I'd have this one again") is the most alive thing about her and is dropped the moment the chart proves right.
*Do:* put Ithurbide's torch and list into the corridor in chapter 2, where she is already funny at work and under-ranked ("I'm a care assistant"). In chapter 16, let her tell Maylis she would have taken the other one. Maylis heard it; the reader wants it said to her.

**6. The death is the gentlest one available. (Ch 11, 13, 16)**
"'It's the eighth this year. I've kept count too.'"
"She wanted it not to be her" is the hardest sentence in the book. What follows cushions it. The one who dies is 94, refused the chair, was walked back by Coralie, was the subject of Maylis's text ("Not 12 tonight. Put her at the front"), and is the eighth grave of the year. Lesca's choice drowned a man of fifty-eight in a cellar and nobody shares it. Maylis's is split five ways.
*Do:* add no deaths. Remove cushions. Cut the priest's count. Have Maylis think the text and not send it, which is more her and costs more. Keep "Keep asking me things" by hanging it on another message.

**7. What twelve years of deciding made of Lesca is reported. (Ch 6, 10)**
"Lesca had put cameras on the gates and sent the film to the gendarmes, and Kévin had done six months in prison, for eels. After that she had given him the job. And the coat."
The prison, the lake, the ponies, the sash in a plastic bag and the four houses each come in a sentence. Before chapter 14 the only thing she decides on the page as mayor is a motion at her own kitchen table. Darrigrand shows how it should be done: "It's four when you're the one counting."
*Do:* in chapter 3, swap part of the verb debate for one live decision, made by sum in under a minute, that costs a named person in the room.

**8. Lesca's turn in the van is argued by the narrator. (Ch 12)**
"She had told Maylis there was no both. She had wanted both herself, the bed and the man. But the motion she had written at her own kitchen table was for a seat and a bed. There was no man in it."
The chapter is a fight of hands until its turn, and then the turn is a paragraph of reasoning. Her brush with death is a rule ("One more ring and she would have sat in it for good") where it should be a fright.
*Do:* let the right hand do it. She sees the reflector, sees the hand locked on the brake as it was on the stair post, and lets go. One sentence. Cut the rule about the ring; her fingers locked on the chair of the dead say it.

**9. Three villagers are met as manuals. (Ch 2, 3)**
"'That's the door,' Kévin said. 'Don't push at it. Lie still under it and you're in, and you've still got your head.'"
Kévin, Madame Etcheto and Arlette each first appear in order to brief a lead on how the thing works. Their voices save them, but it is three in a row.
*Do:* have Maylis lie still on her own first. Then Kévin is surprised by her, not teaching her.

**10. The people at the climax who aren't leads are names. (Ch 13; also ch 8, 10)**
"'Ask Nadine. I only lift them.'"
Nadine holds Coralie's list through the worst ten minutes of the book and is nobody. Bixente is a minibus. The two Arlettes speak alike and I had to check which yard I was in.
*Do:* give Nadine one line of her own in chapter 11's corridor. Give Lesca's Arlette one habit the other lacks.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie's two fingers on the wrist**, from "A hundred and twelve" to the full half minute in room twelve. It is how she loves, how she knows, and how the book tells a living woman from a dead one.
2. **"Either" on the platform.** "'Either,' Maylis said. 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. 'That's how the vote left it.'" The tic becomes the act. With it, "She wanted it not to be her."
3. **Jeannot at 5 in the morning** ("And six. Bugger off.") and letter 42, which "doesn't thank you".
4. **Lesca at the table of the dead.** "The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies… You can put that in the minutes as apologies received." Then her fingers, opened one at a time.
5. **Kévin's hand.** "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much."
