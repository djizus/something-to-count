# Critic's report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.5.** They are personal by chapter 10 and stay so: "Then his death is mine as well, and I've stood beside his widow for eleven Decembers."
- **Tension: 7.5.** Chapters 11 to 13 are at the comparison's level ("She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it broke."). Chapter 8 lets it go slack while the rules are settled.
- **Progression: 6.5.** Between chapters 4 and 9 the house scenes repeat one shape: "I'm taking the spare room" (7), "She went up to the spare room" (9).
- **Comedy: 7.5.** At its best it is as good as the comparison: "'Opened what? There's a name over it. It reads that she opened him.'" It does not hold that level through chapters 8, 10 and 15.
- **Characters: 7.5.** "'And six. Bugger off.'" Jeannot is alive on every page he is on. Coralie is funnier in chapter 1 than she is allowed to be again.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.5.** "'Either,' Maylis said. / 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. 'That's how the vote left it.'" The book's running joke kills someone, and the page does not blink.
- **Prose: 7.** Plain and exact, but it explains its own jokes: "The mayor's vote had made the tie. Then it had broken it."
- **The ending: 8.** "'It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you.'" It costs a death, a job and a marriage, and nobody is forgiven.
- **Clarity: 6.5.** The rules get patched in the middle of the climax: "that ring had laid a place for her here. But she had let go before she was sitting in it."

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. Would I press it on a friend? Yes, and I would tell the friend to push through chapter 8.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Against The Good Place: below, by half a point.**

- Level or above on one thing. The Good Place makes its ethical problem funny by turning it into a routine and then making the routine matter. This book does the same with "Either". It is a shopping-pad joke in chapter 1, a gendarme's joke in chapter 6 ("Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station."), and in chapter 11 it is said with a hand on the handle and it shuts the gate. Then "Both" is named for what it was: "That was Either, with the river in it." The problem is funny for ten chapters and never small.
- Level on reversal. "You're the water" turns the rescuer into the cause while a hall thinks she is thirsty. The kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice re-reads Maïté's "Did you know he was out?", the folder on her cupboard and the light she leaves on. Josiane is wrong in a way that turns out right: "I only had the one name wrong."
- Below on argument. The Good Place puts people in a room and lets them be wrong at each other, out loud, with wit. This book has the room: two versions of one woman, a rule that neither can lie standing up, and the dead playing bingo through the wall. It is funny there once, in chapter 4. After that the two women exchange verdicts.
- Below on the central pair being wrong in their own ways. The villagers are. Coralie is right in every scene until chapter 11, and a person who is always right stops being company.

**Against Crouch:** he is not funny, so the test is whether the comedy costs the thriller. In chapters 11 to 13 it costs nothing and buys a great deal: Monsieur Daguerre's "Here" and "Then they'll want the bins out" make the corridor worse, not lighter. In chapter 8 the comedy and the thriller both wait while the mechanism is explained.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Coralie ends six scenes the same way, and is never wrong (chapters 4 to 9).**
"Coralie picked up the bowl of coffee and poured it down the sink." (8) "'I asked where you'd be,' Coralie said. She went up to the spare room." (9)
Breakfast in 4, the pillow in 7, the landing in 7, the coffee in 8, the pad in 8, the bandage in 9: each ends with Coralie giving a verdict on Maylis and leaving the room. The woman who did Larrère through the oven door becomes an examiner, and I stopped looking forward to her. This is where I stopped smiling for longest.
Do: cut two of the six. Chapter 9 can end on "The hand came off her side." The pad scene in 8 can end on "I'm asking where I am in this." Then let her be wrong once before chapter 11. She has wanted the visitor since the soup ("I'd have this one again"), and neither she nor anyone else is ever allowed to find that funny or shameful.

**2. The steel room is funny once (chapters 8, 12, 16).**
"'It's the ti—' Lesca said. She started again, and it stuck on the same word." Then, four hundred words on: "'I w—' Lesca's fist closed." and "'I'm coming to get—' It stuck there."
In chapter 4 Lesca chairs a meeting of two with nobody in the chair, and it is the best comic idea in the book. She never chairs again. The truth rule produces the same gag six times in the same form: a sentence sticks, she starts again, it sticks. The rule says "standing up", and nobody ever sits down to try a lie.
Do: in chapter 8 keep one stuck sentence ("to get—" becoming "To stay", which is the one that matters) and cut the other two. Give Lesca her agenda back for the Wednesday meeting, so that what she cannot say is an item she has to strike. Use the chair once.

**3. The rules are explained straight, twice, and patched at the climax (chapters 8, 12).**
"Now I know. It comes with me. Keeping me out for a night or two changes nothing, because the river catches up the day I'm back."
Coralie's chart at breakfast (*What. Since when. How do you know.*) has already delivered this, and delivered it funnily. That night Lesca says it again without a joke. In chapter 12, with the van stalled and a man at the end of the track, three sentences explain why she lands in the hall and not in her bed.
Do: cut the steel-room restatement to its one new fact ("One night matters."). Cut the three sentences in 12. She is standing barefoot in the hall, and the reader will accept it.

**4. The last two chapters close every running joke by the same move (15, 16).**
"She had rung it on the ends of Larrère's sentences for eleven years. Now it had rung on hers before she began one."
"I'll be brief" meets silence, the timer rings on her, Marie-Jo speaks to her and not the wall, "Madame le maire" becomes "Lesca, Maylis", "Still in the box" becomes "went for a walk", the oven fails, forty-two arrives, the fingers come out five. Each one is good. Ten in a row, each a reversal, and by the council scene I was predicting them. Chapter 15 is three reversals and little else.
Do: keep the reversals and cut the sentence after each that points at it. Let one go unpaid; the timer is the one to drop, since "I'll be brief" and the surname do the same work better.

**5. The narrator explains the joke.**
"That made twice he had asked her about a kitchen. He hadn't asked once about a river." (6) "For sixteen years Maylis had sat down and said nothing, in the wrong kitchen." (6) "Nobody in Peyrebarthe had ever heard Maylis's mouth forbid anything." (5) "There were five of them, locked on the brake. It was a short count." (12) "She said it to Lesca this time, not to the wall." (15)
In each case the scene has just shown it.
Do: cut every one. Nothing replaces them.

**6. The cut to Coralie's vigil breaks the best comic scene in the book (chapter 6).**
"She had been in room twelve when the old woman told them the rest. She had made a joke of it then."
Larrère has just found the timer silent and started finishing eleven years of sentences. The book leaves him for seven lines in the other village, in the only passage that neither woman witnesses, to remind the reader of a danger that the first paragraph of chapter 7 shows anyway, with a chart.
Do: cut the passage.

**7. The driven scenes count body parts, and the physical comedy slows (chapters 5, 7).**
"Lesca unlocked the box with the key from the belt, fitted the handle and began to turn it with the left hand. The chain came up wet. Lesca counted the clicks of the catch as it came."
"Lesca" is the subject ten times in that one short scene; "the mouth", "the legs", "the eyes", "the jaw" carry every action in the bar. It is funny when it is a line ("Lesca said out loud, to the right hand"). As a method it makes slapstick read like a parts list.
Do: one name per run of actions. Halve the count in the gate scene of 5 and the track walk of 7. Leave the bar in 7 nearly as it is; there the fight for the jaw is the joke.

**8. Chapter 1 teaches the map before anyone funny arrives.**
"First came the care home, then the patch she repaired every spring with four wheelbarrows of clay, then the floodgate. After the gate a track ran out across the flood fields to Jeannot Castagnet's duck hut."
The opening has already shown the bank, the gate, the platform and the track. Chapter 1 shows them again, adds the association, the key and the president, and only then lets the postman walk up to the car. Jeannot's speech at the van window teaches the fields and the water better, and it is funny.
Do: cut the geography paragraph to the key and the cracked rib. Get to "The postman was new" sooner.

**9. Eight lines about the key in chest-deep water (chapter 13).**
"'I put that handle in number six,' he said. / 'I wrote to them too.' / 'I know you did. They took note.'"
This is the one place where a callback weakens a dark moment. The water is still rising, a man is waiting to be carried, and two people stop to settle the letters.
Do: keep "It's up", the key held out, and "It's no use to anybody now. Have it back." Move the rest to the graveside, where number six is already half said.

**10. Two beats in chapter 5 where the joke's mechanism is unclear.**
"The mouth stopped on it. It tried again. / 'Ei—' Lesca said. 'You have three minutes.'"
The steel room has just taught that a word sticks when it is untrue. Here a word sticks in a square, in daylight, for a different reason (she gave it up at thirty-one), and the reader tries the wrong rule first. Earlier, Maylis saying "Either" to block Lesca at breakfast needs a second read to see what she blocked.
Do: one clause at the window to say whose refusal it is. At breakfast, let the reader see what Lesca's breath was for before Maylis spends it.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **"Either" on the platform** (11), and Coralie's reading of "Both" (16). The whole book hangs on a word from a shopping pad.
2. **Larrère through the oven door**, all three times: the eggs (1), the soup that "will be consulted" (8), and "'I'll be brief,' … 'The—'" (16).
3. **The verb for the stone** (3): "saved", "opened", the bell on Maïté's mouth, and her verb when it comes: "*Left*."
4. **Both bingo halls.** The living one (7), from the coffee over Josiane's shoes to "How's that spelled?". The dead one (12): the man with his arm round his card, Jean Castagnet putting down a grain of corn in answer, and "You can put that in the minutes as apologies received."
5. **Jeannot's count**, from "One. Those are my boots" to six points down a phone to letter No. 42. With it, Inguma doing the roadsides on foot while the secretary puts the next letter on the floor to be signed.
