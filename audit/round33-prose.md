Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes 7.5.** They are counted, which is why they hold: "Behind it were forty houses, and the care home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the fields there was one man".
- **Tension 7.5.** Chapters 11 to 13 are at Crouch's level ("The bank had six minutes left."). Chapters 6 to 8 let it out: "She pushed until 4 again, flat on the kitchen floor" covers two nights in two paragraphs of summary.
- **Progression 6.** Rules are still arriving at the climax: "that ring had laid a place for her here. But she had let go before she was sitting in it."
- **Comedy 8.** "Eleven years I've been timed like an egg."
- **Characters 8.** "I don't cook Either. There's no recipe for it."
- **The switch between dark and comic 7.5.** At its best in the flood: "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'" At its worst in chapter 7, where the tooth farce runs over "You're the water."
- **Prose 7.** The base sentence is exact and made of the woman's own work ("Ten minutes was what it took her to hook up the trailer"). It loses a point to the narrator explaining, and to the grammar of the shared body: "Lesca walked her up onto it and stood her on the trapdoor."
- **The ending 8.** "Then he stepped over the string in his rubber boots and began to dig."
- **Clarity 6.** "Inguma had gone to count the corn, so nothing would take Lesca home tonight. Maylis had lost." The narrator has to keep the score because the scene cannot.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Crouch: above him by the line, below him by the page.**

- Above. No sentence in the book is generic. Every comparison comes from the job or the village: "thinner than the manual for the village mower", "the way mud comes home on a boot". Crouch reaches for the nearest phrase and italics.
- Below. Crouch's reader never stops to parse. Here the reader works out whose hand is whose in 5 and 7, and what the current rule is in 7, 8 and 12. His rules arrive inside a panic. These arrive as agenda items, which is funny once (chapter 4) and a cost by the fourth time.
- Level. Chapter 13 from "02:59." to "Forty-one." The sentences shorten into fragments only there ("Torch up. Right arm across her side."), so the change is felt.

**The Good Place: level on voice.** Each person is wrong in their own grammar: Jeannot numbers his grievances, Larrère says "I'll be brief", Sarthou talks like his own report ("I do not record that I would have stayed"), Maïté cannot finish a sentence. That is how that show builds people, and it is done as well here.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The narrator referees the rules at the peaks (7, 8, 12). Structural.**
Chapter 12: "Inguma hadn't taken her home. She had held the wheel against it for a whole ring, and that ring had laid a place for her here. But she had let go before she was sitting in it. One more ring and she would have sat in it for good. As it was, Inguma would have to take her home from here." Five sentences of ruling between the best beat in the chapter ("Go to the hut first.") and the hall of the dead. Chapter 7 has the same voice keeping score: "For one breath, Maylis had won." and, six lines later, "Maylis had lost."
The prose is explaining because there is one rule too many. Delete the rule that a ring of resistance lays a place, and delete the paragraph with it. She refuses to be taken, she is standing in the hall, her name is called, her fingers lock on the seat. The scene already says all of it. In 7, cut both score lines and let "That's till morning," do the work, as it already does.

**2. The grammar of the driven body breaks down in 5 and 7. Structural, because it is the device of the whole middle.**
"The mouth", "the left hand", "the eyes" is a good invention ("Lesca kept the eyes on it longer than a cut deserved"). Two things spoil it.
The narration will not let "she" mean the driver, so the name becomes a drumbeat: fifteen "Lesca" in the 526 words of the walk to the hut, ten in the 382 words at the gate, forty in chapter 5. "Lesca waited for the second hand to reach the top, then walked her down off the bank... Lesca kept to the grass... Lesca didn't slow down... Lesca walked through it... 'Fifteen.'... Lesca walked her up onto it... 'Twenty-five,' Lesca said. Lesca didn't step off."
And the article rule is not kept. "Maylis's mouth spoke without her." (5), "her legs turn towards the café" beside "The legs stopped inside the door" (5), "Maylis could feel her own face smiling" beside "The face went on smiling" (5), "Maylis's left hand had served eleven coffees and her mouth hadn't said another word" (7), "He looked at the mud on her boots and at the watch in her hand" (7).
Set one rule and hold it. In a driven scene the driver is "she" once named, the passenger is always named, and a contested part takes "the" every time. Then join the driver's actions into longer sentences so the name appears once a paragraph.

**3. The moral of the climax is said five times.**
Chapter 12: "This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel." Chapter 13: "If she turned that handle, a man drowned and her hand had done it. If she went on, whatever the river did behind her was the river's doing. She wanted it not to be her." Chapter 16, to Kévin: "I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me." To Coralie, from Coralie: "That was Either, with the river in it." To Lesca: "I drove past the handle to be the one who did."
Coralie's line is the best and the last one adds something (vanity). Keep those two and "She wanted it not to be her." Cut the two sentences before it in 13, the steering-wheel line in 12 and the whole line to Kévin, whose scene belongs to his fingers.

**4. Chapter 3 opens in a voice the book never uses again, then derives one fact three times.**
"The other Maylis Lesca was the one whose village had its flood in 2014 and made her mayor. Call her Lesca." The narrator addresses the reader once in 43,000 words, to hand over what the last line of chapter 2 made the reader want to find. Cut both sentences. The pillow, the coat on the door and "Give me that wrist, Madame le maire" do it within a page.
On the ladder, "Tuesday had been the 24th." is the discovery. It is then restated ("If the dates went on matching, she could read every line of the file as this year's, over there"), read out of the file, and restated again ("Ever since she stood at the stone that morning... Five lines later it was still falling"). Keep the date, the five file lines and "That village hadn't been spared its flood. It hadn't had it yet."

**5. The cutaway in chapter 6 is the only time the narration leaves the two women, and it spends chapter 7's opening.**
"In the other village it was raining... She had been in room twelve when the old woman told them the rest. She had made a joke of it then." This is Coralie alone, with her thought given. Nothing else in the book is told from outside Maylis or Lesca. Cut the four paragraphs. The chart in 7 ("a pulse for every half hour since 6 and two columns of ticks, headed *eyes* and *turned*") is the same information as an object, and it lands harder unannounced.

**6. Explanation after the gesture.**
Chapter 15: "She took the dial and turned it back to nothing. The bell rang." Then: "She had rung it on the ends of Larrère's sentences for eleven years. Now it had rung on hers before she began one." Delete the second paragraph. Also delete:
- 5: "It was the only word she had."
- 5: "Nobody in Peyrebarthe had ever heard Maylis's mouth forbid anything."
- 5: "Maylis knew what that meant by now."
- 6: "Larrère was both. And since 9 that morning he had been keeping a sheet of paper with her signature on it."
- 6: "The mayor's vote had made the tie. Then it had broken it." (the secretary has just said so)
- 8: "The vote had left the gate shut for Friday afternoon. If the rib let Lesca in on Friday night, the gate would have to come up in the dark, and that job would be Maylis's."
- 13: "She had put Lesca out, and it had come too late: the river was through the bank already."
- 15: "Marie-Jo looked at the sash. She had handed her own back across this table in a plastic bag." (third telling, after 6 and 14)

**7. Scenes are set by the clock and by inventory.**
Chapter 5's sections open: "At 8 Maylis was standing on the platform", "At 9 Maylis was standing beside the village van", "At noon Maylis felt her legs turn", "At 7 there was soup", "At 9:05 Coralie turned off the light". Chapter 11 has seven clock openers. Eleven more times a scene fixes itself with the formula "It was 9 on Tuesday morning. She was standing in the door of Larrère's office". The set-up that follows is a run of was/had statements: "Rain was running down the kitchen window. Coralie had just come down in her uniform. The voice was Lesca's. It was lower than Maylis's own. Maylis had her right hand and nothing else."
The clock should mean something when it appears, because 2:51 and 03:11 carry the book. Keep times on the Friday countdown and wherever a minute is plot. Elsewhere open on the action and fold the hour into it.

**8. Stock beats.**
The look that stands in for a decision, a dozen times: "He looked at the timer, then at the one hand lying on top of the other." "He looked at the seven, then at the floor of the van." "She looked at the loaf, then at the way Maylis held her side." "He looked at the string, at the cut grass, at the way she held the shovel." Keep the two where the look is the event (the bus driver with the coins; Jeannot looking "for two breaths" down the corridor) and vary the rest.
Also "It was out before she had time to think" (1), "It had come out before she had a reason" (4), "It was out before she could stop the mouth" (6); and "took her time" four times.

**9. Words and constructions used too often.**
- "the way" as a comparison, 25 times, and one of them three times over: "She read it the way she read a job sheet" (6), "she wrote it out the way she wrote out any job" (8), "She worked it out the way she worked out a job" (13). Keep 13.
- A span of years, 40 times. Chapter 1 has "Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you" and opens the next paragraph "Sixteen years earlier".
- "never once" seven times, from three speakers and the narrator, plus "he never rang me once" from both Larrère and Jeannot.
- Sentences opening "Then", 87.
- The rib as an agent that permits: "The rib let her have an hour of it", "Her rib let her walk, and no faster", "it let her have a third of each breath", "let her have a quarter of a breath", "the rib gave Maylis one breath". The breath fractions in 13 are good. Cut the others.
- "top of the wardrobe" is checked twice inside one scene in 10; the second ("When she looked up once more") weakens the first.

**10. Consistency of usage.**
- Two Englishes. "truck" (3), "dish towel" (7), "baby carriage" (2), "dump" (2) sit beside "torch", "pavement", "cool box", "handbag", "mum", "rubbish bin" and "Bugger off". Make them lorry, tea towel, pram, tip.
- One object, two names: "a red line on the pillar" (3, 8, 14) and "the marker post" (7, 8, 9, 13). Chapter 8 uses both.
- "the steel room" in narration, except "The door of the box" three times.
- A single hut everywhere except "the duck huts" (6) and "the hunters' huts" (11).
- Twenty-four-hour figures mean a screen or a typed file all through the book. In 13 "03:03." and "03:06." fall while her phone is on the van seat; she picks it up at 03:08.
- "a.m." three times (the dateline of 1, "6:10 a.m." in 5, "4 a.m." in 13) against "at 5 in the morning" everywhere else.
- The narrator says "you" only in 13: "the way you carry a child", "the kind you turn by hand".
- Chapter 8's last section opens "she had no push to give it at all" when the last woman named was Coralie.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The oven door.** "'In my tray,' she said, and shut the door on him." Its return in 8, and its failure in 16: "'I'll be brief,' she said... 'The—'".
2. **The prologue's sentences coming back in 13 for the other woman.** "the clay moved under her palm." "She was scared." Same words, no comment.
3. **Room twelve to the count.** From "Twelve was shut." to "'Forty-one,' Coralie said." Nobody says what they feel and nothing is missing.
4. **Jeannot's six points on the phone,** ending "And six. Bugger off.", with "A man had just sworn at her in six points, and he was alive."
5. **Kévin's hand.** "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much." / "It was five."
