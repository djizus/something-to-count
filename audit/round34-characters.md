# Critic report: Characters

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** "I'd have had you. You answer. But what I'd have had is a river over forty-two beds." (8) One sentence holds the marriage and the care home together, and from there both are at risk on the same night.
- **Tension: 7.** "She had worked the roadsides out at the speed of the village tractor. Inguma was doing them on foot." (6) The one scene where Maylis could die in the wrong body is played for the joke. Nobody is in danger on the page before chapter 11.
- **Progression: 7.** "It comes with me. Keeping me out for a night or two changes nothing, because the river catches up the day I'm back." (8) The turn that makes Lesca the cause arrives halfway through the book, after two chapters (5 and 7) of the same shape: Lesca performs in public, the village reads adultery, the night ends with Coralie on the stairs.
- **Comedy: 8.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station." (6)
- **Characters: 8.** "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." (7) The cast is at the bar. The two heroines' worst decisions are not (faults 1 and 2).
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'" (13) A bingo joke from chapter 7 carried into a flooded corridor without changing a word.
- **Prose: 7.** "It wasn't much. She took more than that to the dump in a morning." (16) That is the book at its best. It drops when the narrator argues a character's change for her (fault 5).
- **The ending: 8.** "It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you. I was in no danger, and I'll say so to anyone." (16) Maylis's side costs her everything and is paid. Lesca's side is thin (fault 4).
- **Clarity: 6.** "Inguma takes the visitor home when it has finished counting, and anybody who knows the words can give it something to count." (4) Four rules, two villages, two of everyone. Chapter 10 opens on "Her mother" and takes three paragraphs to say whose.

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. Would I press it on a friend? Yes, on one who reads for people. Not on one who wants Crouch's speed.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Characters: level.** Above Crouch on the cast and the wife, level with The Good Place on the ensemble, below both on the heroines' decisive moments.

- **Above Crouch: the wife judges the copies.** In Dark Matter Daniela is what the Jasons fight over. Coralie prefers the other woman ("I'd have this one again"), walks three of the four steps, refuses on a principle from her own trade, and has a want that is neither of them (Bayonne). She is the most alive person in the book.
- **Level with The Good Place: each is wrong in a form of speech.** Maylis's "Either", Lesca's "No one came to tell him", Larrère's "I'll be brief", Jeannot's "One. Two.", Josiane's syllogism. As with Chidi, the indecision is what damns, and here it kills somebody else. "You're the water" re-reads the rain from page one and turns the rescuer into the cause.
- **Below both: the two decisions that matter most are over in a word and a clause.** The Good Place stages Chidi's dithering at length, because the length is the sin. Crouch makes every choice of Jason's a scene. Here Maylis's fatal Either takes one line (fault 1) and Lesca's decision to carry a river onto forty-two beds takes half a sentence (fault 2).

The lens questions, briefly:

- **Distinct?** Yes, in speech and in the hands. One counts in trailers and wheelbarrows, the other in items and motions.
- **Softened?** Lesca by omission, at two decisions (faults 2 and 5). Maylis in chapter 16, by who is absent (fault 3). Neither is ever described from outside as a woman. They have a rib and a scar and no faces, and that is right.
- **Who would be missed?** Madame Etcheto, Jeannot, Kévin, Monsieur Daguerre, Sarthou, Marie-Jo. Not missed: Maylis's mother, the dealer, the Larrère of Maylis's village, who is a folder and a window.
- **Which relationship carries it?** Maylis and Coralie, and it pays in full: "the car door shutting on a pillow". Maylis and Jeannot pays best. Maylis and Lesca pays in six lines, which is enough. Lesca and her own Coralie does not pay.
- **Does the reader care who lives?** Yes: for Coralie behind the fire door, and for Madame Etcheto, who has three scenes and the best lines in each.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. Chapter 11. The Either that kills is one word.**
"'Either,' Maylis said. / 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said." Then: "She lifted the handle off."
This is the moral centre. It is daylight, the handle is fitted, Jeannot is standing on the bank, and both can be saved. Chapter 16 hangs the whole verdict on it: "At 5 on Friday it was light, and Castagnet was up on the bank at your van window." But on the page she folds in one line, an hour after asking Coralie back and three days after saying "Open" twice in front of the folder. She then unfits the handle herself with no reason given. On first reading it looks like the plot needing the gate shut, and the charge in 16 comes as news.
*Do:* let her turn. Count the turns towards nineteen with the chain moving, and let Jeannot's "It's your hand on the handle" land while it moves. Give the one line of why here, in its first form: with him watching, it would be her hand that filled his hut. Chapter 13's "She wanted it not to be her" then reads as the second time, and the reader holds her to it.

**2. Chapter 10. Lesca decides to cross in a subordinate clause.**
"This Coralie knew. She was four steps away with her door open, but Lesca still meant to go."
The worst thing either woman does knowingly is to take a river over forty-two beds for a bed with two pillows. She decides it straight after the scene that removes her reason: her own Coralie has known about 2:51 for twelve years and has left her door open. The narrator covers for her ("She had told her it was for Castagnet") and the chapter ends on a joke about Bayonne. She is spared the scene in which she chooses.
*Do:* make her try the four steps. Chapter 3 has the model: "Lesca had the sentence ready. This was the woman it was for." / "Nothing. Go back to bed." Put her at the open door with the sentence and let her fail it again. She crosses because she cannot ask at home. Then the crossing is hers and the reader can hold it against her.

**3. Chapter 16. The woman with the right to accuse Maylis never appears.**
"Her daughter wants the words she said each night said over her."
Lesca has faced Maïté across a table and held her ladder for twelve years. Maylis's equivalent is a mention by a priest. Chapter 16 gives Maylis two accusers (Madame Darrigrand, the dealer) and then four kindnesses in a row: Kévin's tow, Marie-Jo's chair, Coralie's bandage, Jeannot's shovel. The cost of her choice has no face among the living, and the grave becomes a penance she sets herself and everyone accepts.
*Do:* put the daughter at the cemetery gate in place of the priest, with the words written out in her hand. She need say very little. Maylis must dig with her watching.

**4. Chapter 15. Lesca's Coralie is paid with a name and nothing else.**
"'Good night, Maylis,' she said, and went into the spare room."
Chapter 10 makes this the most interesting marriage in the book: a woman who kept a landing for twelve years so as not to hear which answer it was. She says "I'll be in the second row, and I'll know which you did." Lesca then confesses to two hundred chairs and never to her. Saturday to Tuesday in that house is skipped. The other Coralie gets three pages to leave. This one gets eight lines.
*Do:* one exchange on the Saturday night. Coralie says what she heard from the second row. Lesca gives her the only true thing she found in chapter 10, that all she can remember is turning the handle, which is neither of Coralie's two answers. Keep the name, the open door and the lamp.

**5. Chapter 12. Lesca's one renunciation is argued by the narrator.**
"This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel."
The only time she gives up what she wants, three paragraphs reason it out for her inside one ring of a phone: there is "no both", the motion had "no man in it", this is "the same thing". The reader is handed the conclusion. Compare chapter 10, where Coralie's speech leaves the reader to do the sum.
*Do:* cut to what she sees. The reflector is no nearer than it was twelve years ago. The hand on the brake is the hand from the stair post. Then "I'm letting go" and the five words.

**6. Chapter 14. Lesca stands a few metres from the living Madame Etcheto and the page gives her nothing.**
"Madame Etcheto sat at the end nearest the stone with her handbag on her knees. Her lips were moving."
One page after the reader has watched her eyes closed, she is back, and Lesca, who heard "Twelve. One, two" and a chair pulled in, looks at her without a flicker. It reads as the narrator forgetting, and it softens the death before chapter 15 uses it properly.
*Do:* one look, and Lesca cannot hold it. Nothing more.

**7. Chapters 9 and 11. Either has a parent and Maylis never sees him.**
"For thirty years he stopped four turns short, to leave a man his water, and he never rang me once."
Six times a man tells her what her father did. She has not one thought about him of her own until he fails to look up from his card. Yet Jeannot has just described the father's own Either: a gate never fully opened, so that both the man and the river got something. The book has the root of her habit in its hand and does not let her notice it.
*Do:* one line when she fits the handle in chapter 11. She knows where four turns short leaves it.

**8. Chapter 8. Maylis's mother gets one speech and disappears.**
"I'll have them on the high ground by Thursday, shut or not."
Lesca's Arlette has three scenes and the hardest lines in the book ("I'm not asking you why. I don't want to be told."). Maylis's lives across the yard, is the only person in the village who acts on her daughter's word, and is not seen again on the weekend that daughter loses her job, her wife and her name.
*Do:* one line in chapter 16. The ponies were on the high ground. That she believed it is all the reader needs.

**9. Chapter 6. Maylis meets the other Coralie and reacts only to herself.**
"Maylis put the left hand over it and held it there."
It is the single scene she has with a Coralie who calls her wife Madame le maire and would carry her pillow back for a woman who sits when told. Maylis thinks about her own sixteen years. She then stops Lesca's hand reaching for Lesca's own wife, and the page does not say whether that is protection, payment for the stairs or jealousy.
*Do:* one line of what she sees in this Coralie that hers does not have, and a reason for the hand.

**10. Chapters 7, 11 and 16. The wrist never comes back.**
"'That's her,' she said. 'In this hand. I've had sixteen years of her saying nothing, and I know it when I've got hold of it.'"
Three scenes establish that Coralie knows Maylis by a wrist that pulls away. Maylis never once reaches for Coralie in the book. Every touch is Coralie's or Lesca's. In chapter 16 Coralie's hand "stayed on it a little longer than a bandage needs" and nothing answers it. The motif is set up and not spent.
*Do:* on the doorstep in chapter 11, when Coralie says "Say it again", Maylis gives her the wrist. Once. It makes the ask bodily, and it makes the pillow in the car cost more.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie's Larrère through the oven door**, all three times: "'In my tray,' she said, and shut the door on him" (1), the soup vote (8), and the one that breaks off at "'The—'" (16).
2. **The landing, chapter 7.** Three steps of four, the pulse under her fingers, "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak."
3. **The phone, chapter 10.** "'2:51,' she said. 'I've known that for twelve years.'" and "Because you'd have told me."
4. **Lesca in the hall of the dead.** "You can put that in the minutes as apologies received." With it, the father in chapter 8: "He marked a number. He didn't look up, and she took the hand away."
5. **Room twelve and the count, chapter 13.** "She held it for the full half minute." The line through the last name. "'Forty-one,' Coralie said."
