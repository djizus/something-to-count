## 2. Against the comparisons

Crouch is not funny and does not try to be. The book beats him on comedy by default, and that is worth nothing. The live comparison is The Good Place. **Below it, by a point.**

Level with it in two things:

1. **The joke and the moral problem are the same material.** The village's evasions are both its comedy and its guilt: a stone with no verb ("It reads that she opened him"), a mayor who talks through a gap, a woman who writes *Either* on a shopping pad. When the choice comes, the running gag is what kills: "'Either,' Maylis said. / 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said." (11). The problem is funny for ten chapters and is not made small.
2. **The reversals re-read what came before.** "Castagnet, house" on the phone screen re-reads the kitchen clock. "'He was dry.' / 'Don't tell me what he was.'" re-reads the rescue. "When they called twelve, somebody sat down" re-reads the bingo. "'I said that house. I only had the one name wrong.'" re-reads four chapters of Josiane.

Below it in three:

1. **The central pair hardly talk.** The Good Place's people grind on each other in every scene. The woman who says Either and the woman who chairs everything get one scene of talk (4). After that they wrestle.
2. **Too many people are funny the same way.** The Good Place's four are each wrong differently. Here nine people are funny by holding a sheet, a pad, a form or a register.
3. **Timing.** The Good Place states a rule once, inside a joke. Here the rule comes back as a sentence of narration at the punchline.

On the switch:

- **Is it clean?** Yes. The method is one method and it works every time: a gag returns and cannot be finished. The oven door (16), "I'll be brief" followed by nothing (15), "This time nobody called out" (11), the dealer's "Had the key and went for a walk, did you?" (16).
- **Where a joke weakens a dark moment:** once. Chapter 13, at the care home door, with Hourcade waiting to be carried and room twelve unopened, Jeannot and Maylis stop for "'I put that handle in number thirty.' / 'I wrote to them too.' / 'I know you did. They took note.'" The water is rising and the scene idles for seventeen lines.
- **Where the dark smothers the comedy:** the second half of chapter 10, and the first third of chapter 7.
- **Are the people funny in themselves?** Coralie, Jeannot, Larrère, Madame Etcheto and Josiane are. Lesca is when she chairs. Maylis is the straight woman and is good company. Kévin could be and is kept off the page.
- **Do the comic scenes build?** The chrysanthemums (4), the mayor's day (6) and the association at the café door (8) build. The bingo (7) builds and is then explained. Larrère untimed (6) stops as it starts.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

### 1. The double act is one scene long (structural; chapters 4, 5 to 8, 12, 16)

> "'Then it's carried, or it's a tie, and in a tie the chair decides.'
> 'There's nobody in the chair.'" (4)

> "'That's a day,' Lesca said to the right hand." (5)

The premise is two versions of one woman. The comedy of that premise is the two of them in a room. It happens once, in chapter 4, and it is the best exchange they have. From chapter 5 they share a body and stop talking: Lesca addresses a hand, and the hand answers with a tipped spoon and a shut tap. Those are good physical gags. They are not a pair. The later steel-room scenes are short and solemn: fourteen lines on Sunday night (8), a negotiation on Wednesday (8), ten lines in 12, a farewell in 16. All the verbal comedy goes to the villagers, and the two leads become the least funny relationship in their own book.

**Do:** give them a second full meeting where the Sunday-night scene in chapter 8 now stands, and put Maylis in the chair. She has just spent a day with the timer and said "I want that" (6), and that line is never paid between the two of them. Let her run the meeting, time Lesca, and beat her on a point of order. Then let Lesca put Coralie's question to her while she is standing: that morning Maylis answered "It's twenty minutes to Bayonne", and in that room she cannot. This also repairs fault 5.

### 2. The café refuses her offstage (structural; chapter 11)

> "She had tried the café first, but the nine who had signed on Tuesday had watched Jeannot's hand go up. A vote was a vote."

The café is the book's chorus. It has been funny for ten chapters: the pad, the cross, Josiane's theory, "She's got a mayor in her". On the Friday that chorus shuts its door on her, and that is the moment the comedy becomes the cause of a death. It is given two sentences of summary. The reader never watches the room that made them smile say no. The Castagnet farce is also left without a head: it is built carefully across chapters 4, 5 and 7 and then pays only in Josiane's one line in 16.

**Do:** write the scene. Maylis asks the counter for two arms for one night. Bordenave reads her the cross. Josiane says what she thinks Maylis wants Jeannot out of his hut for. Nobody gets off a stool. Then the dealer's line in 16 is the café's second verdict on her, not its first.

### 3. Rules are restated at the punchline (chapters 5, 7, 8)

> "Lesca hadn't finished the words, so Inguma had nothing to count and would take her home." (7)

> "Inguma had gone to count the corn, so nothing would take Lesca home tonight." (7)

The bingo is the set piece. Its turn is that Madame Etcheto, being kind, finishes the prayer and undoes everything Maylis has held her jaw shut for. That is a first-rate comic reversal, and the narrator explains it before it lands and again after. The old woman's "That's till morning" already does the work. The body-sharing scenes are also written in a parts list that stiffens them: "the mouth", "the jaw", "the left hand", "the eyes", and "Lesca" as the subject of four sentences running, because "she" would be unclear.

**Do:** cut both sentences. State once, in chapter 4, that the owner can take the mouth for a few words and pays for it, then stop accounting for it scene by scene. In driven scenes, pick one body part per paragraph and let the rest be "she".

### 4. The kitchen scenes have one shape (chapters 4, 5, 7, 8, 9)

> "'For a minute there I had somebody,' she said. She took her keys off the hook." (5)

> "Coralie picked up the bowl of coffee and poured it down the sink." (8)

> "'I asked where you'd be,' Coralie said. She went up to the spare room." (9)

Coralie asks. Maylis under-answers. Coralie takes something out of the room: a torn page, her keys, her pillow, the coffee, herself. Six scenes end this way. By the fourth I had the last line before it came, and the best company in the book had turned into a reproach on a schedule. Between the eggs in chapter 1 and the doorstep in chapter 11 the house has no scene that ends warm.

**Do:** chapter 9 is the place. "'Duck,' Maylis said." and "'I'll have to ask you everything like that.'" is the one time she gets it right. End that scene with Coralie's hand still on the rib. Move "Where will you be?" to the doorstep in 11, where it leads straight to "Be in the house." In chapter 8 keep "And then there's me, as a distance" and drop one of the two other exits.

### 5. The truth rule makes one joke and then four identical solemn beats (chapters 4, 8, 12)

> "'It comes because no—'" (4)
> "'It's the ti—'" (8)
> "'I w—'" (8)
> "'This time I'm coming to get—' It stuck there." (8)

A room where a woman on her feet cannot say a false thing is the funniest idea in the book's machinery. It produces one joke, "'Tell me it's Tuesday.'" Then it is used four times with the same staging: the fist closes, the sentence restarts, the truer one comes out. After chapter 4 it only ever catches Lesca. Maylis has lied by leaving things out for sixteen years, and she is never stood up and asked.

**Do:** catch Maylis with it (see fault 1). Cut "I w—"; the sentence after it carries the meaning. Keep "It came out whole. They both heard it." in 16, which is the rule's proper payoff.

### 6. Nine people are funny by paperwork, and the one who isn't is missing (throughout)

> "'For the minutes.'" (6) · "'I am reporting a line.'" (7) · "'Which do I put?'" (5) · "'Is this a request, or a notification?'" (11) · "'I've got nothing on the sheet for this.'" (14)

Jeannot numbers, Lesca chairs, Sarthou reports, Bordenave records, the secretary minutes, the deputy mayor reads with a finger, the clerk at Hontaube has a form, the young man from Pau has a sheet, Coralie rules columns. Each is good. Together they are one joke in nine mouths, and by the Hontaube call I saw the device before the line. Everyone in the village is shrewd and dry. Nobody is plainly foolish. The exception is Kévin: "I want to fly. As high as the church. Is that stupid?" (2). In Maylis's village he is then off the page until chapter 8, where he is up a ladder and silent.

**Do:** put Kévin in the café at noon in 5 and at the bingo in 7. He is the only one who would take "She's got a mayor in her" at its word and ask the question nobody with a pad would ask. His two-questions-at-a-time habit is the one register in the book not made of paper, and it makes "Five" and "I wanted six" hit harder for having been funny first.

### 7. Larrère untimed stops as it starts (chapter 6)

> "'Eleven years I've been timed like an egg,' he said. 'I have sentences in that register with no ends on them. The secretary will find one under the cameras on the gates. "A man can no longer." It rang there. He can no longer stand at his own gate without Pau watching. I'm told I wave.'"

This is the best comic situation in the book: a man cut off by a bell for eleven years, and the bell is not coming. It delivers one finished sentence. The scene cuts to Coralie's bedside, and when it returns he is on the adultery. "By then he had been on his feet for eleven minutes." The reader got forty seconds of them.

**Do:** three more sentences from the register, finished at last, each pettier than the one before, ahead of the cut. The smaller his freed sentences are, the harder "When you stood on that platform, did you—" lands when the bell does come.

### 8. The moral problem arrives as an argument (chapters 9 and 10)

> "'So for twelve years I've had two answers. Either you opened it for forty houses, and I live with the mayor. Or you opened it because I was in that building.'" (10)

Everywhere else the problem turns up as a piece of procedure: a verb for a stone, a box to tick, a motion. Here it is laid out in a paragraph, by the wittiest person in the book, with nothing to puncture it. Lesca then says both halves again on the bed, and chapter 9 had already said it: "nothing in it told her which of the two Lesca had turned the handle for." That is three statements in two chapters. This is the longest stretch where I stopped smiling. The scene is not too dark for a smile; the phone log held me. The book stopped using its own method.

**Do:** keep the phone, "2:51", "'Because you'd have told me.'" and "'The phone doesn't say how long it rang. It says not answered.'" Cut the two-answers paragraph down to "I was in that building." Lesca's two sentences said aloud to an empty room are the procedural version, a motion read out to hear whether it is true, and they are enough. Cut the narrator's sentence in 9.

### 9. The last chapter explains itself three times (chapter 16)

> "'I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me.'"

> "'At the gate I thought I could do better than you,' she said. 'I drove past the handle to be the one who did.'"

Coralie has it in one line, and it is funny and terrible: "That was Either, with the river in it." The line to Kévin earns its place because he answers it with his hand. The steel-room exchange then has both women recite their motives to each other, all of which the reader watched happen.

**Do:** cut the steel-room scene to the two questions about the landing, "Don't come again." and "I won't."

### 10. Lines that need a word

- "Maylis had crossed from one village to the other, and Josiane had still got there first." (6). Larrère repeating Josiane word for word is the joke. This explains it. Cut.
- "Nobody in Peyrebarthe had ever heard Maylis's mouth forbid anything." (5). Jeannot's "and now it forbids me" does this. Cut.
- "And I'm standing there with my purse out, thinking, twice. I'd have liked to hear the second one. I'd have missed the first, looking for my purse." (8). Read three times; no smile. Go from "saying open, twice" to "My ponies are on that grass, Maylis."
- "each one whole and then in parts" appears five times (7, 8, 12 twice, 14). After the first, "Thirty-four. Three, four." shows it.
- "Call her Lesca." (3). The only sentence in the book where the narrator turns to the reader.
- The key exchange in 13 quoted in section 2. Jeannot's letter in 16 already says who was written to about the handle. Cut the three lines and get Hourcade carried.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Either.** From the shopping pad and "Eggs is what Either gets in this house" (1), through "Either is not a person" (6), to the platform (11), "Both" (13) and "I've cooked it" (16). It is the book's best device and it is rationed correctly.
2. **The gap in the window and the oven door.** The window going "all the way down, into the door" (5), the association chaired from the driver's seat (8), "I'll be brief" followed by nothing (15), and the impression Coralie cannot finish (16).
3. **Room twelve.** "'It's in the plan now. Staff to wait. Staff not to join in.'", "'With cards?'" (2), and the fact that she is the one who dies. "'Nobody emptied hers.'" (15).
4. **The verb for the stone.** "'It reads that she opened him.'" (3) through to "*Left*" (15).
5. **Jeannot's numbers.** "'One. Those are my boots.'" (4), the six points ending "Bugger off" (9), and No. 42 (16). He stops counting when it is serious. Leave that exactly as it is.
