# Critic's report: Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. 42,135 words. Counts below are whole-manuscript counts of the exact word or phrase.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** "'Forty-one,' Coralie said." (13) One number carries the whole cost, and the prose had the restraint to stop there.
- **Tension: 7.** "It was an hour and nine minutes short of 4." (11) Chapters 9, 11 and 13 are as tight as the comparison. Chapter 12 stops for an argument at ring eight (fault 1).
- **Progression: 6.** "On Sunday night Maylis could have pushed, but she lay still. Keeping Lesca out meant having a better plan, and she didn't have one." (4) Decisions in the middle are reported by the narrator, and chapters 6 to 8 lay the same pieces down again.
- **Comedy: 7.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (6) Each speaker is funny in a syntax of their own. The smile goes when the jaw-and-hand mechanics take over the page (5, 7).
- **Characters: 7.** "'One. It's Jeannot. Two. Jean's for the stone.'" (7) The villagers are made of speech habits and they hold. Coralie talks in finished paragraphs (fault 5).
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'" (13) The bingo joke returns in the flood without a change of register. Nothing in the comparisons does this better.
- **Prose: 7.** Best: "It was the smallest thing that had happened to her since Friday." (16) Worst: "Maylis had never smiled at Jeannot Castagnet in her life, and the whole café was watching her do it." (5) The first trusts the reader; the second tells them what the smile meant.
- **The ending: 7.** "He went and got it. Then he stepped over the string in his rubber boots and began to dig." The last page is right. Before it, Maylis states her motive three times in three versions (fault 6).
- **Clarity: 6.** "Every half hour she put two fingers on the wrist and wrote down what she counted." (6) The sentence before it has Maylis as its subject. In the two-in-one-body scenes the reader keeps a ledger of whose hand is whose.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. From chapter 9 I could not have stopped. In 6 to 8 I was working.
**Press it on a friend?** Yes, with the warning to get to chapter 9.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Level with Crouch overall: above him sentence by sentence, below him on ease.**

- **Above.** Crouch's sentences are functional and often stock ("my heart is pounding"). This book's are exact about objects: the shopping pad with its boxes, the timer, the window that goes "all the way down, into the door. Maylis hadn't known it could." It has no filler, hedges, intensifiers or decorative dashes. A pattern check for formula prose finds little: a few colons doing a full stop's work ("it had come too late: the river was through the bank already", 13) and closing triads ("the desk, the stone, a square that said good morning", 6; "at the string, at the cut grass, at the way she held the shovel", 16).
- **Below.** A Crouch reader never has to work out who is acting. Here five chapters turn on "the mouth", "the jaw", "the left hand", and the convention is applied unevenly (fault 4). Crouch states a rule once and then breaks it; this narrator restates the rules in mid-scene (fault 8).
- **Below.** Crouch ends a chapter and the next begins further on. Here the short length makes repetition expensive: chapters 6 to 8 are 9,701 words, 23% of the book, and re-lay the gate, the forty houses, the plan and the last scene's events.
- **Level with The Good Place** on people being wrong in their own grammar: Larrère's "I'll be brief", Jeannot's numbered points, Sarthou's uncontracted "I am reporting a line", Lesca's "All those in favour." And on the reversal: "You're the water" makes every earlier "it was still raining" and "under a dry roof" re-read as plot.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The climax of Lesca's story is reasoned, not seen (12).**
"She had told Maylis there was no both. She had wanted both herself, the bed and Castagnet. But the motion she had written at her own kitchen table was for a seat and a bed. There was no man in it." Then: "Twelve years ago she had let his phone ring once and then done what she had come to do. This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel."
At ring eight, with Inguma on the chest and the engine dead, the prose goes into the past perfect and argues the theme. The paragraph before has already shown it: "the reflector was red, and no nearer than it had been twelve years ago."
*Do:* cut both quoted passages. Keep "As long as she held the wheel, nobody was going down that track at all" and go straight to "'I'm letting go.'" The same cut applies to the last page of 10 ("She had seen him, eleven days ago, on a stool in a café…"), which retells the café scene of 5 after the scene has ended on Coralie's phone.

**2. Chapter 3 opens by giving away what the chapter then discovers.**
"The other Maylis Lesca was the one whose village had its flood in 2014 and made her mayor. Call her Lesca."
"Call her Lesca" is the only time the narrator speaks to the reader, and the sentence before it hands over the stone, the flood and the mayoralty that the next three pages reveal one object at a time. The second paragraph is the real opening: "She lay on her back with rain falling on the roof. It could have been her own bedroom."
*Do:* delete the first paragraph. Let "Madame le maire" on this-world Coralie's lips name her; move the "eleven years across the landing" fact to where the alarm rings on the wrong side.

**3. The middle restates standing facts (5 to 8).**
The afternoon plan is given in full five times: "In daylight, while the tide is going out, by order, with the fields cleared" (4); "in daylight, while the tide is going out, with the fields cleared" (5); "in daylight, on the falling tide" (8); "On Friday at 2, on the falling tide, with nobody on the fields, by your order" (8); "she wanted the river let onto the fields in daylight, by order" (8). "Forty houses" appears 12 times, "forty-two" 13, "ten minutes" 8. In 5 Lesca explains the gate to the hand that keeps its key: "'This gate is a door in that bank … Raise it, and the river runs out over the fields.'" In 8 Coralie recounts the bingo scene the reader finished four pages earlier: "'She got three words out at that bar … The hall thought she was thirsty.'"
*Do:* the plan in full once (4), then "Friday at 2". Cut every recap of a scene already shown. This is about 1,500 words out of 5 to 8 and is the biggest single gain in pace.

**4. The driven-body convention is not held.**
The system is good: "the" for a part being driven, a possessive for the part its owner still has. It slips. "Maylis felt her legs turn towards the café" and, three lines on, "The legs stopped inside the door" (5). "Maylis could feel her own face smiling at him" then "The face went on smiling" (5). "Maylis's mouth spoke without her" (5) against "the mouth" 21 times elsewhere. "The legs" 7, "her legs" 6.
Chapter 5 also contests too many parts at once: legs, eyes, mouth, face, chest, both hands. Chapter 7 contests one, the jaw, and is the clearer and funnier for it.
*Do:* one rule with no exceptions, stated by use in 3 and never varied. One contested part per scene.

**5. Coralie does the reader's work in finished paragraphs (4, 7, 10, 16).**
"'So for twelve years I've had two answers. Either you opened it for forty houses, and I live with the mayor. Or you opened it because I was in that building.'" (10) The same doubt has just been stated by the narrator ("nothing in it told her which of the two Lesca had turned the handle for", 9) and is stated a third time when Lesca tests "I opened it for forty houses" and "I opened it for Coralie" aloud.
*Do:* keep Lesca's two test sentences, which are the scene. Cut the narrator's line in 9. Cut Coralie's to "I was in that building. The shifts were on the fridge door, and you'd read them." Her best lines are the short ones: "I know that word. I've cooked it."

**6. The ending gives Maylis's motive three times, and not the same motive (16).**
Chapter 13 has it once and exactly: "She wanted it not to be her." Then 16: "'I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me'" (to Kévin); "'At the gate I thought I could do better than you. I drove past the handle to be the one who did'" (to Lesca); "'I picked'" (to Coralie). Not wanting to be the hand, wanting to beat Lesca, and having chosen are three different accounts.
*Do:* keep the line to Kévin, which pays off his "Don't keep a gate shut for me". Cut the one to Lesca, or make it the same account. The steel-room scene then runs from "Forty-one" to "Don't come again", which is all it needs.

**7. Significance by duration and "never".**
"Years" 41 times, "never" 39, "nobody" 59; "for N years/winters" 26. In narration it tells the reader why a gesture matters. Chapter 5 has four: "had stood over that pad every morning for sixteen years and had never got as far as one tick"; "for eighteen years without once looking at it"; "had never smiled at Jeannot Castagnet in her life"; "Nobody in Peyrebarthe had ever heard Maylis's mouth forbid anything." Chapter 15 has three in ten lines ("For eleven years they had stood…", "For eleven years she had watched…", "He had waited eleven years…").
*Do:* leave it in speech, where it is how these people argue ("Eleven years I've been timed like an egg"). In narration, one per chapter. The window going down needs none.

**8. The narrator footnotes the mechanism and the joke.**
"Lesca hadn't finished the words, so Inguma had nothing to count and would take her home." Ten lines later: "Inguma had gone to count the corn, so nothing would take Lesca home tonight." (7) Madame Etcheto then says it in voice: "'That's till morning.'" Also: "Inguma hadn't taken her home. She had held the wheel against it for a whole ring, and this was where that put her" (12); "she knew why. Where Lesca came from, he was a name cut in a stone" (5); "In Peyrebarthe they had said that to anyone who came late, ever since the June of the steel room" (1), which explains the box joke before the box has been described.
*Do:* cut all five. Where a rule must be said, a character says it.

**9. Stock sentence shapes.**
- *The look.* "Looked" 93 times, "looked at" 53. As a reaction beat it clusters at the end: "He looked at the seven, then at the floor of the van"; "The driver looked at the coins, then at the suitcase"; "She looked at the loaf, then at the way Maylis held her side"; "He looked at the lit kitchen window, then at Maïté."
- *The entrance with a prop.* "With her bag on her arm", "with the ham under his arm", "with the pillow under her arm", "with his association's book under his arm": about 50 of this shape.
- *The trailing identification.* "He kept the café's score pad." "He was also Jeannot's cousin." "She ran the care home down in the Port." "He held the hunters' seat in the association."
- *Line of speech, then the time.* "It was 6:10 a.m. on Monday 30 November" (5); "It was 9 on Tuesday morning" (6); "It was Thursday evening" (9); "It was 9 on Friday morning" (10). Sixteen paragraphs open "It was".
- *Wanted, but, so.* Three times in the first hundred lines of 1: "But Coralie had to be up before 6, so Maylis didn't ask"; "But the duck meant an hour at the stove … So she wrote *Either*"; "But Larrère paid her wages, so she didn't knock on the glass."
- *The sack.* Six sacks for one sound.
*Do:* halve each. Keep one "wanted, but, so" in chapter 1; the pad does the rest.

**10. Slips and consistency.**
- Pronouns: "Every half hour she put two fingers on the wrist" (6, "she" is Coralie); "As Madame Etcheto's went by, she put her hand on the wheel" (15); "Then his headlights came down the hill again" (13, Kévin, last named three paragraphs earlier, with the minibus also out).
- Point of view: the intercut in 6 enters Coralie's head ("She had made a joke of it then"), the only time the book leaves the two Maylises.
- Second person: "the kind you turn by hand", "the way you carry a child" (13).
- Clock: "2:51 a.m.", "6:10 a.m.", "4 a.m." against bare "at 4", "at 9" everywhere else. Drop the three "a.m."
- The handle: "At eighteen, the bottom of the gate was a hand's width clear … At the nineteenth the handle stopped" (5), but in 11 the catch takes "its first tooth" and then "Eighteen more turns would lift it a hand. The nineteenth would need her shoulder", which makes it the twentieth.
- Vocabulary: British throughout (torch, windscreen, pavement, rubbish bin, bugger off) except "truck" (3), "baby carriage" (2), "dish towel" (7), "dump" (2).
- Consistent and correct: "No one" only in the refrain; "Madame le maire" with its small m; dates and weekdays.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The opening (00) and its return.** "The headlights lit up the rain" and "the clay moved under her palm" come back word for word in 11 and 13. "It rang once."
2. **The speech signatures and how they break.** Larrère's "There's the question of… It isn't settled, and you're not to worry about it"; Coralie at the oven door; and in 16 the same impression failing at "'The—'". Then, at Maïté's gate: "'I'll be brief,' he said. … He said nothing."
3. **Chapter 13 from the dip to the hall.** The timestamps, "Torch up. Right arm across her side.", the rooms nine, ten, eleven, "There was a hatbox on it. Nothing else."
4. **The confession and its travel (14).** "Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him." "The loudspeaker on the bank road said 'drowned him' after the square had finished hearing it." "A child asked what the lady had done. Somebody told the child."
5. **The small closings of 16.** "'I wanted six, Maylis.'" "the car door shutting on a pillow." "'It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you.'" The last sentence.
