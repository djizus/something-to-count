# Critic's report: Thriller

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the care home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the fields there was one man, at his duck hut" (3). Countable and on the table by the third chapter. Marked down because only the one man and Coralie have faces; the forty houses stay a number until their furniture is on the pavement.
- **Tension: 6.** "Then the screen said 01:12. She hadn't seen it change." (11) is the height it reaches. It does not hold that height: chapters 6, 9, 10 and 15 let the clock stand, and the book's secret is given away in the opening.
- **Progression: 6.** "It rang once and nobody answered." (9) repeats "It rang once." (00). The premise turns once, at "You're the water" (7). After that the last 45 per cent carries out a known date at a known place, and three chapters go back over 2014.
- **Comedy: 8.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (6).
- **Characters: 8.** "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'" (7).
- **The switch between dark and comic: 7.** Clean: "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" (16). Not clean: "'You can put that in the minutes as apologies received.'" (12), where a joke lets a woman walk out of her own death.
- **Prose: 7.** "It sounded like a truck tipping out a load of gravel a long way off, except that the sound didn't stop." (13). Marked down for tail sentences that explain the scene: "This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel." (12).
- **The ending: 7.** "He went and got it. Then he stepped over the string in his rubber boots and began to dig." (16). The last image is right. It takes three chapters to reach, and one of the two women pays less than she was told she would.
- **Clarity: 6.** "'One night matters. If I'm in your village at 2:51 on Friday night, your bank goes. If I'm not, it holds.'" (8). The rule the whole final night stands on is announced, and nothing on the page supports it. It is the ninth rule.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. From the first page of 11 I could not have stopped. I would have put it down once, in 6.

**Press it on a friend?** On the friend who loved The Good Place, yes. On the friend who reads Crouch for speed, no, not until the middle moves.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch as a thriller; above him at the choice.**

- **Level: speed into the premise.** Dark Matter has its man in the wrong world by the end of its second chapter. This book has "'Come back across,' her mouth said. Maylis hadn't said it." at the end of 2, and a dated clock by the end of 3, read off a file: "That village hadn't had its flood yet." That is his pace and his method.
- **Below: what happens after the first turn.** Crouch multiplies in the last third: one Jason becomes a hundred, one false memory becomes the end of the world. Here the premise turns once (the helper is the cause) and then narrows to one gate on one night whose minutes the reader already has. The surprises left are of character, not of premise. They are good ones, but a Crouch reader will feel the book stop getting bigger at chapter 8.
- **Below: fear for the marriage.** Daniela lives with the wrong Jason for a month and does not know. Coralie knows in under a minute ("Who is that?", 3), has it in two boxes by breakfast, and by 8 is holding the impostor's wrist flat on the table. What replaces the fear is better as character (she prefers the impostor: "I'd have this one again.") but it lasts three chapters, and Lesca never takes anything that cannot be taken back.
- **Above: the choice.** Dark Matter lets love choose and ratifies it. The Good Place plays the trolley problem as a lecture and a gag. This book makes a woman stand between the lever and the man, gives her a reason no reader can respect and every reader recognises ("She wanted it not to be her."), and kills someone with it. Neither comparison does anything that hard.
- **Level with The Good Place: the reversal's design.** "It comes because no—" sticking in Lesca's mouth in 4 re-reads correctly once 8 arrives, and Bordenave's cross against Friday the 4th proves the reversal and loses the vote with the same fact.

**Where the reader is afraid:** on the stairs in 5; at Coralie's thumb over the doctor's number in 6; at her third step on the landing in 7; through the whole of 11 after dusk; at the fire door in 13 ("Each time she came back in, she looked down the corridor before she looked for a chair."). **Where the reader is not afraid:** for Lesca, anywhere; for the Port as a whole; for anyone in 9, 10 or 15.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The opening gives away the secret, and the book then reveals it three times. (00, 3, 6, 9, 10, 14)**
"She looked down that track while the wipers crossed the windscreen twice. Then she took out her phone and rang a number that was already in it." By the stone in 3 any reader knows she looked, knew and rang. So Maïté's "Did you know he was out?" (3) is not a question, Larrère's cut-off "When you stood on that platform, did you—" (6) is not suspense, the full replay in 9 is confirmation ("Only the priest knew she had looked down the track first" is false: the reader has known for nine chapters), Coralie's "2:51" (10) confirms a confirmation, and the square in 14 hears what the reader heard on page one. The text stages a ladder of disclosures on a fact it disclosed before chapter 1.
*Do:* end the opening on "She took the handle out of its box. She held it for three breaths." Cut the turn of the van, the look and the call. Then "No one came to tell him" reads as true in 3 and as a lie on re-reading, which is the comparison's own method; 9 becomes the reveal it is written as; 10 becomes a second blow (somebody else knew); 14 pays in public. No new writing is needed.

**2. The impostor never wins a round, and the rivalry is over by chapter 8. (5, 7, 8)**
"The pulling stopped. Lesca was gone, and Inguma with her." (5). "'I do this all week,' she said." (8). Every contest between the two women is stopped by a referee: Inguma's timetable on the stairs, Coralie's judgment on the landing, Coralie's two hands in the kitchen. Lesca gets one bowl of soup. The thing Maylis is to fear on Friday, "a whole life with one hand", is then itemised in a speech (8) because it was never shown. From 9 on, Lesca is weather.
*Do:* let her take one thing that stays taken. Let Monday's count run until morning, so that she spends Monday night in the bed beside Coralie with Maylis awake in the right hand. Nothing need happen but two fingers on a wrist. Coralie's refusal on the landing in 7 then weighs more, because she knows what she has already allowed, and Friday has something behind it.

**3. The rule the final night stands on is announced, not earned. (8)**
"'One night matters. If I'm in your village at 2:51 on Friday night, your bank goes. If I'm not, it holds.' Nothing had stopped a word of that." The evidence on the page says the opposite: the river catches up the day she is back. The rule is certified only by another rule, that nobody can lie standing in the steel room. That device is used seven times. It is strong when it exposes a motive ("To stay," she said. "That's the one that comes out.") and a cheat when it certifies mechanics. This is the rule that gives Maylis a third option, staying awake, and so produces the "Either" at 5 p.m.; it carries the tragedy and has the least under it.
*Do:* keep the truth room for motives only. Let Maylis reach the one-night idea herself, from Lesca's file: the bank started to go at 2:50 and the thing comes at 2:51. Let her say it as a hope. Then her bet is hers, and wrong in a way the reader watched her build.

**4. Lesca talks her way out of the hall of the dead. (12)**
"'The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies,' she said, in the voice she used for the prefecture. 'She has a ceremony at 11, and she will not be taking this seat.'" The death rule is stated twice ("The one it cannot take home dies in her own bed", 2; "That's where I sit if I stay with you", 8) and shown with her name on a card. When it falls due she declines it with a joke, walks down an aisle, and is carried home. Holding the wheel "for a whole ring" costs her a hospital corridor. A rule of death that can be refused by procedure was never a threat, and the reader learns this at the worst moment.
*Do:* she sits. Her fingers lock on the chair (already there) and she cannot open them. What opens them is the thing planted at the end of 10: "I'm ringing the hospital in Bayonne." Two fingers on her wrist, from a woman who came in at 2:55 as she said she would. Then the landing saves her life, and she owes it.

**5. The clock stops for two chapters on the eve of the night. (9, 10)**
"He read it to her off his sheet, from the anthem to the photograph." (10). Chapter 9 replays 2014; the first half of 10 is a ceremony rehearsal with tape strips and a swept doorstep, while the reader's mind is on a river over the line in the other village. Two consecutive chapters look back twelve years with the present set to "tonight".
*Do:* open 10 on "You left this on the kitchen table" and give the chapter to Coralie and the speech. Keep Arlette's visit to ten lines. Move the rehearsal to the Wednesday of chapter 6, or cut it to the eight strips and "The others have died since then, in those beds, of the things people die of." Cut 9's replay to what is new: the reflector, Coralie's car in the yard, the kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice.

**6. Chapter 6 is the longest stretch of the first half and buys one line. (6)**
"The gendarmes had put a whole night into three lines. Not one of the three said whether anybody had gone down that track." Maylis crosses to read a page the reader has already read in 3. Its one new fact, "Trapdoor locked on the inside", is what sends her down the track at 02:57, and it is passed over on the way to a council farce and an eleven-minute speech. This is where the Crouch reader puts the book down.
*Do:* cut a third: the mower, half of Larrère's speech, the explanation of Marie-Jo's sash. Make the locked trapdoor the turn of the file scene and let her react to it. Keep the back-lane line, the two hands voting, and the cut to Coralie with the phone, which is the only fear in the chapter and is eight lines long.

**7. After "Forty-one" the reader is kept out of the flooded village for two chapters, and one of them has no risk in it. (15)**
"'Against.' No hand went up." Chapter 14 earns its place; it is Lesca's own climax. Chapter 15 is four codas with every outcome known before it starts, and it runs to Tuesday before 16 goes back to Sunday.
*Do:* fold Maïté's verb and Larrère at the gate into the evening of 14. Move the council vote and "Good night, Maylis" into 16, after the car door shuts on the pillow and before the last meeting in the steel room, so that "Across the landing. Four steps. Yours?" / "She took the pillow." answers a scene the reader has just left.

**8. Her isolation on the night is done in one sentence. (11)**
"She had tried the café first, but the nine who had signed on Tuesday had watched Jeannot's hand go up. A vote was a vote." Nine people who live under a leaking bank, with an orange warning out and water on the gendarme's step, will not walk down a track and knock, and the refusal happens offstage. The same sentence is the last chance to give the Port faces before it floods.
*Do:* play it, in fifteen lines, at the counter: she asks, Bordenave turns the pad to the cross, Madame Darrigrand with the baby says why not. The hole closes, the Port gets two more faces, and Darrigrand's "You had the key" in 16 comes from someone the reader heard refuse.

**9. The 5 p.m. platform does not say what she is betting on. (11)**
"'Either,' Maylis said. 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said." Then: "He was off the platform and down the steps before she could take it back." She has all the time there is to take it back; she lifts the handle off herself. The scene is the real point of no return, and it reads as the author needing the gate shut, because her other plan (stay awake till 4 and have neither flood nor broken order) is three chapters back.
*Do:* one line of her bet, on the platform, before the word. And cut "before she could take it back": she chooses to unhook the handle.

**10. Line-level habits that leak tension. (2, 3, 4, 5, 12)**
"Call her Lesca." (3): the only time the narrator speaks to the reader, at the moment of highest curiosity. The same closing move ends two Maylis chapters running: "Maylis hadn't said it." (2), "She hadn't told them to." (4). Tail sentences explain what the scene showed: "It was the only word she had." (5), "Nobody in Peyrebarthe had ever heard Maylis's mouth forbid anything." (5), "This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel." (12).
*Do:* open 3 in the bed with the two pillows and let "Madame le maire" name her. End 4 on the kitchen. Cut the three tails.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The owner keeps her right hand.** Every physical scene comes from it: the stair post, the coffee tap, the two hands voting, the handbrake.
2. **Bordenave's pad and the cross against Friday the 4th.** One fact proves she is the water and costs her the vote. "If she can be out on Friday the 4th, she can be out on Friday the 11th."
3. **The dip.** Jeannot's planted "You'd sit there till Easter", the van on its belly, and "She wanted it not to be her. Maylis turned her back on the lamp and went on."
4. **Kévin's fingers.** "Five," in the headlights with a woman's chair half up, and "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much."
5. **The corridor in 13, to the last word.** "Did she answer you? Through the door?" / "Coralie read out the next name." And "Forty-one."
