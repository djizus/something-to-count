## 2. Against the comparisons

**Characters: above Crouch, a shade under The Good Place. Level with the bar overall.**

- **Above Crouch on the double.** Jason2 in Dark Matter is Jason with a different CV. Lesca is a different method: "A meeting needs somebody in the chair. Since neither of us is in it, I'll chair from here." Maylis thinks in job sheets ("Twenty-five minutes was a trailer of leaves to the dump and back"), Lesca in motions. Nobody could swap a line between them.
- **Above Crouch on the wife.** Daniela is what the Jasons fight over. Coralie has an ethic and a want of her own, and she is the one who ends the triangle, by pinning a wrist: "I do this all week."
- **Level with The Good Place on the ensemble.** Each is wrong in a way that is both their joke and a cause of the disaster: Maylis's Either, Larrère's gap in the window, Jeannot's contrariness ("I'm not coming up out of it for a number"), Sarthou's file. Jeannot is still wrong on the last page ("I was in no danger, and I'll say so to anyone"), which is the book's nerve.
- **Below The Good Place in two places.** Its people change one another by arguing; here the two selves hardly talk, and a room that stops lies does Lesca's changing for her. And its examiner is examined: Chidi is as wrong as Eleanor. Coralie never is.

Direct answers to the lens:

- **Distinct, wanted-for, alive?** Both, yes. Lesca is the louder; Maylis is alive mainly in chapters 4, 6, 11, 13 and 16, and is a hand and a jaw in 5 and 7.
- **Softened or excused?** Lesca is not: "I wouldn't have stopped." Maylis is, twice over: by the narrator early (fault 5) and by the village late (fault 2).
- **Seen from outside as a woman?** Neither lead. Maïté is (fault 7).
- **Who would be missed?** Jeannot first, then Madame Etcheto, Kévin, Larrère. Not Bordenave, the dealer or Josiane apart from one another.
- **Which relationship carries it, and does it pay?** Maylis and Coralie, on one question asked for sixteen years. It pays in chapter 16. Lesca and her own Coralie pay in one scene (10) and one line (15).
- **Does the reader care who lives?** For Jeannot and Coralie, yes, to the point of reading chapter 13 without stopping. For Lesca at the table of the dead, less than the book needs (fault 1).

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. Lesca's last crossing has lost its object. (8, 10, 12) Structural.**
By Friday she has heard Coralie refuse her ("You'll go back down those stairs", then "a river over forty-two beds"), she knows she is the flood ("It comes with me"), her mother has told her how to stay home ("Sleep on your side, Maylis. Tonight. With your knees up"), and her own Coralie has just opened twelve years to her. The book answers all four in narration: "This Coralie knew. She was four steps away with her door open, but Lesca still meant to go." A woman famous for a sum (forty houses against one man) never does this one: a bed that has said no, against forty houses and forty-two beds. She reads as obtuse where she should be tragic, and the reader stops caring whether she ends at the bingo table.
*Do:* make the end of chapter 10 a decision the reader watches. She lies down on her side with her knees up, door open, her Coralie's door open across the landing. Before 2:51 she turns onto her back. No comment from the narrator. Cut the paragraph that recaps the café.

**2. The cost is shared out until Maylis's part is small. (13, 15, 16) Structural.**
One death: a woman of 94 who insisted on her room, whom Coralie would not wake ("That part is mine"), under Larrère's order, delayed by Jeannot's refusal, and claimed outright by Lesca ("It was you." "Yes."). Then chapter 16 hands Maylis five kindnesses against two blows: Sarthou ("I do not record that I would have stayed"), Kévin's tow, Marie-Jo's chair, Jeannot's letter and shovel, Coralie's bandage. The central choice costs her a job and a pillow; nobody she owes ever stands in front of her.
*Do:* give the cost a claimant. Replace the priest at the cemetery gate with Madame Etcheto's daughter, who has heard the café's version and asks who is digging. Cut Sarthou's aside and Marie-Jo's chair. Leave Jeannot's letter as the only defence; it is worth more alone.

**3. Coralie is the examiner who is never examined. (4, 7, 8, 16)**
"I've asked you one question for sixteen years, and drawn you boxes for it." She has also wanted a floor in Bayonne since she was nineteen and kept the form behind the fruit bowl since March without a word. Maylis notices and uses it only as a reason to give way: "She had waited since March to tell anybody." That is Coralie's own Either, and no one says so. She is right in every scene, which makes her the book's judge and costs her some life.
*Do:* in the chapter 16 kitchen, let Maylis say it once, flatly: nine months behind the fruit bowl, and no box drawn for that. Coralie's answer would be the line she does not yet have.

**4. The steel room tells Lesca's truth for her. (4, 8, 12)**
"'I w—' Lesca's fist closed." "'To stay,' she said. 'That's the one that comes out.'" Three stuck sentences in chapter 8 alone. Her self-knowledge arrives by mechanism, so the only truth she chooses is in the square, and by then the reader has heard a machine say it for her.
*Do:* keep the discovery ("It's Saturday night"), the one stuck sentence that matters ("to get—" / "To stay") and the last ("I won't." "It came out whole."). Cut "It's the ti—" and "I w—".

**5. Maylis's silences come with a kind reason attached. (1, 8)**
"But Coralie had to be up before 6, so Maylis didn't ask." "But the duck meant an hour at the stove for Coralie." "But Larrère paid her wages, so she didn't knock on the glass." "But Coralie had wanted that floor since she was nineteen." Each Either is explained as consideration before the reader can suspect it of being fear. Jeannot is the first to say what it is, in chapter 6: "That's how Larrère asks for a thing."
*Do:* cut the reason from two of the three in chapter 1. Let one early Either be plain cowardice, so the one on the platform is the worst and not the first.

**6. The mechanism silences Maylis where the silence should be hers. (7)**
On the landing, with Coralie two steps from the bed: "Maylis had used herself up at the bar. She couldn't get one word out to say who was really asking." The scene is about a woman who will not ask, and the plot takes her mouth away so that she cannot. It excuses her at the exact moment the book exists to accuse her.
*Do:* leave her the mouth. Lesca lets go of the jaw, as she does in the kitchen next morning. Maylis has it for the whole of Coralie's wait and uses it for nothing. The wrist still pulls.

**7. Maïté is props and broken sentences. (4, 6, 15, 16)**
"'That's winter things,' she said. 'In the— It's blankets.'" Two bowls, a folder, a suitcase, a black coat, a blue dressing gown. She is wife, widow or mistress in every scene, and her one want of her own (the verb) is about her husband's stone. The stutter stands in for a person.
*Do:* one finished sentence that is hers and not about a man. The bus is the place: she is short of the fare and says where she is going and why, to the driver, who does not care.

**8. Maylis's mother disappears. (8, then nothing)**
"She hasn't got stamps, she's got the count." One scene, the liveliest minor voice on Maylis's side, and she lives across the yard. After the flood, after the car door shuts on a pillow, she is not there. Lesca's mother gets three scenes and "I'm not asking you why."
*Do:* put her in chapter 16. She is the one in the yard when Coralie's car leaves, with a bucket, and she says something about ponies.

**9. Lesca's turn in the van is argued, not felt. (12)**
"Twelve years ago she had let his phone ring once and then done what she had come to do. This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel." The paragraph before it has already shown the reflector "no nearer than it had been twelve years ago". The narrator draws the parallel for her and she lets go as a conclusion.
*Do:* cut those two sentences and "She had told Maylis there was no both. She had wanted both herself, the bed and Castagnet." Go from the reflector to "I'm letting go."

**10. The café is one voice in three bodies. (5, 7, 16)**
"'She's got a mayor in her,' the dealer said." Bordenave has a pad and a cellar; Josiane has one theory carried through four chapters; the dealer has no name, no house and no stake, and is given the cruellest line in the book: "Had the key and went for a walk, did you?"
*Do:* name him and put his house in the Port, so the line comes from a man with mud on his wallpaper.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The owner keeps her right hand.** The stair post, the tipped spoon, the tap, the handbrake. It is the two women's whole argument, and it is why "Your hand on the post stopped me" can be said in chapter 16.
2. **Coralie at the oven door, three times.** The third, where she bends to the gap and cannot finish, is the best use of a running joke in the book.
3. **Jeannot whole.** The numbered points, "And six. Bugger off", a letter that "doesn't thank you", and the last word of the book dropping "little".
4. **Either on the platform, "She wanted it not to be her", and the grave.** The line through 11, 13 and 16. Nothing softens it; do not let anything.
5. **Lesca's Coralie.** "Because you'd have told me." And "Good night, Maylis."
