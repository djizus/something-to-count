# Critic's report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.5.** "'Then which do you want?' Behind Coralie somebody was calling a name. 'The gate, or Castagnet? Say which, Maylis. For once.'" (13). The house, the man and the forty-two beds are all on one phone call. Before chapter 9 the stakes are a date in a file.
- **Tension: 7.** "It was an hour and nine minutes short of 4." (11). From there to "Forty-one" I did not look up. Chapters 8 and 10 run slack.
- **Progression: 6.5.** "Maylis had two nights behind her. She meant to have a third before she asked Coralie anything." (7). From the end of 6 to the end of 8 the same two positions are restated: keep her out, can't; ask me, can't.
- **Comedy: 7.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (6). Chapters 1, 2 and 4 to 7 are at the level of the comparison. I stopped smiling in the second half of 3, the first third of 8, most of 10, and all of 14 and 15, and two comic scenes fall short of their own premise.
- **Characters: 7.5.** "'Eleven years I've been timed like an egg.'" (6). The village is an 8. The two leads are funny for one chapter each and Coralie is funny for one chapter in sixteen.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" (16). The joke that cannot be finished is the cleanest switch in the book.
- **Prose: 7.** "She had worked the roadsides out at the speed of the village tractor. Inguma was doing them on foot." (6). Deadpan that makes the joke by measuring. The same prose stops to explain itself too often (see clarity).
- **The ending: 8.** "'It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you.'" (16). The smile comes back on the last page and it has been paid for.
- **Clarity: 6.** "Inguma had gone to count the corn, so nothing would take Lesca home tonight." (7). A farce that needs a sentence of rules after its punchline is carrying too many rules. I count eight by chapter 8.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes.
**Would I press it on a friend?** Yes, on chapters 5 to 7 and the last page, and I would tell them that chapter 3 is the toll.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Against The Good Place: below, by one point.**

- *Level:* the joke is the ethics. Chidi's indecision is the running gag and the reason he is damned. Maylis's "Either" is the same move, done as well: it is a shopping-pad joke in 1, it sticks in her throat in 4, it shuts the gate in 11 ("'Either,' Maylis said. 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said"), and it is the verdict in 16 ("That was Either, with the river in it"). The stone's missing verb does the trolley problem as a committee item ("It reads that she opened him") without making it small.
- *Below:* in The Good Place the four leads are each funny on their own initiative in every scene. Here the comedy is delegated to the village. Maylis's wit is in the narration and never in her mouth; Lesca's is one register; Coralie's is switched off after chapter 1 and switched on twice more for the oven door.
- *Below:* The Good Place keeps its comic engine running into its darkest episodes, and the engine is what delivers the blow. Here the engine (the café, the pad, Josiane's theory) is parked after chapter 8 and returns for four lines in 16. The catastrophe is delivered by a blackmail folder and a stuck van, neither of which was ever funny.
- *Below:* the reversal. "You're the water" re-reads the rain, the way "this is the Bad Place" re-reads a season. But the season was re-read through things the viewer had laughed at. The rain was only weather, and the proof arrives afterwards as a chart with three columns.

**Against Crouch: above for wit, below for fear.** The other self who wants the wife is Dark Matter's threat. Here it is a tipped spoon and a hand locked on a stair post, which is better company than anything in Crouch and frightens less. The chapter ends ("Are you coming up?", "The arms of the chair were warm", "Forty-one") are at his level.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The chorus goes home before the last act (11, and 9 to 15).**
"She had tried the café first, but the nine who had signed on Tuesday had watched Jeannot's hand go up. A vote was a vote."
The scene in which the village refuses her is two sentences of summary. The café, Bordenave's pad, the dealer and Josiane's adultery theory are the comic engine of chapters 1 to 8, and none of them is on the page between 8 and the middle of 16. So the farce and the flood run on separate rails: the village's misreading costs Maylis embarrassment and never costs her a pair of hands.
*Do:* play it. Friday, noon, the café. She asks for two men at the gate for one night. Bordenave consults the pad: a cross on the 4th, right on the 8th, one for one. The dealer says a vote is a vote. Josiane will not have any man walk down to Castagnet's hut in the dark with the woman who is after him, and will not have her go down alone either. The joke they have been telling about her for two weeks is why nobody comes. Jeannot at the trapdoor already works this way ("You were told the way to my kitchen as well"), so it is in the book's grain. Pay for the space by cutting Larrère's second folder speech in 11 ("I have a letter in here in your handwriting..."), which repeats chapter 6.

**2. Coralie has one scene and plays it eight times (1, 4, 7, 8, 9, 11, 13, 16).**
"'I've had sixteen years of her saying nothing, and I know it when I've got hold of it.'" (7)
"Tick one." "You'll have to ask." "I'm asking where I am in this." "I asked where you'd be." "Keep asking me things." "Say which, Maylis. For once." "What do you want, Maylis?" Each is the same examination, and she announces the book's thesis each time: "Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer" (1), "I've waited a long time to be asked for one thing in this house" (4), "I've had sixteen and a shopping pad" (5), "I've asked you one question for sixteen years" (16). The woman who does Larrère through an oven door is the best company in the book and from chapter 4 she is an examiner.
*Do:* keep the thesis in her mouth twice, in 1 and in 16, and cut the rest. In the middle chapters let her be funny about something that is not Maylis's silence. Chapter 9 shows how: "'I'll have to ask you everything like that.'" The joke carries the wound without stating it.

**3. Maylis is funny in the narration and mute in the dialogue (throughout).**
"Twenty-five minutes was a trailer of leaves to the dump and back. Ten was the time it took to hook the trailer up." (7)
Her comic voice is the workman's measure: the file "thinner than the manual for the village mower", Coralie's bag ("She took more than that to the dump in a morning"). She never says one of these aloud. Her spoken jokes in sixteen chapters are "Put it in forty-two" and "I go in at the front, Guy". In chapter 6, in a body nobody expects silence from, she is the funniest person in the room, and then it stops.
*Do:* let her say the measures to the people who would take them: Jeannot, Kévin, Bordenave. She can be exact about leaves and dumb about wants. It sharpens "Either" instead of blunting it, because the reader hears what she can say.

**4. The best farce situation in the premise gets one bowl of soup (6).**
"'Do you know what I'd give for a woman at this table who sat down when she was told? I'd carry my pillow back across that landing tonight.' / For sixteen years Maylis had sat down and said nothing, in the wrong kitchen."
Each woman is the wife the other one's Coralie wants. The book states this in one line of narration and moves on to the council. Lesca gets a whole day of temptation in 5 ("I'd have this one again"); Maylis gets half a page.
*Do:* no new crossing. Lengthen this meal. Let Lesca's Coralie test the obedient mayor through the whole of it (sit, eat, pass that, not a word about the committee) while the right hand goes for the keys again and again, and let Maylis find out how easy it would be to stay. Then "I won't pay that" in 8 is said by someone who has been offered the same bargain.

**5. The reversal is proved by a chart, and a hole is patched in dialogue (8).**
"'Keeping me out for a night or two changes nothing, because the river catches up the day I'm back. You saw it do that on Saturday. One night matters.'"
This is the author closing the question "why not just keep her out?" through Lesca's mouth. The scene before it lays out "You're the water" in three ruled columns, *What. Since when. How do you know.* In two pages that kitchen scene goes from Coralie flirting with the visitor, to a lecture, to "one nose between them", to coffee poured down the sink.
*Do:* make the evidence something already laughed at. Bordenave's cross is that evidence and it gets six lines in 7. Give the café its triumph over the mayor who was thirty centimetres out, let Maylis be the only one in the room who sees what the dry boards mean, and cut the chart to Coralie's one good line: "'A tide comes in twice a day. It doesn't take Thursday off.'" Cut "One night matters" to what Lesca cannot say standing up.

**6. The association meets through a car window and the scene does nothing with it (8).**
"Larrère, its president, chaired them from the driver's seat of his car. He had parked it at the open door with the engine running."
Two lines of premise ("'I'm heating the whole square'"), then a sheet of paper against the glass and a speech. Coralie's oven version that evening ("'The soup will be consulted'") is funnier than the meeting it mocks.
*Do:* run the procedure through the gap first: members repeating themselves towards the door, the hands counted from the driver's seat, somebody sent out in the rain to report a show of three. Then hold the sheet with Kévin's name to the glass. At present the cold arrives before the scene has got warm.

**7. The landing speech is a CV (7).**
"'I held my first hand on that floor, at nineteen, under a night nurse called Ithurbide. She ran thirty beds with a torch and a list, and I wanted to be her before I'd heard of Peyrebarthe. I've had the form since March. I posted it today, and I posted it for me.'"
The bingo, the best set piece in the book, ends on backstory recited at midnight to a stranger in her partner's body. It also makes the envelope in 16 a formality.
*Do:* on the landing she says only that she posted a form today, and that this one may ask what for. Lesca asks. That is the comedy of the woman who answers, and it hurts Maylis in the wrist more. Put Ithurbide, the torch and the list in the corridor in chapter 2, as something Coralie is funny about while she waits for Madame Etcheto to finish.

**8. The man from Pau is a clipboard (10, 14).**
"He looked at his sheet. He left the kiss on it."
He has four beats in 10 and each is "he consulted his sheet". The woman on the phone at Hontaube is a person in eight lines ("She said it kindly"). He stands on the stage at the book's public climax and has the best exit line in it ("'I've been asked to ask. Whether the prefect might have the medal back, until things are clearer'"), and it comes from nobody.
*Do:* give him one want in 10. It is his first ceremony and it must run to time; or he has been warned about this mayor. One opinion of his own and the medal line lands twice as hard.

**9. The ending states its moral five times (13, 16).**
"'I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me.'" (16, to Kévin)
Chapter 13 has already said "She wanted it not to be her." In 16 Madame Darrigrand, Sarthou and the dealer each deliver the verdict, Coralie delivers it best, and Maylis then diagnoses herself to Kévin and again to Lesca ("'At the gate I thought I could do better than you'").
*Do:* cut the line to Kévin; his flying needs no reply but "It was five." Keep the dealer's line, which does the village in nine words, and Coralie's. In the steel room keep "Forty-one", "She took the pillow", "Don't come again", "I won't."

**10. Two chapters of people turning away (14, 15).**
"Madame Etcheto kept looking at Lesca until the chair had turned her away." / "He said nothing." / "'Good night, Maylis,' she said, and went into the spare room."
After "Forty-one" the reader is kept out of Maylis's Port for two chapters. Chapter 15 is three short scenes on one note, each ending on an exit, and Lesca hardly speaks in either chapter. The pay-offs are right ("I'll be brief" answered by silence; "Surname first") but nobody is company for twenty pages.
*Do:* run 15 as the tail of 14 at half its length. Keep Etcheto's "Nobody emptied hers", the garden gate, and the last six lines of the council. Cut the council's preamble; the timer wound to nothing does it alone.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The forty-two line, end to end.** "Put it in forty-two" (1), "You're on its second page" (5), the six points at 5 a.m. ending "'And six. Bugger off.'" with "Jeannot had just sworn at her in six points, and he was alive" (9), and No. 42 delivered by hand, with the second shovel (16).
2. **Larrère untimed (6).** "'I have sentences in that register with no ends on them... "A man can no longer." It rang there... I'm told I wave.'" And its crosscut with the pulse going down the pad.
3. **The care plan and the bingo of the dead (2).** "'She reported him. She said he had put it off its count, and it came at 1... Staff to wait. Staff not to join in.'" / "'After that she plays bingo with the rest of the dead.' 'With cards?'"
4. **The oven door, all three times (1, 8, 16),** and the line that follows the third: "'I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it.'"
5. **The mishearing at the bar (7).** "'You're the water.'" / "'She wants water.'" / "'She's got a bar full of coffee.'" / "'How do I put that? It's got no date.'" Through to Madame Etcheto undoing Maylis's plan out of kindness, and "'How's that spelled?'"
