# Critic report: Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. About 42,400 words, about 4,400 sentences.

Counts used below come from the text itself. Mean sentence length is 9.5 words and the median is 8. Half of all sentences are 8 words or fewer. Two in a hundred reach 25 words. 43% of sentences open with She, He, It, The, Then or a character's name.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** "I'd have had you. You answer. But what I'd have had is a river over forty-two beds." (8) The marriage and the flood are made one stake in a single line.
- **Tension: 7.** "She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it broke." (13) Chapters 11 to 13 are at the comparison's level. Chapters 7 and 8 are not: two nights of pushing are told in two paragraphs and then declared void.
- **Progression: 7.** "Keeping me out for a night or two changes nothing, because the river catches up the day I'm back." (8) The dated countdown never slips, but this sentence cancels what chapter 7 spent its first half on.
- **Comedy: 8.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station." (6)
- **Characters: 8.** "I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it." (16)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'It's Saturday.' 'Then they'll want the bins out,' he said." (13) Said in a flooded corridor, and it makes the corridor worse.
- **Prose: 7.** "This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel." (12) The sentence-making is above the bar. The narrator steps in to explain at the two moments where it most needed to stay out, and the rhythm does not vary.
- **The ending: 8.** "I know what you picked. I was the other one." (16)
- **Clarity: 6.** "So that's a fourth point, and I didn't have it on my list." (4) Four rules of the room, the right-hand rule, the counting rule, the hall and the rule that the river travels with the visitor. Each is clear when stated. The book then states them again each time they operate, which reads as doubt that the reader has them.

Would I have finished it unpaid: yes. Would I press it on a friend: yes, on the one who loved The Good Place. To the Crouch reader I would say that it starts working at the end of chapter 3 and to hold on through chapter 8.

## 2. Against the comparisons, for prose

**Level overall. Above Crouch at the sentence, below him in ease, level with The Good Place in the mouths of its people.**

1. **Above Crouch: the images come from one place.** Every comparison is drawn from the job of a woman who cuts roadsides. "Twenty-five minutes was a trailer of leaves to the dump and back. Ten was the time it took to hook the trailer up." Crouch reaches for the nearest cinematic image. This book has perhaps a dozen similes and not one is borrowed.
2. **Below Crouch: he explains his machine once.** The box in Dark Matter and the chair in Recursion each get one scene of explanation, and after that they are only used. Here the rules are given as an agenda in chapter 4 (which is funny) and then re-given in chapters 5, 6, 7, 8 and 11 (which is not). A Crouch page falls down the eye. In chapters 5 and 7 of this book the reader is parsing whose limb is whose.
3. **Level with The Good Place: each person is wrong in their own grammar.** Larrère cannot finish ("So there's the question of the handle. It isn't settled, and you're not to worry about it"), Jeannot numbers, Sarthou does not contract, Maïté breaks off, Marie-Jo addresses a wall. No line needs its tag. Where the series puts its moral finding in somebody else's mouth as a joke, this book does that too (Madame Darrigrand, Coralie) and then has the narrator say it as well.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The two decisions of the climax are explained by the narrator. Chapters 12 and 13, repeated in 16.**

> "She had told Maylis there was no both. She had wanted both herself, the bed and Castagnet. [...] Twelve years ago she had let his phone ring once and then done what she had come to do. This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel." (12)

> "If she turned that handle, Jeannot drowned and her hand had done it. If she went on, whatever the river did behind her was the river's doing. She wanted it not to be her." (13)

For fifteen chapters the narrator reports hands, doors and clocks and lets the reader judge. At the two points the book exists for, it stops and delivers the verdict as an argument. Lesca's release reads as a syllogism she completes. Maylis's choice is diagrammed as a pair of conditionals before she makes it, so that when Madame Darrigrand says "fetching a man out of a dry hole" and Coralie says "I was the other one", the reader has already been told. Then Maylis says it a third time to Kévin: "I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me."

What to do. In 12, end the paragraph at "the reflector was red, and no nearer than it had been twelve years ago." Keep one thought, because it is hers and it is concrete: "the motion she had written at her own kitchen table was for a seat and a bed. There was no man in it." Cut "She knew what that cost. She had seen the card with her name on it", cut "there was no both", cut the steering-wheel aphorism, and go to "I'm letting go." In 13, cut the three sentences. The scene already has the lamp behind her, the reflector in front, three steps back and Coralie's "for once". Let her turn her back on the lamp unexplained. The line to Kévin in 16 then becomes the first time anybody says it, and it lands as a discovery.

**2. The rules are restated every time they are used. Chapters 5 to 8, and 11.**

> "Lesca hadn't finished the words, so Inguma had nothing to count and would take her home." (7)

> "Inguma had gone to count the corn, so nothing would take Lesca home tonight." (7, three paragraphs later)

> "The vote had left the gate shut for Friday afternoon. If the rib let Lesca in on Friday night, the gate would have to come up in the dark, and that job would be Maylis's." (8)

> "If she couldn't keep Lesca out on Friday, she wanted the river let onto the fields in daylight, by order." (8)

Also "A day over there meant giving Inguma a day's counting" (6), "That's a day" (5), "She knew now why Inguma always came at 2:51, first to Lesca and then to her" (9), "She needed a second pair of hands. One woman couldn't raise the floodgate and also get Jeannot up out of his duck hut" (11). Chapter 8 is the worst: the three-column chart, the steel-room speech and the pad list each re-lay ground the reader holds.

What to do. One pass through 5 to 8 that removes every sentence whose only job is to say what a rule does or where the plot stands. The tablecloths lying down again need no gloss. Coralie's chart in 8 is the single recap the book needs, because a character is being told for the first time. Keep that and cut the rest. This is where the middle's drag comes from, and it is a cutting job, not a rebuilding one.

**3. Chapters 9 and 10 each tell the reader what the other one shows.**

> "Lesca had had forty houses to open the gate for. She had also had Coralie's car in that yard. Maylis had been given the whole night, but nothing in it told her which of the two Lesca had turned the handle for." (9)

In the next chapter Coralie says it in her own words, in the best scene of the marriage: "So for twelve years I've had two answers." The narrator has spent it. Then chapter 10 closes with a page that re-narrates chapter 5's café and chapter 8's steel room from Lesca's side:

> "She had stood there smiling at him with Maylis's face, and all she had found to say to him was a bill for a tank of diesel. [...] But on Wednesday, in the steel room, she hadn't been able to finish that sentence. What had come out was that she was going over to stay"

What to do. Cut the paragraph in 9. From the closing page of 10 keep the grey hair ("Her own Castagnet hadn't lived long enough to go grey") and "She had wanted him to go on arguing." Cut everything that recounts a scene the reader watched. Go from "nothing was sitting on it" to those two sentences to Coralie's last line.

**4. The rhythm does not vary, so emphasis stops working. The whole book. Worst in 6 and 8.**

The median sentence is between 7 and 10 words in every numbered chapter. The one-line paragraph is the book's device for weight, and it is used for everything: "So she could cross." "She ticked both." "She liked it." "Inguma got down." "She drove past." "One chair was empty." "It was 4 a.m." By chapter 8 it carries no more weight than a full stop. "Then" opens 121 sentences. Nearly every section closes on a short flat declarative.

The proof that the prose can do otherwise is chapter 14, which has the longest sentences in the book and is its best-written chapter: "She watched her sentence travel back through the square and down the road, row by row, at walking pace." And the walk to the hut in 13, the one place where syntax changes under pressure: "Mud to the ankle at every step. The torch in her left hand, her right arm pressed across her side."

What to do. In each chapter choose the two or three moments that deserve the one-line paragraph and fold the others into the paragraph above. Let one passage per chapter run long where the action is continuous (the gate in 5, the floor of the mayor's office in 6, the track in 7). Remove "Then" wherever the order of events is already plain.

**5. Three places where the narrator leaves its post. Chapters 3, 6 and 13.**

> "The other Maylis Lesca was the one whose village had its flood in 2014 and made her mayor. Call her Lesca." (3)

This is the only sentence in the book addressed to the reader, and it sits on top of a first paragraph that gives away what the chapter then reveals properly, through the stone and the file. In 6 the point of view goes to Coralie three times, with reasoning ("A doctor would put Maylis in an ambulance to Bayonne. And Madame Etcheto had said it was this bed the thing came to"). Nowhere else is the book in her head. In 13 the narrator says "you" twice: "the kind you turn by hand", "the way you carry a child".

What to do. In 3, start on "She lay on her back with rain falling on the roof. It could have been her own bedroom" and simply use the name Lesca from the first sentence that needs one. The reader takes it without being instructed, and "Madame le maire" confirms it within a page. In 6, keep the three inserts, because the cross-cut is what makes that chapter tense, but give only what a chart gives: the hour, the number, the thumb over the doctor's name. Cut Coralie's reasons. In 13, "a siren turned by hand" and "the way a child is carried".

**6. The grammar of the shared body hammers the name and slips once. Chapters 5 and 7.**

> "Lesca unlocked the box with the key from the belt, fitted the handle and began to turn it with the left hand. The chain came up wet. Lesca counted the clicks of the catch as it came." (5)

> "Lesca kept the eyes on it longer than a cut deserved. 'I have that,' Lesca said." (5)

> "Lesca kept the eyes on the patch and didn't answer him. Then she walked her up onto the bank" (5)

The convention is that "she" is Maylis and the driver is always named. It produces "Lesca" 42 times in chapter 5 and 33 times in 7, often as the subject of consecutive sentences. In the third quotation the convention breaks: "she" is Lesca and "her" is Maylis, in a chapter where "she" has meant Maylis on every other line. That is the place I had to stop and re-read.

What to do. Fix the slip. Then relax the rule by one notch: in a paragraph where only Lesca acts, name her once and let "she" carry the rest. Keep the strict form only in sentences where both women move.

**7. Chapter 1 runs the same three-step sentence three times.**

> "But Coralie had to be up before 6, so Maylis didn't ask."

> "But the duck meant an hour at the stove for Coralie, after a day's work. So she wrote *Either*"

> "But Larrère paid her wages, so she didn't knock on the glass."

She wanted, but, so she didn't. It is the character, and saying it is right. Saying it three times in one syntax within a hundred lines turns a trait into a formula before the reader has met her. Across the book ", so" joins 47 sentences and "So" opens 27 more. It is the narrator's standard hinge and it makes motive sound like arithmetic.

What to do. Keep the first, because it sets the trait. Let the pad do the second with no reason given: she wants the duck, she writes *Either*. Keep the third. Elsewhere, cut "so" where the cause is the previous sentence.

**8. The same beat closes chapter 2, closes chapter 4 and opens chapter 5.**

> "Her left hand came across and closed over Coralie's fingers. Maylis hadn't told it to. 'Come back across,' her mouth said. Maylis hadn't said it." (2)

> "Her legs swung out of the bed and carried her down to the kitchen. She hadn't told them to." (4)

> "Maylis's mouth spoke without her." (5)

The body acts and the narrator adds that she did not order it. The first time it is the hook of the book. The second and third are the same hook, and the added sentence explains what "her mouth said" has already done.

What to do. In 2, cut "Maylis hadn't told it to" and keep the last line. End 4 on "carried her down to the kitchen." Open 5 on the speech: "'I want Larrère in my office at 9.'"

**9. Stock gestures that are not refrains.**

The book's refrains pay: 2:51, the two fingers on the wrist (which end on Madame Etcheto's), "I'll be brief", the arms of the chair. Beside them runs a set of fixed epithets that only repeat. The arm "across her side" or "against her side" ten times. "Rubber boots" ten times, usually "in his rubber boots". "Didn't look up" six times. "Kept her fingers where they were" three times, across both Coralies. "In her uniform" seven times. "Went on" twenty-two times. "Still" sixty-seven. "Hand" or "hands" 292 times, once every 145 words, counting the measures ("a hand's width", "a hand of water", "hand over hand", "by hand").

What to do. Keep the hand as a unit for Jeannot's water, where it is his word, and find another measure for the gate, the trapdoor and the door of room twelve. Give Jeannot his boots at first sight, at the hut and in the last line, and nowhere between. Let the rib be felt through what it stops her doing, which the book already does well, and drop the held arm except where somebody looks at it.

**10. Usage and small unclarities.**

- **Dialect.** Spelling and most nouns are British: "torch", "pavement", "windscreen", "rubbish bin", "mum", "bugger off". Against them stand "baby carriage" (16), "truck" (10, 13), "dish towel" (throughout), "grass trimmer" (7, 11). Choose. "Pram" and "lorry" are no harder.
- **Figures.** Pulses are in figures everywhere ("Your pulse is 112", "130", "34") except "'Ninety, and coming down'" (3), in the same chapter as "130".
- **Times.** Bare hours throughout ("At 9", "At 5 she pressed"), except "6:10 a.m." (5) and "It was 4 a.m." (13). Chapter 1 has a dateline and no other chapter does.
- **"Wheel tracks" on "the track".** "She drove in second gear through wheel tracks full of water"; "He knew every wheel track"; "two wheel tracks in it. That's my letter seventeen". The word for the road and the word for the grooves in it are the same word. "Ruts".
- **"Anyone" three times, "anybody" eight.** "No one" is rightly kept for the refrain and "nobody" used everywhere else. Do the same here.
- **"The handkerchief"** (5) arrives with a definite article and no earlier mention.
- **A line I could not read.** "At home she got him a few centimetres at a time." (6) It comes straight after Lesca's wrist twisting under her hand, and for a moment "him" has no owner. It means the car window. Say the window.
- **The first emotion in the book is stated.** "She was scared." (00) Every other sentence on that page is an action. Cut it. "Ten more minutes, she thought" is the fear.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The similes from the job.** "It hit the floor like a full sack dropped off a trailer." "The way mud comes home on a boot." "She read it the way she read a job sheet, for what the job would need." "She took more than that to the dump in a morning." Add none from anywhere else.
2. **The body with the definite article.** "Lesca told the mouth to open. It was stiff, but it was hers to use." "The eyes wouldn't leave him." The names may be thinned (fault 6). The article must stay. It is the book's one invention at the level of grammar and it is why the landing scene in 7 works.
3. **The speech of each villager.** Larrère's unfinished administrative sentences and Coralie's version of them through the oven door, down to the last one, which she cannot finish. Jeannot's "One. Two." Sarthou's "I do not record that I would have stayed." Maïté's "In the— It's blankets."
4. **Room twelve.** "The water was level with the bed. It had been higher. It had left a line on the wallpaper a metre above the pillow." Then the half minute on the wrist, the pencil line, the hatbox, and "Forty-one." Not a word.
5. **The confession and what the square does with it.** "The loudspeaker on the bank road said 'drowned him' after the square had finished hearing it." The deputy turning the sheet over to see if there is more on the back. The man beyond the last loudspeaker who claps. And the naming that pays off after it: "Lesca, Maylis. Surname first", "Good night, Maylis", and Jeannot's last word to her, which for the first time is not "little".
