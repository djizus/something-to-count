# Round 35: Reader

Read 00 to 16 in order, in full, in one sitting. About 42,700 words.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** "It's a whole life with one hand. You eat at my table, and I tip the spoon." I knew what Maylis would lose as exactly as I knew what the Port would lose.
- **Tension: 8.** "The last line on the chart said 36." From chapter 5 I was afraid for a body, a bed and a bank at once. In chapters 1 to 4 it is a 6: once the thing on the wardrobe has rules it is a bus ("showed each of them its back. Then it climbed into the empty chair").
- **Progression: 7.** "'I w—' Lesca's fist closed. 'I can't stand here and say I won't.'" That is the second time in chapter 8 she is asked not to come and cannot answer. The six days before the Friday mark time.
- **Comedy: 8.** "And six. Bugger off." I smiled from the oven door in chapter 1 to that line in chapter 9, with one gap in chapter 8.
- **Characters: 8.** "Because you'd have told me." Coralie, in both villages, is the person I would miss most. Jeannot second.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 9.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" The joke that will not come out is the darkest line in the last chapter.
- **Prose: 7.** "Maylis did worry about it. She had written three times to say so. But Larrère paid her wages, so she didn't knock on the glass." I never read a sentence twice for its grammar. That one shape (she wanted, but, so she didn't) carries nearly every feeling Maylis has, and I heard it by chapter 8.
- **The ending: 8.** "She heard the passage, then the front door, then the car door shutting on a pillow." It costs her the job, the wife and the village's good word, and then gives her one man with a shovel.
- **Clarity: 7.** "And in the hall, when they called twelve, somebody sat down." The worst thing Lesca does reaches me as a riddle. Four other places made me go back (listed in section 3).

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. After chapter 5 I would not have stopped for a meal.

**Would I press it on a friend?** Yes, and I would tell the friend to get as far as the Monday in the café. A book that needs that instruction is short of the bar.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, by a little.** Chapters 9 to 13 are above Crouch. Chapters 1 to 4, 8 and the first half of 10 are below him.

1. **Crouch never makes me work to know where I am or what the rules are.** Here I worked in chapter 1 (the map of the bank and who owns what), in chapter 6 (the complaint and the nets), in chapter 7 (river heights) and in chapter 12 (what the hall of the dead can take from her). None of it needed outside knowledge. All of it needed a second look.
2. **The Good Place reversal is here and it is the right size.** "You're the water" re-reads the rain, the prophecy, the rescue and the motion Lesca wrote in chapter 3. The hall hearing it as thirst is funnier than anything in Crouch. It lands a notch softer than it should because Maylis works it out by subtraction at a marker post and I was a beat behind her.
3. **The choice is better than either comparison's.** One woman turned the handle and drowned a man. The other would not, went for the man, and an old woman drowned in her bed. Neither is let off and nobody says the word trolley. Crouch settles Dark Matter by having the wife choose. This book does not settle it, and chapter 13 is better than any set piece of his I can recall.

What I will remember in a month: the thing with leaves and rivets turning its back; Larrère through the oven door; the car window going "all the way down, into the door"; "And six. Bugger off."; "It rang once" against nine rings; Coralie saying "Because you'd have told me"; Kévin counting five in the headlights and later saying "I wanted six"; "Forty-one"; "Had the key and went for a walk, did you?"

Where I could not stop: the end of 2 into 3; all of 5, 6 and 7; then 9 to 13 without a break.

Where I was bored or skimming: the square in chapter 1 between the wardrobe and the eggs; the middle of chapter 8; the first half of chapter 10; chapter 15.

What I still do not understand at the end: why Lesca could get up from the seat with her name on it; who Maïté's suitcase was packed for; why Maylis said Either at 5 p.m. with the handle on.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. Chapter 11. The hinge of the book goes by in one word, and I did not believe it.**
"The river went by under the boards. 'Either,' Maylis said."
She has the handle fitted, daylight, Jeannot above ground behind her. On Tuesday she said "Open" twice at the counter. An hour ago she asked Coralie back. Then she says Either, and after he has gone down the steps she takes the handle off herself ("She lifted the handle off"). Later in the same chapter: "There was still time to go down the track and knock. She didn't go." Twice she does not act and twice I am not told why. Everything after it (the bank, room twelve, the pillow in the car) depends on this moment, and I read it as the story needing the gate shut. At the dip in chapter 13 the same kind of moment gets "She wanted it not to be her", and that is the best sentence in the book.
*What I would do:* give 5 p.m. its sentence too. Chapter 1 told me what Either is: she hands the choice to whoever would pay for it ("the duck meant an hour at the stove for Coralie"). Here the one who pays is Jeannot, and he is standing in front of her asking. Say so in a line. Then one line for why her hand lifts the handle off once he has gone, and one at 1:12 for why she stays in the van.

**2. Chapter 8. Seven scenes, and the same scene twice.**
"'Then stay in your own bed on Friday,' Maylis said. Lesca opened her mouth to answer. Nothing came out of it." Two thousand words later: "So I'm asking. Don't come on Friday." "I w—"
This is the one place I could have put the book down for the night. Kitchen, steel room, the river at the line, the vote, the yard, the oven, the list, steel room again. The second steel room has everything that matters ("To stay", the life with one hand, "Both", the card). The first has one rule.
*What I would do:* one steel-room scene, the Wednesday one, with the rule about 2:51 moved into it. That takes a scene out of the chapter and makes Lesca's silence happen once.

**3. Chapter 12. I did not know what the hall could take from her, so her walking out cost nothing.**
"'The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies,' she said, in the voice she used for the prefecture. 'She has a ceremony at 11, and she will not be taking this seat.'"
Chapter 8 told me the seat was the price of staying ("That's where I sit if I stay with you"). Here she sits in it, declines it and leaves, and the line is funny. The price comes at the top of chapter 14 ("'Twelve. One, two.' Somebody pulled a chair in to a table") and is explained in chapter 15 to a woman in a wheelchair. At the climax of Lesca's story I thought she had got off free.
*What I would do:* move those two lines from the top of 14 to the end of 12, after the lights go out. Then I go into chapter 13 knowing twelve has been called, and every time Nadine or Coralie says "twelve" it lands.

**4. Chapters 4 to 9. Coralie asks, Maylis gives a measurement, Coralie leaves the room. Seven times.**
Chapter 8: "I know where I am. I'm asking where I am in this." Chapter 9: "'I asked where you'd be,' Coralie said. She went up to the spare room."
She tears the page off (4), takes her keys (5), turns to the wall (6), takes the pillow (7), pours the coffee down the sink (8), leaves the pencil across the pad (8), goes up to the spare room (9). By chapter 8 I could see each exit coming, and the last two are the same complaint in nearly the same words a day apart. By chapter 16 I thought she was right to go, which is a smaller feeling than the one the pillow in the car is reaching for.
*What I would do:* cut the reprise in chapter 9. End that scene on her hand holding the rib while Maylis laughs. It is the only time after chapter 1 that I saw the two of them easy together, and it is taken away in eight lines.

**5. Chapter 10, first half. The flood is tonight and I am watching a man stick tape to a stage.**
"'A point of order,' Lesca said. 'You have the flowers as one item and the kiss as the next. They're one item.'"
I skimmed from Arlette's coat to the eighth strip. Arlette repeats her warning from chapter 3. The rehearsal earns its place at one line only: "The others have died since then, in those beds, of the things people die of."
*What I would do:* keep Arlette's "Sleep on your side" and "at my table at 7", cut the rest of her. Start the rehearsal at the front row and get to the eight strips in half the words.

**6. Chapter 7. The biggest turn in the book reaches me as a subtraction.**
"'Fifty-five under,' Lesca said out loud, to the right hand. 'My file has the same.' Maylis read it with her. Yesterday Bordenave had found a metre there, and his cellar had been dry."
Seventy under was promised, a metre was found, fifty-five is in the file. I had to hold three figures and two days to see that the river stops when Lesca is kept out. I got it, a beat late. "She wants water" is only funny if I am ahead of the hall.
*What I would do:* one plain sentence at the marker post in Maylis's words: two nights without her and the road dried, one night with her and it rained by noon. The paragraph of explanation at breakfast in chapter 8 can then be half as long.

**7. Chapter 6. The complaint and the nets: read twice, and it matters.**
"'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" Then, eight lines on: "There are green nets under the floorboards in your hut, folded wet."
In 300 words: a complaint against persons unknown, a gendarme who is a cousin, one man with two offices, her name in a file, a threat built on something I was told once in a list in chapter 4, a poster, a promise for Friday. It is why Jeannot votes shut in chapter 8 ("That's how Larrère asks for a thing"). I only saw that on the way back.
*What I would do:* one sentence, before she crosses to the counter, on what that file does to her, so that I know why she stoops to it.

**8. Chapters 4 and 16. I do not know what Maïté wanted.**
"I saw the wardrobe."
To read that line I needed "a light brown suitcase with both its straps done up" from twelve chapters earlier. I had it, just. I still cannot say whether she was leaving with Larrère, leaving Jeannot, or leaving both. In the other village she gets the best line of chapter 15 (*Left*). Here she gets a bus and "the fare ready in her glove, in coins, but it wasn't enough".
*What I would do:* one line from her, in the kitchen in chapter 4 or on the bus step, that tells me who the suitcase was for.

**9. Chapter 1. The whole map and the whole staff list in one page.**
"The bank was her job too: the care home at one end, then the patch she repaired every spring with four wheelbarrows of clay, then the floodgate." Then the association, the key, the president who is also the mayor, and Jeannot: "One. A duck comes down onto standing water. On Sunday night I had a hand of water on those fields, left there by the last high tide."
I kept bank, gate and hut from the opening and let the rest go. I picked it up again in chapter 5, when Lesca says "This gate is a door in that bank". The postman kept me reading. Jeannot's lesson on water did not.
*What I would do:* cut his "Two" to one line. The reader needs "he drained my water", not the tide table.

**10. Chapter 16. Everything comes back to be signed for.**
"She took the letter and buttoned it into her chest pocket, with the card from the steel room."
The tall L, the tray, the suitcase with two straps, the envelope behind the fruit bowl, the card in the pocket, the three turns of bandage, the eleven stairs, No. 42, the second shovel. I counted more than thirty returns in 2,800 words. Some carry a change (the oven door, the pillow, five fingers, forty-two). The rest only tick, and I began to watch the ticking.
*What I would do:* keep the ones that have changed since I last saw them. Drop the tall L, the tray and the card in the pocket.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 13, first line to last.** The clock on the phone, "a man who weighed nothing", "Then they'll want the bins out", Kévin's five fingers at 4 a.m., the hatbox on the wardrobe, "Forty-one".
2. **Chapter 10, from "You left this on the kitchen table" to the end.** "2:51. I've known that for twelve years." "Because you'd have told me."
3. **The right hand.** The stair post in chapter 5, "That's her. In this hand" in chapter 7, the handbrake and "You'll count the fingers of my right hand. Then you'll take her home" in chapter 12.
4. **Larrère through the oven door, three times,** and the third one she cannot finish.
5. **Jeannot's voice.** The numbered points, "And six. Bugger off", letter No. 42 brought by hand, and the last line of the book.
