# Critic's report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** 'I'd have had you. You answer. But what I'd have had is a river over forty-two beds.' (8)
- **Tension: 7.** 'It was an hour and nine minutes short of 4.' (11)
- **Progression: 6.** 'Then stay in your own bed on Friday,' in chapter 8, is the request of chapter 4 ('Then stop coming') and is made a third time four pages later ('So I'm asking. Don't come on Friday.').
- **Comedy: 7.** An 8 up to the end of chapter 8, a 6 after it. 'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.' (6)
- **Characters: 8.** 'It's four when you're the one counting. When it's your house it's one, and they knock it down.' (10)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** 'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—' (16)
- **Prose: 7.** Deadpan that works, and a ledger that does not: 'The left hand was holding a cup under the tap of the coffee pot. Lesca was still pulling at the jaw and wasn't watching the cup.' (7)
- **The ending: 7.** 'It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you.' (16)
- **Clarity: 6.** 'Ei—' Lesca said. 'You have three minutes.' (5) I could not tell what stopped the word.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes.
**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who likes village comedy, yes. I would not tell someone who loves The Good Place that this is funnier, because after the midpoint it is not.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Crouch: above.** He does not attempt comedy. Nothing in Dark Matter or Recursion is meant to raise a smile, and the ensemble here is the thing this book has that his do not.

**The Good Place: level to the end of chapter 8, below from 9 to 15.**

- **Level: each person is wrong by a rule of their own.** Larrère announces brevity and never signs. Jeannot numbers his grievances. Sarthou speaks in reports and bills the village for his labour. Josiane finds adultery and gets the house right. Daguerre says 'Here'. That is the series' method, and it is done as well here.
- **Level: the gags re-read.** 'Either' is a joke about dinner in chapter 1 and the act that shuts the gate in chapter 11. 'I'll be brief' ends in a man who says nothing (15) and a woman who cannot finish the impression (16). 'Still in the box, were you?' becomes 'Had the key and went for a walk, did you?' The last use changes the first, which is what the series' reversals do.
- **Below: the leads and the argument.** In the series the funniest people are the leads, the funniest material is the ethical argument itself, and the afterlife is the joke. Here the two women stop producing comedy at the midpoint, the sum of forty houses against one man is made funny once, by a man with a broom, and the hall of the dead is played straight.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The premise's comic engine is switched off at the midpoint, and the leads go quiet with it. (Chapters 8 to 16.)**
Four set pieces in four chapters: Lesca loose in Maylis's village (5), Maylis loose in Lesca's (6), one jaw at the bingo bar (7), one nose in the kitchen (8). The last gag of the kind is 'They had one nose between them, so now neither of them could breathe.' After that neither woman is funny again. Lesca's mania for procedure, which made her company in chapter 4 ('Then it's carried, or it's a tie, and in a tie the chair decides.' / 'There's nobody in the chair.'), is absent from both steel-room scenes in chapter 8, which are two straight negotiations over the same request. So 'She didn't call anything to order' in chapter 16 pays off a habit the reader saw in that room once, twelve chapters earlier. Maylis's own register, measuring the world in trailers and wheelbarrows ('Twenty-five minutes was a trailer of leaves to the dump and back'), appears perhaps eight times in the book.
*Do:* merge the two steel-room scenes of chapter 8 into one and run it as a meeting, with items, a motion, and Maylis refusing to second it. Keep 'Nil' as the figure Lesca enters. In chapter 10, let what 'comes home on a boot' leak in public: Lesca at the rehearsal knowing something only the other village knows. Give Maylis her units in every chapter.

**2. After 'I drowned him', the village in Lesca's world goes silent. (Chapters 14 and 15.)**
The book makes its moral problem funny while it is hidden (the stone's verb, the timer, the vote) and stops making it funny at the moment it is out. The reaction is promised and never shown: 'They'll be in my kitchen tomorrow morning with this, the whole of the Port.' Chapter 15 is three scenes in which a comic prop comes back grave: the verb, 'I'll be brief', the timer. It is the one chapter where I did not smile once and was not gripped either. In the series a reveal makes the people funnier, because each takes it wrong in their own way.
*Do:* give chapter 15 one live scene of that village digesting the sentence. The Port coming up her steps, as Arlette said they would, each with the lake or the four houses or the cameras dressed up as this. Or the committee meeting once more on the verb, now that it has one and still cannot cut it. Keep the Etcheto exchange and Larrère's silence at the gate.

**3. The hall of the dead is the funniest idea in the book and is played solemn. (Chapters 8, 12, 14.)**
The set-up is a joke: 'After that she plays bingo with the rest of the dead.' / 'With cards?' Three visits later it has had two: 'A man in the third row had his arm around his card so that his neighbour couldn't copy it', and the woman who 'lifted her handbag off the empty chair and put it on her knees'. Otherwise heads are down and nobody looks up. Lesca's 'You can put that in the minutes as apologies received' shows what the room can do.
*Do:* let the dead be the village, as petty as the living. A disputed line. A caller with Marie-Jo's patience ('I can wait. The duck can't.'). Leave Castagnet exactly as he is: 'He put a grain of corn on a card' must stay the only answer she gets from him.

**4. The narrator explains the gag, before it and after it. (Chapters 5, 7, 8.)**
Physical comedy needs the picture first. Here the cause arrives before the effect: 'Lesca was still pulling at the jaw and wasn't watching the cup. The coffee came up to the top and went over.' And in chapter 5 the strategy is laid out before the stumble: 'One Either, and Lesca could pass for her until noon, when the café would be full.' The best irony at the bingo, an old woman kindly finishing a prayer and wrecking Maylis's plan by it, is bracketed by two sentences of rules: 'Lesca hadn't finished the words, so Inguma had nothing to count and would take her home' and 'Inguma had gone to count the corn, so nothing would take Lesca home tonight.' The body-part ledger ('the left hand', 'the jaw', 'the mouth') makes the reader do accounts in the middle of farce.
*Do:* in every shared-body scene, show what the room sees first (coffee on Josiane's shoes), then whose hand it was, or leave that out. Put the rule once, before the scene, not inside it.

**5. Chapters 15 and 16 close every account. (Chapters 15, 16.)**
In two chapters the verb, 'I'll be brief', the timer, 'surname first', the oven door, the boxes on the pad, forty-two, five fingers, 'Still in the box', 'that house', three turns of bandage, the stacked chairs and the bingo voice are each given a grave twin. By the tenth the reader sees the method, and recognition replaces the smile. The weakest are the ones that exist only to be callbacks: 'That's the mayor's—' She looked down at her register. 'That's the rule.' (15) and 'One,' she said, in the voice she called the numbers in. 'On its own. Sit down on it.' (16)
*Do:* cut those two. Leave one running joke unclosed and still running on the last page.

**6. The sum itself is argued funny once. (Whole book; chapter 8 is the missed place.)**
Forty houses and forty-two beds against one van is the book's trolley. It is skewered by Darrigrand ('It's four when you're the one counting') and by Coralie's 'The others have died since then, in those beds, of the things people die of.' The two women who have to do the sum never have the comic version of the argument. They have this: 'There's no both. You walked it with me, and you did the sum twice on the way back. You turn the handle first.' / 'Both.'
*Do:* in the merged meeting of fault 1, let Lesca put the sum as a motion and Maylis amend it. One rebuild answers faults 1 and 6.

**7. A rule gag whose mechanism I could not read. (Chapter 5.)**
'The mouth stopped on it. It tried again. "Ei—" Lesca said. "You have three minutes."' Chapter 4's rule is that a woman cannot say an untrue thing 'standing up in this room'. This is the square. I did not know whether the rule had followed her out, whether Maylis had blocked the word, or whether Lesca could not bear to say it. A gag the reader has to query is not funny.
*Do:* one clause that gives the stop to Lesca: she gave the word up at thirty-one and it will not come back for her.

**8. One beat too many for Josiane at the bingo. (Chapter 7.)**
'You can go three weeks without bread. Without water it's three days. She's just told him which he is.' This is the author's wit in her mouth. The mishearing has already landed with the sisters ('She wants water.' / 'She's got a bar full of coffee.'), and Josiane has already read the scene as adultery twice in the same hall.
*Do:* cut it. Go from the sisters to Bordenave's 'It's got no date.'

**9. The two-handed vote has to be solved before it can be laughed at. (Chapter 6.)**
'That was two hands for the mayor speaking on the 12th, and only one mayor.' The reader must turn two votes against a motion that the prefect speak alone into two votes for the mayor speaking. It is the best scene in the book and it stumbles on its own punchline.
*Do:* drop the gloss. 'Both hands were up against him. The secretary counted one of them.'

**10. The deadpan button becomes a habit. (Chapter 10, and throughout.)**
The young man from Pau closes three beats in forty lines the same way: 'He left the kiss on it.' 'He marked it.' 'He stopped counting.' The same flat three-or-four-word sentence ends beats everywhere ('The cards went down.' 'The window went up.').
*Do:* keep 'He left the kiss on it' and 'He stopped counting', which is the turn. Cut 'He marked it', and thin the rest to one a scene.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie at the oven door, all three times.** The eggs (1), the soup that 'will be consulted' (8), and the one she cannot finish (16).
2. **The line of 'Either'.** 'I don't cook Either. There's no recipe for it', Sarthou's summons, then the platform: 'Either,' Maylis said. / 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. Then 'That was Either, with the river in it.' A running joke that becomes the fatal act.
3. **The council in chapter 6.** Larrère untimed ('Eleven years I've been timed like an egg… I'm told I wave'), the hand that signs letters on the floor, and the cuts to the pulse chart, which fall in the right places.
4. **Jeannot, entire.** The numbered points, 'And six. Bugger off', 'Forty-two's started. You're on its second page', a letter that 'doesn't thank you', and the second shovel.
5. **Madame Etcheto and the counting.** 'Staff to wait. Staff not to join in.' The roadsides worked out 'at the speed of the village tractor' while 'Inguma was doing them on foot'. 'It was a short count.' And twelve: 'That's my door', then 'Twelve. One, two.' and a chair pulled in to a table.
