# Critic report, round 36: Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. 43,226 words. Counts below are from the text as it stands.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** They are stated in quantities the reader can hold. "The trapdoor was twenty-five minutes away, on foot, in the dark, with the dip under water. The bank had ten." (ch 9)
- **Tension: 7.** Chapters 9 to 13 hold. Chapters 3 and 4 put the threat into summary and procedure. "She turned the page and went on down the dates." (ch 3)
- **Progression: 7.** Every chapter changes the state of play, but the rules of the crossing keep arriving as late as chapter 8, and each arrives as a statement. "So that's a fourth point, and I didn't have it on my list." (ch 4)
- **Comedy: 8.** The jokes are made of the book's own rules and nouns. "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station." (ch 6)
- **Characters: 8.** They are told apart by syntax, without tags. "There's a key, and a handle, and Larrère, and a van. And then there's me, as a distance." (ch 8)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** It happens inside a line and costs neither side. "'Here,' he said. 'I know you are.'" (ch 13, in the flooded corridor)
- **Prose: 7.** Exact, plain, and too often followed by its own explanation. "This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel." (ch 12)
- **The ending: 8.** It closes on an act and leaves the letter unread. "Then he stepped over the string in his rubber boots and began to dig." (ch 16)
- **Clarity: 6.** When two women share a body the grammar makes the reader stop. "By her count he had half a wheelbarrow there. Lesca kept the eyes on the patch and didn't answer him. Then she walked her up onto the bank." (ch 5)

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. Would I press it on a friend? Yes, and I would tell them chapter 3 is the slow one.

## 2. Against the comparisons

For prose: **level overall. Above Crouch line for line, below him on ease. Level with The Good Place on comic lines, below it on how often a rule is said.**

1. **Above: chapter endings.** A Crouch chapter ends on a one-line paragraph that names the threat. Every one of these seventeen chapters ends on an act, an object or a spoken line, and none on a sentence that says what to fear: "It rang once." "LESCA, MAYLIS." "'Forty-one,' Coralie said." "the car door shutting on a pillow" is the same method inside a chapter. The reader is trusted at the exact place Crouch explains.
2. **Above: stakes in the character's own units.** "Twenty-five minutes was a trailer of leaves to the dump and back. Ten was the time it took to hook the trailer up." Crouch states a dilemma in the abstract. This states it as a sum a roadside worker would do, and the sum is the plot.
3. **Below: ease.** A Crouch reader never reads a sentence twice. Here chapters 5, 7, 8 and 12 ask for it, because "the mouth", "her mouth", "the left hand", "her right hand", "Lesca" and "she" all move in one paragraph. And the narrator, having shown a thing, tells it. Fifty sentences of that are the distance between this prose and the bar.
4. **Level, then below: rules as comedy.** The Good Place gives a rule one line and one joke and never repeats it. The meeting in the chair does exactly that ("'Then it's carried, or it's a tie, and in a tie the chair decides.' 'There's nobody in the chair.'"). But the same rules are then restated in narration each time they operate: "Lesca hadn't finished the words, so Inguma had nothing to count and would take her home." (ch 7)

The pattern audit came back nearly clean, which is worth recording. No semicolons. 22 dashes, every one a cut-off speech. Nine colons in narration. Four uses of very/really/quite/rather in the whole book, no "suddenly", three "as if". No narrator triads, no rhetorical questions from the narrator, no announcing sentences. What is left is listed below.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. The two decisions the book exists for are argued by the narrator while they are being taken (ch 12, ch 13)

Chapter 12, eighth ring, van stalled on the slope:

> "She had told Maylis there was no both. She had wanted both herself, the bed and Castagnet. But the motion she had written at her own kitchen table was for a seat and a bed. There was no man in it."
>
> "Twelve years ago she had let his phone ring once and then done what she had come to do. This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel."

Chapter 13, at the dip:

> "If she turned that handle, Jeannot drowned and her hand had done it. If she went on, whatever the river did behind her was the river's doing."

**What is wrong.** These are the two turns the previous eleven chapters built. In both, the action stops and the narrator makes the case, in pluperfect, with the parallel spelled out. The chapter had already shown it: the headlights down the track, the reflector "no nearer than it had been twelve years ago", the ring count. "The same thing, done with a steering wheel" is the reader's thought, taken from them. In chapter 13 the two "If" sentences do the moral arithmetic that chapter 16 then does aloud ("I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me"), so the confession to Kévin arrives as a repeat.

**What I would do.** Chapter 12: go from "the reflector was red, and no nearer than it had been twelve years ago" to "As long as she held the wheel, nobody was going down that track at all" to "'I'm letting go.'" Cut the motion paragraph, cut "This was the same thing", cut "She knew what that cost. She had seen the card with her name on it" (the hall shows the cost thirty lines later), and cut "Inguma hadn't taken her home. She had held the wheel against it for a whole ring, and this was where that put her." Chapter 13: delete the two "If" sentences and keep "She wanted it not to be her." alone. Six words, and chapter 16 becomes the first time the reason is said in full. Also cut "She had told Coralie both. That had been true for as long as the van kept moving." The stuck van already said it.

### 2. The gloss: a shown beat, then a "had" sentence that says what it meant (whole book)

"Had" appears 585 times, 13.5 per thousand words. About 150 sentences open "She had", "Maylis had", "Lesca had". Many carry needed fact. About fifty carry the meaning of the line before. A sample:

- ch 00: "She was scared." (the only named feeling in a page of pure action)
- ch 1: "What she wanted was for Coralie to stay awake and keep her fingers on that wrist. But Coralie had to be up before 6, so Maylis didn't ask." The same wanted / but / so-she-didn't shape comes twice more in the chapter (the duck, the glass).
- ch 4: "It had always come when she needed it. It had never been true, and it wasn't true now. She did want one of them, badly."
- ch 5: "Maylis had stood over that pad every morning for sixteen years and had never got as far as one tick." "Nobody in Peyrebarthe had ever heard Maylis's mouth forbid anything." "Maylis had never smiled at Jeannot Castagnet in her life, and the whole café was watching her do it."
- ch 7: "Inguma had gone to count the corn, so nothing would take Lesca home tonight." Madame Etcheto's "That's till morning" follows and does the job.
- ch 9: "She knew now why Inguma always came at 2:51, first to Lesca and then to her."
- ch 11: "She was awake, and this night was hers." "But she might not get another chance, and that frightened her more than asking did."
- ch 13: "Lesca had let his phone ring once and then turned the handle. Maylis had let it ring nine times, and Jeannot was alive." "She hadn't turned it one centimetre. And Coralie was down there, under the gap."
- ch 14: "It was her ceremony. She had written the order of it herself, and the next item was printed on the programme."
- ch 16: "It was the smallest thing that had happened to her since Friday."

**What is wrong.** Coralie has already named the fault inside the book: "That's Either again, with more words." Each gloss is correct and each one slows the page by a sentence the reader had already supplied. Fifty of them is the difference between prose that is exact and prose that is easy.

**What I would do.** One test for every "had" sentence that follows a beat: does it give a fact the reader lacks, or the meaning of what they just saw? Facts stay ("She had written three times to say so"). Meanings go. Where the gloss is a joke it stays, because the joke is new information: "For sixteen years Maylis had sat down and said nothing, in the wrong kitchen." "She hadn't known whether he had a back lane here. He had."

### 3. The grammar of a shared body is not settled (ch 5, 7, 8, 12)

> "By her count he had half a wheelbarrow there. Lesca kept the eyes on the patch and didn't answer him. Then she walked her up onto the bank, to the platform of the floodgate." (ch 5)

"Her count" is Maylis, "she" is Lesca, "her" is Maylis again. Elsewhere the driven parts take "the" ("The legs stopped inside the door. The eyes had gone to the back of his neck") and elsewhere "her" ("Maylis felt her legs turn towards the café", "Maylis could feel her own face smiling at him", "her mouth hadn't said another word" in the same bingo scene as "The teeth came apart"). To keep the pronouns apart the name is repeated: "Lesca" 42 times in chapter 5's 2,882 words, eight times as the subject of consecutive mechanical steps at the gate ("Lesca unlocked… Lesca counted… Lesca waited… Lesca kept… Lesca tied").

**What is wrong.** This is the book's central device and the reader is given three notations for it. The cost is clarity at the comic high points, where a reread kills the laugh.

**What I would do.** Fix one notation and audit all four chapters against it. Driven parts always take "the". The owner's one hand always takes the possessive. No bare "she" or "her" in a paragraph where both women act. Then cut the step-by-step: the gate test in chapter 5 needs the nineteenth turn and the handle through her fist, and four of its eight "Lesca" sentences can go. Replace "walked her" (seven uses across chapters 5 to 7) after the first.

### 4. Chapter 3 is told, and it opens with the narrator speaking to the reader

> "The other Maylis Lesca was the one whose village had its flood in 2014 and made her mayor. Call her Lesca."

> "Ever since she stood at the stone that morning, she had taken the other village for the lucky one. Its river had stayed behind its bank in 2014, so its Castagnet had lived."

**What is wrong.** "Call her Lesca" is the only time in 43,000 words that the narrator addresses the reader. It is efficient and it is a different voice. The sentence before it gives away, in a relative clause, what the chapter's next two scenes are there to show (the stone, the council table). And the chapter runs at 26 "had" per thousand words, double the book and four times chapter 11. The second woman, her marriage, her rule, her file and the turn that the flood is still to come over there are all delivered in backfill, straight after the best chapter ending so far.

**What I would do.** Keep the naming, lose the summary: open on the body ("She had just woken up in Maylis's bed…") and name her in one bare sentence. Let the stone and Coralie's "Madame le maire" say she is mayor. For the turn, stop at the five file lines and "Today was the 28th." then "That village hadn't had its flood yet." The paragraph between them explains the inference the dates have just made. Cut "It had been needed for Larrère, who was mayor before her": the timer scene shows it within ten lines.

### 5. One sentence length for everything

Mean sentence length is 9.6 words. 1.5% of sentences run past 25 words. Chapter 3, a committee meeting and a file, averages 9.8. Chapter 13, the bank breaking, averages 9.6.

> "The mayor's body wasn't tired. Maylis was the one getting weaker, and she knew why. At home she had left a body on its back with nobody in it, and Madame Etcheto had said what happened to that one." (ch 6)

**What is wrong.** The short declarative is the book's voice and it is right for the flood. But when a council room and a drowning move at the same sentence length, the short sentence has no speed left to give when it is needed. It also makes summary read as a list of equal facts.

**What I would do.** Do not lengthen the action. In the quiet scenes, fold the fact into the act so that some sentences run to twenty-five or thirty words, and the drop to five words in chapters 11 to 13 is felt. Most of the folding falls out of fault 2: where a gloss carries a needed fact, join it to the sentence it follows.

### 6. Scenes open on the clock

Forty sentences begin "At" plus a figure. Eighty-eight begin "Then". Chapter 5's scenes open: "It was 6:10 a.m. on Monday 30 November", "At 8 Lesca walked her down", "At 9 Maylis was standing", "At noon Maylis felt", "At 7 there was soup", "At 9:05 Coralie turned off". Chapters 4, 5, 6 and 10 open on the same two-step: a line of action or speech, then "It was 9 on Tuesday morning, and…".

**What is wrong.** From chapter 11 the clock is the story and "02:59." alone on a line is the best pacing device in the book. Before that it is a duty log, and it spends the device early.

**What I would do.** Keep the figure where a minute matters (2:51, the Friday, the pulse chart). Elsewhere open on the thing happening and let the hour come second or not at all: the soup is at 7 because the pad says so.

### 7. "Hand"

298 uses, one word in every 145. Hands are the mechanism, so most are owed. But the word is also the book's unit of measure, twelve times: "a hand of water", "a hand's width clear of the stone", "open about the width of a hand", "lift the gate a hand". And once it is a watch:

> "Lesca had taken the watch off Maylis's wrist and was holding it in the left hand, out of the rain. […] Lesca waited for the second hand to reach the top." (ch 7)

**What is wrong.** In a chapter where the reader is tracking which hand belongs to whom, "the second hand" reads for a beat as the other hand.

**What I would do.** "A hand of water" is Jeannot's unit and he demonstrates it on the van door, so it stays with him. Change the narrator's other measures: doors open "a crack", the gate lifts "clear of the stone". Change "the second hand" to "waited for the minute to turn".

### 8. Echoes that have become stamps, and props introduced four times

- "each one whole and then in parts": five times (ch 7, 8, 12 twice, 14).
- The sack: "like a full sack dropped off a trailer", "the sound of a sack being dragged", "the sound a full sack makes when it is lowered down a step", "the sound of a sack being lowered down a step", "like a sack".
- "her arm across her side" and variants: ten.
- "in his rubber boots": ten, including Bordenave.
- "a cardboard folder", with the indefinite article, in chapters 4, 6, 8 and 16, for one folder.
- "the white kitchen timer", full epithet, each time.
- Jeannot (ch 9) and Larrère (ch 11) both say of her father, word for word, "he never rang me once".

**What is wrong.** The book's real echoes work because they are rare and exact: the reflector "screwed on at an angle", "The headlights lit up the rain", two fingers on the wrist. The stamps dilute them. The shared sentence makes two enemies sound like one writer.

**What I would do.** After first mention: "the folder", "the timer", "his boots". Keep the boots at the cellar (ch 9), the hall (ch 13) and the last line, where they mean he is alive. Keep "whole and then in parts" in chapter 7 and once in the hall of the dead. Give Larrère his own sentence about the father.

### 9. "It" and "she" with two possible owners

> "Every breath went through the crack in the rib. It pressed down just as hard." (ch 7; "It" is Inguma, the nearest noun is the rib)

> "If it never came, all of this was hers […] she might have said yes to it. But it came with Kévin's cap and one pillow." (ch 6; "it" is Inguma, then the life, then the life again with Inguma's verb)

> "Two words were all Maylis could take before Lesca had the jaw back. By then it was no use to her." (ch 3)

> "At 2:51 on Wednesday the 9th she let Inguma sit" (ch 8; the last woman named is Coralie)

> "'That wasn't for Castagnet,' Madame Etcheto said to Coralie, and sat up over her duck. But she was watching Maylis the way she would have watched a child…" (ch 7; "But" joins nothing)

**What I would do.** Name Inguma whenever another "it" is within two sentences. Chapter 11 already does it right: "Inguma pressed down just as hard."

### 10. Usage that has not been decided

- **Dialect.** British: "pavement", "rubbish bin", "torch", "mum", "bugger off", "strip lights". American: "truck" (three), "dish towel" (seven), "baby carriage" (two), "dump", "grass trimmer". Pick one.
- **"anyone" / "anybody".** The narrator uses "anybody", "somebody", "nobody" throughout, then "anyone who came late" (ch 1) and "before anyone could ask for it" (ch 14). "No one came to tell him" is the fixed phrase and should be the only "one".
- **"the box" / "the steel room".** The village says "the box" and the narrator says "the steel room", except "The door of the box opened" three times (ch 4, ch 8 twice).
- **The measure of the leak.** "as wide as a door" (ch 00, 13), "across the width of a door" (ch 5), "on the width of a door" (ch 6, not English).
- **Sarthou.** "Sarthou, the gendarme" in chapter 5, "Adjudant Sarthou" once in chapter 6, never again.
- **Ages.** "She's ninety-four" (ch 2) and "a woman of 94" (ch 11). The second is a text message and can stay if that is the rule; say so by keeping every other age in words, as now.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The flood night, chapter 9**, from "She turned it faster than it had ever been turned" to "the torch found the sole of a rubber boot". In particular: "Under her feet the river went through the opening with a noise she had never heard it make, and out on the fields the dark ground began to shine."
2. **The sum, chapter 7.** "Twenty-five minutes was a trailer of leaves to the dump and back. Ten was the time it took to hook the trailer up. Maylis did the sum at the water and again at the fence. It came out the same both times."
3. **The apologies, chapter 12.** "'The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies,' she said, in the voice she used for the prefecture. 'She has a ceremony at 11, and she will not be taking this seat.' […] 'You can put that in the minutes as apologies received.'"
4. **The confession and its echo, chapter 14.** "The loudspeaker on the bank road said 'drowned him' after the square had finished hearing it." "She watched her sentence travel back through the square and down the road, row by row, at walking pace." "A child asked what the lady had done. Somebody told the child."
5. **Coralie on Either, and the oven door.** "Eggs is what Either gets in this house." (ch 1) "I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it." (ch 16) And the third telling of the gag, which stops at "'I'll be brief,' […] 'The—'" and is left alone. Nothing is added to explain why she cannot finish it. That restraint is what faults 1 and 2 ask for everywhere else.
