# Critic report: Thriller

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `16.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** One man against forty houses and forty-two beds, with the sum printed on the page: "The trapdoor was twenty-five minutes away, on foot, in the dark, with the dip under water. The bank had ten."
- **Tension: 7.** At its best it is a number falling while a man talks: "The last line on the chart said 36." It is a 7 because from the twist to the night the only plan on the table is "I'll sit up in the van, awake, until 4, when it gives up", and the book has already told me that plan fails.
- **Progression: 7.** Chapter 3 moves faster than Crouch ("Today was the 28th."). Then, from Tuesday 8 to Thursday 10 December, the only thing that advances is the river: "It had come up a step a day since."
- **Comedy: 8.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" The comedy raises the stakes more often than it lowers them, because each joke is a villager refusing to move.
- **Characters: 8.** Coralie is the reason the marriage is worth being afraid for: "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'"
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Nineteen,' Madame Etcheto said. 'That's not many.' / This time nobody called out." It fails once, at the bingo bar (fault 2).
- **Prose: 8.** "Then the water rose up the door and covered it, and the red light went out." Faster to read than Crouch and better made.
- **The ending: 8.** "She heard the passage, then the front door, then the car door shutting on a pillow." It costs more than a Crouch ending does.
- **Clarity: 6.** I could not tell what counts as Lesca being "in". "'On Wednesday night and on Thursday night I held that thing off my chest until 4, and she didn't get in'" says a night in the chair is enough. "'If I'm in your village at 2:51 on Friday night, your bank goes'" sounds like driving. The climax depends on the difference.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes, in two sittings. The break came in the middle of chapter 8, after the kitchen fight and before the café vote.

**Press it on a friend?** On a friend who loved The Good Place, yes. On a friend who reads only Crouch, not until chapters 8 to 10 are fixed.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch from the twist to the night, level with him on the night, above him on what the choice costs. Through this lens the book is a 7 against a bar of 8.**

- **Below: escalation by consequence.** In Dark Matter the box has four rules, and every later horror (the many Jasons) follows from them without a new rule. In Recursion each use of the chair makes the next problem worse. Here the rules arrive by announcement in a meeting ("'There are three points, and I'll say them once'", then "'So that's a fourth point'"), and I count ten by chapter 9. The one the climax hangs on is stated by the woman with the motive: "'If I'm not, it holds.'"
- **Below: pressure from the double in the back half.** Crouch keeps the other Jason in the house with Daniela until the end. Here the other woman is at her most dangerous at "'I'd have this one again'" (chapter 5), and is found out and refused by chapter 7. From Sunday 6 to 2:52 on Saturday 12 she does not drive once.
- **Level: the set piece and the chapter ends.** Chapters 11 to 13 run on a clock as tight as anything in Crouch, and the times add up when checked. Twelve of the seventeen chapter ends would pass in his books: "It rang once." "'Come back across,' her mouth said. Maylis hadn't said it." "'Are you coming up?'" "The arms of the chair were warm." "'Forty-one,' Coralie said."
- **Above: the choice.** A Crouch hero chooses his wife and wins her. Maylis chooses not to be the one who did it ("She wanted it not to be her"), loses the wife, and is told so in a sentence: "'I know what you picked. I was the other one.'" The Good Place staged the trolley problem as a joke with blood on it. This book stages it with a handle 600 metres behind her and no joke, and both answers leave one dead.
- **Above in idea, below in staging: the reversal.** That the rescuer is the flood re-reads the first seven chapters as the Season 1 ending re-reads the season. But The Good Place gives that reversal a scene, a face and a last line. This book gives it a marker post, a bingo gag and a chart (fault 2).

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. Between the twist and the night, nobody tries the defence the book's own lore hands them (chapters 8 and 9)

> 'I can't push, so on Friday I won't lie down. I'll sit up in the van, awake, until 4, when it gives up.'

Against it:

> 'You're not to come till you've finished.' / 'Does it work?' / 'She rings at 3.' (chapter 2)

> 'She gave it that, blade by blade, and it was at it till daylight.' (chapter 3)

> 'She said them so she could stay in her own bed till morning' (chapter 10)

The words exist to keep the thing off a sleeper all night. Maylis knows them, has a count of roadsides that held it for thirteen hours, and has to keep it off for one night. She never tries it and nobody says why it would not work. The only evidence against it is one sentence in chapter 6 ("Inguma came down at its usual minute and sat on her"), with no explanation. A Crouch reader finds this hole on the Sunday and reads the next five days waiting for somebody to notice it.

The same five days are the slackest in the book. Maylis asks for the gate twice at a counter and writes a list. Lesca is a voice in a room. The plan that remains has been declared dead twice, by Kévin: "'I've had that twice, wide awake'" (chapter 2) and "'The thing that sits on you doesn't need you asleep'" (chapter 11).

**Do:** put one attempt in the Tuesday-to-Thursday stretch. Maylis gives it a long count to keep it off, it comes at 2:51 anyway, and in the room where nobody can lie she learns why (it is Lesca's minute, not hers; the count only governs the fetching home). That closes the hole, gives the sag an attempt that fails at a cost, and makes the van the last resort and not the only idea. Then cut Kévin's second warning in chapter 11, so that the roof bending in is a shock.

### 2. The book's best reversal is discovered in a subordinate clause, announced under a gag and explained in a chart (chapters 7 and 8)

The discovery:

> Yesterday Bordenave had found a metre there, and his cellar had been dry. / She had nobody to say it to and no mouth to say it with.

The announcement:

> 'You're the water.' […] 'She wants water,' one of the Lassalle sisters said.

The explanation, next morning:

> 'She's the flood,' Maylis said. 'There's another Peyrebarthe, where the river came through the bank on 11 December 2014.'

This is the turn that re-reads the book, and I got it in three weakened instalments: first as a hint I could have walked past, then as a line the scene makes fun of before I understood it, then as a recital to Coralie. Chapter 7 also carries six scenes (two nights of pushing, the cross, the timed walk, bingo, Arlette, the landing), so nothing in it is given room.

**Do:** split chapter 7 at the trapdoor. On the platform let Maylis do the sum where the reader can see it: the cross on the pad, the dry road, 45 centimetres in a day, the first night on her back and the first night of rain. End the chapter with her standing on Jeannot's trapdoor, inside the woman who is timing the walk to save him and is the reason he needs saving. Then the bingo scene plays with the reader knowing what the three words mean and the hall not knowing, which is both funnier and more frightening. The kitchen chart becomes Coralie testing what the reader already holds.

### 3. The decision that floods the Port is taken in a paragraph of recollection (chapter 10)

> This Coralie knew. She was four steps away with her door open, but Lesca still meant to go.

One page earlier her own Coralie has removed the reason Lesca gave in the steel room, a house "where nobody knew about 2:51". The antagonist then chooses to carry a river onto forty-two beds, and the book reports it in the pluperfect, with a memory of the back of a man's neck. This is the darkest act in the story and it has no scene.

The book also never settles whether she could have stayed away: "'I can't. It has sat on me every night for twelve years, and I've never kept it off'" (chapter 4), "'on Friday I won't try'" (chapter 8), then "'I won't.' / It came out whole" (chapter 16). So she could. Chapter 10 has to show her choosing.

**Do:** give the choice an action. Arlette has told her how to stay ("'Sleep on your side, Maylis. Tonight. With your knees up, so it has nowhere to sit'"). Let Lesca lie down on her side, with the light showing from the open door across the landing, and then turn onto her back. That one movement is the scene, and it needs no explanation after it.

### 4. Too many rules, laid down by assertion, and a room that turns assertion into fact (chapters 4 and 8)

> 'Standing up in this room, a woman can't say a thing she knows isn't true.'

> 'If I'm in your village at 2:51 on Friday night, your bank goes, as mine was going. If I'm not, it holds.' / Nothing had stopped a word of that.

The rule says "knows". Lesca cannot know the second sentence; she has one week of river levels. But the room lets it through, so the reader must take a guess as law, and the climax is built on it. The truth room is a fine comic device and a dangerous thriller one: it certifies whatever the plot needs.

The count of rules by chapter 9: shared knowledge in the chair, staying seated, the owner's right hand, the count, the truth room, the body left behind, the river, the pushing, the fading hand, the hall of the dead. Four of them do the work.

**Do:** state once, in plain words, that a night in the chair counts as her being in the village. Make the 2:51 rule something Maylis proves and does not hear (fault 1's failed attempt can carry it). Let the room stop Lesca on "it holds" and make her say "I think it holds".

### 5. At the handle she makes a choice that reads as arithmetic (chapter 13)

> For three breaths she kept her foot off the pedal. / Lesca had let his phone ring once and then turned the handle. Maylis had let it ring nine times, and Jeannot was alive. / She drove past.

The dip is the choice the book means, and it lands: "She wanted it not to be her." But the dip is only reached because of this one, three minutes earlier, with most of the bank's ten minutes left and the handle level with her eyes. On the night it is covered by a job estimate ("Three minutes to get there. Two to get him up the ladder"). The motive arrives three chapters later: "'I drove past the handle to be the one who did.'" By then it is a confession about a moment I read as a plan.

**Do:** put the wanting at the handle, in the same plain form as the line at the dip. One sentence. The reader should watch her choose pride at 02:56 and cowardice at 02:59, and know the difference at the time.

### 6. Chapter 1 loses its nerve for 1,200 words

> 'I've got a registered letter for the mayor,' the postman said into the gap.

The first 300 words of chapter 1 are the best dread in the book. Then the thing on the wardrobe is not mentioned for the length of the square: a postman, a window, a handle, a duck hunter's three points. Every plant is needed later. None of them is under any pressure from the night before. This is the first place a Crouch reader puts the book down, with the premise still two chapters off.

**Do:** cut the square by a third and run the night through it. She has woken with a stone in her head and a name above hers "which she couldn't read". Let her nearly read it while Jeannot is talking to her through his van window. Then the scene has a current under it and the plants ride on it.

### 7. Chapter 10 spends the eve of the night on tape marks

> 'A point of order,' Lesca said. 'You have the flowers as one item and the kiss as the next. They're one item.'

Chapter 9 ends on "before dark". Chapter 10 then gives 900 words to a rehearsal with a young man and a clipboard. What chapter 14 needs from it is four things: the front row, "Here", Madame Etcheto nearest the stone, the medal on the coat. What the reader needs is two: "'The others have died since then, in those beds, of the things people die of'" and "'You're holding on to that stone, Madame le maire.'"

**Do:** halve it. Keep Arlette, Coralie's strips of tape and Darrigrand's step. Lose the kiss, the photographer and the seating argument.

### 8. A question the prologue has answered is played four times as an open one

> 'Did you know he was out?' (chapter 3)

> 'When you stood on that platform, did you—' (chapter 6)

> 'That's not a no.' (chapter 8)

The prologue shows her turn the van, look down the track and ring a number. By chapter 3 any reader has it. Yet chapter 6 ends its council scene on Larrère's cut-off question as if it were a cliffhanger. The real question is when she will say it, and to whom, and that one works (chapters 10 and 14).

**Do:** stop the characters circling it. Keep Maïté's question in chapter 3, because it sets the evasion. In chapter 6 let Larrère finish his question and let Lesca's hand ring the bell on the silence after it. The tension is in her not answering, not in his not asking.

### 9. A threat is raised over the right hand and never collected (chapters 8 and 12)

> 'If she gets in again on Friday night, there may be no hand to come back to.'

On the night:

> The one on the brake was the weaker, and it had got there first.

The hand takes the top of the wheel, covers the eyes, pulls the handbrake and holds it against a whole body, at no cost. The key dropped in the yard and the weak squeeze on Thursday promised that this hand would fail when it mattered.

**Do:** make it fail in the van, a finger at a time, while she says the words. The count she gives is "the fingers of my right hand". Let there be fewer of them holding by the time the thing has counted.

### 10. After "Forty-one" the book has three chapters and one live question, and it holds the question back (chapters 15 and 16)

> 'The motion is carried, Madame le—'

Chapter 15 is four files closed in order: Madame Etcheto told, the verb given, Larrère sent down the lane, the vote taken. Each is right. Together they read like the minutes. Chapter 16 walks Maylis through six verdicts before it reaches the only thing I was still afraid of, and the envelope that decides it appears in the same scene: "'Bayonne's written. The geriatric floor, on nights. I start in January.'"

**Do:** show the envelope cut open behind the fruit bowl at first light, before Maylis walks down into the Port. Then the folder, the baby carriage and the café are read under it. Cut the council scene to the sash, the timer turned to nothing and "'Lesca, Maylis,' Lesca said. 'Surname first.'"

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The prologue, and its answer in chapter 9.** "It rang once." Then: "It rang once. She kept the phone to her ear. It rang a second time, then a third." Then Jeannot's six points down to "'Bugger off.'"
2. **The file in chapter 3,** from "*24 November 2014. Under the line. Rain since the night.*" through "Today was the 28th." to the motion with the comma added.
3. **The pulse chart cut against the untimed speech in chapter 6.** 64 falling to 36 at home while Larrère, "timed like an egg" for eleven years, is at last allowed to finish a sentence.
4. **Bordenave's cross, and the "Either" it leads to.** One wrong forecast proves the twist, discredits the warning and decides the vote ("'If she can be out on Friday the 4th, she can be out on Friday the 11th'"). It ends at 5 p.m. on the platform: "'Either,' Maylis said. / 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said."
5. **Chapter 13 from "Castagnet, house" to "Forty-one",** with Kévin's hand in his own headlights ("'Five,' he said") and "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'" And in chapter 14, the sentence going back through the square "at walking pace" and the man beyond the last loudspeaker who starts to clap.
