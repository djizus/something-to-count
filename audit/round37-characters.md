# Critic report: Characters

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "'I'd have had you. You answer. But what I'd have had is a river over forty-two beds.'" (9). The stakes only become a person's here, halfway through. Before that they are a date on a stone.
- **Tension: 7.5.** "'The gate, or Castagnet? Say which, Maylis. For once.' / 'Both.'" (14).
- **Progression: 6.** "'Either,' Maylis said." (12). The hinge of the book is the word from chapter 1, unchanged, an hour after she has asked Coralie back and three chapters after she said "Open" twice.
- **Comedy: 8.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (6).
- **Characters: 7.5.** "'That's her,' she said. 'In this hand. I've had sixteen years of her saying nothing, and I know it when I've got hold of it.'" (8). Coralie is a 9 and Lesca an 8. Maylis is a 6, because for a third of the book a wrist is all there is of her.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.5.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" (17). The running joke fails in the mouth that made it.
- **Prose: 7.** "She wanted it not to be her." (14). That is the method at its best. The same flat report is used for every feeling in the book, so the people are known by what their hands do and almost never by what they think.
- **The ending: 7.5.** "She heard the passage, then the front door, then the car door shutting on a pillow." (17). The marriage ends properly. The two women's own ending is six lines.
- **Clarity: 6.** "'The count tells it when to take the visitor home,' Lesca said. 'It doesn't keep it off.'" (9). A rule is still being corrected in chapter 9, and how much each woman is to blame depends on those rules.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes.
**Press it on a friend?** On the friend who watched The Good Place for the people. Not on the one who reads Crouch for speed.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Above Crouch on character. A notch below The Good Place.** Overall the book sits a little under the bar.

1. **Above Crouch: the wife is a person.** Daniela in Dark Matter exists to be lost. Coralie has a want that is older than the plot and she acts on it: "I've had the form since March. I posted it today, and I posted it for me." (8). She also has a fault of her own that kills: "*Asleep. I'm not waking a woman of 94 for orange.*" (12).
2. **Level with The Good Place: everyone is wrong in their own way, and every wrongness carries weight on the night.** Jeannot's hand goes up out of pride. Larrère orders from another man's kitchen. Coralie won't wake her. Maylis says Either. Lesca, standing where she can't lie, says "To stay". No one is merely unlucky.
3. **Below both: the doubles.** The last third of Dark Matter is a man facing himself. The Good Place grinds its pairs against each other for whole episodes. These two women meet four times, standing, to an agenda, and the chair hands each of them the other's knowledge so that they never have to ask.

**The lens questions.**

- *Distinct?* Yes, by speech and by method. Lesca thinks in items and motions, Maylis in trailers and wheelbarrows. In private they are the same woman who cannot ask, which is the design, and it holds.
- *Wanted-for?* Lesca, yes, from "Nothing. Go back to bed." (3). Maylis, yes in 1, 6, 12 and 17, and no in 5, 7 and 8.
- *Alive?* Lesca is alive whenever she is on the page. Maylis is alive when she has a body.
- *Softened or excused?* Lesca twice (fault 3). Maylis twice (faults 1 and 6).
- *Described from outside as a woman?* No. Neither is ever looked at. The price is that neither can be seen: no age, no face, and no shock when one of them first sees the other (fault 2).
- *Who would be missed?* Madame Etcheto, Larrère, Kévin, Jeannot, Darrigrand and his step, Daguerre and his "Here". Not Sarthou, Bordenave or the dealer.
- *Which relationship carries it, and does it pay?* Maylis and Coralie, and it pays in full. Lesca and her Coralie pays three-quarters. Maylis and Lesca does not pay.
- *Does the reader care who lives?* For Coralie, for a page and a half in 14, completely. For Madame Etcheto, after the fact. For Jeannot, mostly on Lesca's behalf.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The choice that decides everything is one word, and the narrator excuses it. (12)**
"'Either,' Maylis said. / 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. 'That's how the vote left it.' / He was off the platform and down the steps before she could take it back."
At 5 it is daylight, Jeannot is standing on the bank, the handle is fitted and nobody would die. This is the real choice of the book, as Madame Darrigrand says in 17. It gets one line and no inside. It comes an hour after "Come back across the landing", on the same day she faced Larrère about the back lane, and after Kévin has taken his six months off the scale. The character has been climbing for eleven chapters and the plot pushes her back down with a reflex. "Before she could take it back" then tells the reader she would have. A minute later she is at his van window and doesn't.
*Do:* make it a scene the length of the dip. Let her have "Open" ready, since she has said it twice at a counter. Let Jeannot's "It's your hand on the handle" be what stops it: she can ask for the gate, but she cannot do it to a man's face on her own name. Reach Either through two or three exchanges. Cut "before she could take it back". At his window, make it plain that she has a second chance and lets it go.

**2. The two women never meet as people. (4, 9, 13, 17)**
"'Good,' Lesca said. 'You can get up. That saves a week. I have a number of things to tell you'" (4). "'In this chair, what one of us knows, the other gets.'" (4). "Neither of them sat down." (17).
The premise is two versions of one person. They share four scenes, all conducted as meetings, and the chair rule means neither ever has to ask the other anything. Maylis sees her own face in a chair and the book goes straight to a joke about who chairs. Her own temptation by Lesca's life lasts one paragraph: "She liked it. She slowed down to get more of it." (6), then "it came with Kévin's cap and one pillow" and it is gone. The best idea in the book is that each Coralie wants the other woman. "'I'd have this one again.'" (5). "'I'd carry my pillow back across that landing tonight.'" (6). Each woman was in the hand for the other's sentence, and they never say it to each other.
*Do:* one page, in the last chair scene of 9, before the wall opens. Standing, where neither can lie, each says what she heard in the other's kitchen. Lesca has to say out loud why she can't take her own Coralie's offer. Give Maylis one beat of looking at her in 4.

**3. Lesca's Coralie is never told what the crossing was for or what it cost. (11, 16)**
"'I'm going to get him out.'" (11), against "'To stay,' she said. 'That's the one that comes out.'" (9), and then "'Good night, Maylis,' she said, and went into the spare room." (16).
Lesca tells her Coralie the version she can only say sitting down. She went over for another Coralie's bed, and an old woman drowned for it. The one tender thing she gets at the end comes from the woman she tried to replace, on half the truth. Madame Etcheto is told and Coralie is not. A page after "I drowned him", the widow also hands a share of the night to Larrère while Lesca stands on her ladder.
*Do:* on the landing in 16, Coralie asks "Did you get him out?" and Lesca answers. Let "Good night, Maylis" come after that or not at all.

**4. Maylis is a passenger for a third of the book. (5, 7, 8)**
"Maylis had her right hand and nothing else." (5). "Somebody had to tell the village. She would rather it was Lesca, who signed things." (4).
In these chapters the reader learns Lesca by what she does and Maylis by what she blocks: she tips a spoon, shuts a tap, holds a post, clamps a jaw. Every want is a veto. Chapter 6 proves she can carry a chapter the moment she has legs: "'I want that,' Maylis said."
*Do:* add nothing. Convert. In each driven chapter give the hand one act that goes after something. Taking the watch on the trapdoor in 7 is the model.

**5. Everybody refuses to be told. (4, 11, 12, 14, 15, 17)**
"'Don't tell me now.'" (Coralie, 11). "'I'm not asking where.'" (Arlette, 11). "'Don't tell me now.'" (the other Coralie, 12). "'Don't tell me what he was.'" (14). "'Don't give me the minutes.'" (14). "'And I'm not asking you why. I don't want to be told.'" (Arlette, 15). "'Don't give me the measurement.'" (17).
For Lesca's Coralie in 11 this is a character: twelve years of not asking, with her reason. By 17 it is how both Coralies, the mother and Maïté all close a scene, and they blur into one voice.
*Do:* keep it for Lesca's Coralie, and for "Don't give me the measurement", which pays off the tic. Let Arlette ask why. Let Maylis's Coralie, in the corridor, want to be told.

**6. Chapter 17 forgives Maylis three times in a row, once by the wrong woman.**
"'One,' she said, in the voice she called the numbers in. 'On its own. Sit down on it.'"
Kévin tows the van. Marie-Jo gives her a chair. Jeannot writes four pages and picks up a shovel. Against them stand Madame Darrigrand and half a joke from the dealer. Kévin is paying a debt and Jeannot's letter "doesn't thank you", so those two hold. Marie-Jo has no history with this Maylis; the weight of her gesture is borrowed from the other village. Meanwhile Maylis's own mother, across the yard, has one scene in the whole book (9) and is absent from the night and the Sunday.
*Do:* cut the chair, or give that beat to Arlette and make it harder.

**7. The Castagnet marriage is never on the page. (3, 4, 6, 17)**
"'In the— It's blankets.'" (4). "'In his— In his minute.'" (6). "'After it you— Don't kiss me.'" (6).
Jeannot and Maïté never share a scene in either village. The man who would be mourned and the woman who mourns him have to be taken on trust, and so does the suitcase. Maïté's only speech pattern in both worlds is the sentence that breaks off. She leaves the book watched from a café door.
*Do:* one glimpse of his lunch on her table. Give her one sentence in 4 that she finishes.

**8. Lesca's turn is explained to the reader. (13)**
"Twelve years ago she had let his phone ring once and then done what she had come to do. This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel."
This is the moment she stops being the woman who came to stay. The stalled van, the locked hand and "the reflector was red, and no nearer than it had been twelve years ago" have already done it. Then the narrator states the moral.
*Do:* cut the two sentences. Go from the reflector to "'I'm letting go.'"

**9. Larrère becomes a recording in Maylis's village. (6, 9, 12)**
"'I have a letter in here in your handwriting. It's your resignation, with a gap where the date goes.'" (12).
The reader watched her sign it in 6 and saw the sheet against the glass in 9. The third telling adds nothing, and the man who was a pleasure in 1 and in the un-timed speech of 6 has one move left.
*Do:* in 12, the hand flat on the folder is enough. Cut the speech.

**10. The narrator diagnoses Maylis three times in chapter 1.**
"But Coralie had to be up before 6, so Maylis didn't ask." "But the duck meant an hour at the stove for Coralie, after a day's work. So she wrote *Either*". "But Larrère paid her wages, so she didn't knock on the glass."
Want, but, so she didn't: three times in one chapter. The first two build her. The third restates what the window has just shown.
*Do:* cut the third.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 11, the phone on the chest of drawers.** "'Because you'd have told me.'" "'The phone doesn't say how long it rang. It says not answered.'"
2. **The owner keeps her right hand, and everything built on it:** the stair post, the wrist pulling away under two fingers, the handbrake.
3. **Kévin's count.** "'Five,' he said." (14) and "'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much.'" (17).
4. **Chapter 17, from "'I know what you picked. I was the other one.'" through "'I want to dig the grave myself'" to the pillow in the car.** Do not make her answer "You".
5. **Larrère's window and the timer that doesn't ring.** "'Eleven years I've been timed like an egg.'" (6). And "'I'll be brief.'" followed by nothing (16).
