# Critic's report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** "Forty houses, forty-two beds, a car in the yard of the care home and a van at the end of a track." The sum is stated once and never has to be restated.
- **Tension: 7.** "She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it broke." Chapters 12 to 14 hold. Chapter 7 and the second half of 9 do not.
- **Progression: 7.** "The rain came when Lesca came. When she was kept out, it stopped." The book's one mid-course reversal is told three times (7, 8, 9) and so moves once and then marks time twice.
- **Comedy: 7.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station." The lines are at the level of the comparison. The scenes are shorter than their ideas and there is only one comic register.
- **Characters: 8.** "It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you. I was in no danger, and I'll say so to anyone." Everybody is wrong in their own way and stays that way after being rescued.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 9.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" The same gag, the third time, is the grief. This is the thing the book does better than either comparison.
- **Prose: 7.** "This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel." A narrator who has just shown a thing well then says what it was.
- **The ending: 8.** "Then he stepped over the string in his rubber boots and began to dig." It costs a death, a job and a marriage, and the last gesture is from the man who will not say thank you.
- **Clarity: 6.** "The count tells it when to take the visitor home. It doesn't keep it off." That correction arrives in chapter 9. I had the rule wrong until then, and so had Maylis.

Would I have finished it unpaid: yes. Would I press it on a friend: yes, with the instruction to get as far as chapter 5 before deciding.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Against The Good Place: level on the moral joke, below on build, above on the switch.**

- *Level.* The book does what the series does with Chidi: it takes a failing that is funny at breakfast and makes it the thing that kills somebody. "I don't cook Either. There's no recipe for it" in chapter 1 becomes "That was Either, with the river in it" in chapter 17, and the word has not changed. The trolley problem is put in a village's mouth without a philosopher in sight: "It's four when you're the one counting. When it's your house it's one, and they knock it down." The stone that needs a verb ("It reads that she opened him") is the whole ethical argument as a committee item. None of this makes the problem small.
- *Below.* The series builds a comic scene until it breaks something. Here a comic scene makes its point and stops, usually because the plot needs the page. Chapter 5's café and chapter 6's council are the only two that are allowed to run, and they are the two best chapters. The series also delivers its reversals as jokes. This book's reversal, "You're the water", is said in the funniest room in the book, and is misheard by a hall, and would be a Good Place moment if the narrator had not explained it to me a chapter earlier.
- *Below.* One register. The comedy is deadpan procedure (motions, timers, minutes, registered post, care plans) plus a chorus at a counter. It is a good register and it is the right one for a book about people who count so as not to choose. But the series has five comic voices in a room and this book has one voice in five mouths. Coralie is the only person who is funny in a different way, by mimicry, and she is taken out of the comedy after chapter 10.
- *Above.* The series seldom lets a death stay a death. This book drowns a woman of ninety-four in her bed and four pages later has "One. On its own. Sit down on it", and neither damages the other. The best joke in the manuscript is told in the hall of the dead: "You can put that in the minutes as apologies received."

**Against Crouch:** he is not funny and does not try to be, so on this lens the book is above him by default. Where it is below him matters to the comedy all the same: a Crouch reader is never asked to hold a rule in mind in order to get a beat, and here several jokes and the main set piece depend on one.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The reversal is explained before it is played, and then twice more. (Chapters 7, 8, 9.)**
"The rain came when Lesca came. When she was kept out, it stopped. A flood was coming on the 11th because Lesca had brought it over with her."
Seven paragraphs of Maylis doing a sum on a platform hand me the turn of the book as arithmetic, with nobody in the scene to say it to. One chapter later she gets three words out at a bar, "You're the water", and a Lassalle sister says "She wants water" and her sister says "She's got a bar full of coffee." That is the reveal and the laugh in one beat, and it is wasted, because I already know. Then chapter 9 does it a third time as Coralie's chart, "*What. Since when. How do you know.*", which is the telling that is in character and funny.
What I would do: in chapter 7 keep the evidence (Bordenave's cross, the dry road, forty-five centimetres made up in a day) and cut the conclusion. End the platform passage on her doing the sum and having no mouth to say the answer with. Let the bar be the first time the reader hears it, at the moment the hall gets it wrong. Let the chart be the only explanation. This also gives the bingo scene a question to run on: why is she holding her teeth shut?

**2. The book's central joke turns fatal in one line, and the page lets her off. (Chapter 12.)**
"'Either,' Maylis said. 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. 'That's how the vote left it.' He was off the platform and down the steps before she could take it back."
This is the hinge of the whole book: the handle fitted, daylight, the man she wants to save standing on the boards, and the word that "had always come when she needed it" comes. It gets eight lines. Worse, "before she could take it back" hands her an excuse. Nothing stops her taking it back. He has only walked down some steps; the handle is still on; she is the one who lifts it off. The comparison gives this kind of paralysis time, so that the audience laughs and then stops laughing.
What I would do: cut "before she could take it back." Let her stand there with the catch in its first tooth after he has gone and nobody preventing her, and take the handle off herself, at length. And use what the steel room set up: in there the word stuck in her throat because it wasn't true. Out here nothing stops it. Show her notice that it came out easily.

**3. The adultery farce never costs anybody anything. (Chapters 5 to 12.)**
"A woman takes flowers to the wife because she's after the husband or because she's after the wife. It's adultery whichever she's after, and it's in that house."
The chorus is right about the house and wrong about the name, and "I only had the one name wrong" in chapter 17 is a perfect payoff. But between the setup and the payoff the rumour does no work. Jeannot, who is its subject, never answers it. Maïté never hears it. Coralie laughs at it once ("I've had sixteen and a shopping pad"). Larrère cites it once. A misunderstanding in a farce has to make somebody do the wrong thing.
What I would do: make it the reason the trapdoor stays locked at dusk. At present he refuses on arithmetic ("I'm not coming up out of it for a number"). He has half the better reason already: "You were told the way to my kitchen as well." Give him the rest. He will not be seen climbing out of a hole in a field at night with the woman the café has written down beside his name. Then the comic thread is carrying the tragedy, and Josiane's counter has helped drown room twelve.

**4. Chapter 7 has no company in it.**
"A cracked rib mends if it's left alone. Push on it and it goes through into the lung. I've seen one do it. He was eighty-one."
I stopped smiling at the top of this chapter and did not start again until Jeannot's "That's the best thing on that pad", which is six lines in a hundred and fifteen. The funniest voice in the book is used here as an instruction leaflet: eight sentences of case history, delivered with a pillow under her arm, so that "coins" and "pink" can be collected two pages later. Then the two pushed nights, then the sum, then the walk with a watch. It is all fuse.
What I would do: cut the old man to the two things that are needed (the taste and the colour) and have Coralie say them the way she says everything else, angry and exact, in three sentences. Run the two nights as one. With fault 1 fixed, the chapter is half its length and ends on the trapdoor.

**5. The narrator explains the beat that has just landed.**
"It was the only word she had." (5) "Nobody in Peyrebarthe had ever heard Maylis's mouth forbid anything." (5) "Inguma had gone to count the corn, so nothing would take Lesca home tonight." (8) "She was awake, and this night was hers." (12) "This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel." (13) "She had put Lesca out, and it had come too late: the river was through the bank already." (14)
Deadpan comedy lives on the line after the joke being silence. Each of these is the narrator leaning in to make sure. In chapter 5 "Either" has just cost her the only good breakfast in sixteen years; I do not need to be told it was the only word she had. In chapter 13 the steering wheel is the phone; I saw it.
What I would do: cut all six and go looking for the others. Where the sentence carries a fact the reader needs, give the fact to a person.

**6. The bingo scene stops to state a rule at its peak. (Chapter 8.)**
"The cold reached the bar and came over it. Lesca hadn't finished the words, so Inguma had nothing to count and would take her home."
This is the one full set piece: the flooding coffee, "Have you got toothache, Maylis?" over the microphone, Josiane's commentary to the second row, the tablecloths lifting table by table. It builds properly until the moment it should break, and there the narrator turns to the reader and does the accounts. Madame Etcheto finishing the prayer "over my duck" is the best comic and worst moral act in the book, an old woman kindly helping the wrong one, and it arrives behind two sentences of mechanism.
What I would do: cut the rule sentence before her speech and the one after it. "That's till morning," said to Coralie with both hands on a duck, tells me everything. Also finish the game. The big basket is announced and never won.

**7. Lesca stops chairing. (Chapter 9, both steel-room scenes.)**
"'The count tells it when to take the visitor home,' Lesca said. 'It doesn't keep it off. 2:51 is my minute.'"
In chapter 4 the rules of the book are delivered as a meeting with nobody in the chair ("Then it's carried, or it's a tie, and in a tie the chair decides." "There's nobody in the chair."), and that is how the comparison delivers rules: as a joke about the person explaining them. By chapter 9 she is just explaining them. The procedure is what makes her funny and what makes her frightening, and it thins exactly when the stakes go up. It also spends in advance the line in chapter 17, "She didn't call anything to order", which should be the first time she hasn't.
What I would do: keep her on an agenda until the last night. "Don't come on Friday" is an item she rules out of order. "Nil" is already a remnant of this ("I've written it down. Nil."); build back up to it.

**8. The premise is handed over in a caption. (Chapter 3, first lines.)**
"The other Maylis Lesca was the one whose village had its flood in 2014 and made her mayor. Call her Lesca."
Chapter 2 ends on "'Come back across,' her mouth said. Maylis hadn't said it." I turned the page wanting to know who, and the narrator told me in a sentence and gave me a name to use, in a voice that addresses the reader nowhere else in the book. The paragraph that follows does the job without help: two pillows, rain on a roof that has been dry for two weeks, the yellow coat "she had handed in with her keys when she became mayor."
What I would do: cut the first two sentences and the third's "eleven years" briefing. Let the name arrive when her own Coralie says "Madame le maire", and let the narrator begin calling her Lesca from the next line.

**9. Two blocks of briefing sit in front of the first smile. (Chapters 1 and 2.)**
"The bank was her job too: the care home at one end, then the patch she repaired every spring with four wheelbarrows of clay, then the floodgate." and "It's a thing you can learn, waking up inside your dreams. The phone goes off on the hour and you count."
The first real smile is the postman asking the woman with the rake "Is the man who works for the village around?", and it is very good. Before it the reader has taken on a sleep paralysis, a 2014 experiment, a stone, a shopping pad, the layout of a bank, the ownership of a gate and a cracked rib. Kévin's lesson in chapter 2 is the same thing: a paragraph of how-to from a character whose comic trait, everywhere else, is that he only asks questions. Both pay off heavily later. Neither is company while it is happening.
What I would do: move the bank's geography into the Jeannot exchange, where a man is already listing his grievances in order. Give Kévin's method to Kévin as questions and a failure: he has never once got six.

**10. The running joke and the death toll share a number. (Chapters 1, 14, 17.)**
"I've paid for forty-one registered letters" and "'Forty-one,' Coralie said."
Forty-one is Jeannot's letters, the book's longest-running gag, repeated in chapters 1, 5, 6 and 17. It is also the last word of chapter 14, the count of the living with one chair empty, the gravest line in the manuscript. Forty-two is both the beds and the letter that defends her. At "Forty-one" I thought, for a moment, of registered post. If the rhyme is meant, nobody in the book notices it and it reads as an accident; if it is not meant, it is one.
What I would do: decide. Either let Jeannot see it in chapter 17 (the forty-second is the one that isn't a complaint, and the forty-second bed is the one she is digging for), or move the letters ten numbers away.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie at the oven door, all three times.** Chapter 1 ("In my tray," and shuts the door on him), chapter 9 ("The soup will be consulted"), chapter 17, where she bends to the gap, gets as far as "The—" and cannot. The third is the best line in the book and it is half a word.
2. **The hall where the dead play bingo, and Lesca refusing her seat in it.** The man with his arm around his card so his neighbour can't copy. The woman lifting her handbag off the empty chair. "The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies. She has a ceremony at 11, and she will not be taking this seat."
3. **Jeannot's six points down the phone straight after the drowning** ("And six. Bugger off."), and letter No. 42 brought by hand because he is not paying to register it. He is saved and unthankful and right about the dry floor, and the book never corrects him.
4. **The council in chapter 6.** Larrère with the bell held off him: "Eleven years I've been timed like an egg. I have sentences in that register with no ends on them." The secretary bending down to put a letter on the floor beside the hand, which signs it. Two hands up for one mayor: "The chair votes once, Madame le maire." All of it with the pulse chart falling in the other village.
5. **The minute after the confession in chapter 15.** The deputy turning the sheet over to see whether there is more on the back. The man beyond the last loudspeaker who thinks she has finished and claps. "I've got nothing on the sheet for this." And Marie-Jo, who has not said her name since 2018: "Somebody has to stack them."

Also leave alone: "Had the key and went for a walk, did you?" replacing "Still in the box, were you?"; the chrysanthemums going "in the sink, on top of the potato peelings"; "They had one nose between them, so now neither of them could breathe"; and the rib as the thing that stops Maylis laughing, so that in a body with all its ribs she laughs through a minute's silence.

- **04.** A meeting with nobody in the chair; a pot of chrysanthemums carried to a living man's wife. The two best comic ideas in the first third, back to back. **8**
