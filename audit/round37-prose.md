# Critic's report: Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. About 43,600 words. Nothing else was opened.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** The sum is exact and the reader can do it too: "The trapdoor was twenty-five minutes away, on foot, in the dark, with the dip under water. The bank had ten." (10)
- **Tension: 7.** Chapters 12 to 14 hold: "She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it broke." (14) Chapters 7 to 9 reason where they should press.
- **Progression: 6.** The reversal arrives as arithmetic and is then repeated: "The rain came when Lesca came. When she was kept out, it stopped." (7)
- **Comedy: 7.** I smiled most at the people who are wrong in a fixed way: "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (6)
- **Characters: 8.** Coralie is the best thing in the book and gets its best line: "'I know what you picked. I was the other one.'" (17)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** In the flooded corridor: "'It's Saturday.' 'Then they'll want the bins out,' he said." (14)
- **Prose: 7.** At its best it measures the world in Maylis's jobs: "Twenty-five minutes was a trailer of leaves to the dump and back. Ten was the time it took to hook the trailer up." (8) At its worst it explains its own joke: "That was two hands for the mayor speaking on the 12th, and only one mayor." (6)
- **The ending: 8.** The last image costs nothing extra and says everything: "Then he stepped over the string in his rubber boots and began to dig." (17) The chair scene before it is weaker.
- **Clarity: 6.** With two women in one body the pronouns sometimes fail: "Two words were all Maylis could take before Lesca had the jaw back. By then it was no use to her." (3) Whose use, and which her?

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who reads novels, yes. On the Crouch reader the bar names, no: that reader stops in chapter 7 or 9.

## 2. Against the comparisons (prose)

**Level overall. Above in how a sentence is made, below in pace and in trust.**

1. **Above Crouch in the making of sentences.** Crouch writes fast, plain and often second-hand. Here almost nothing is second-hand. There are about a dozen similes in the whole book, almost no adverbs, no semicolons, and a dash only where a speaker breaks off. Objects do the work that Crouch gives to italics and one-line paragraphs: the shopping pad, the two fingers on the wrist, the car window, the kitchen timer, the pillow. The pad alone carries the marriage from "Eggs is what Either gets in this house" to "That was Either, with the river in it."

2. **Below Crouch in variation of pace.** A Crouch chapter changes its paragraph length violently when the danger changes. This book runs at one pulse. By my count the average sentence is under ten words, about 95 sentences open with "Then" and about 475 with "She". Raking leaves in chapter 1 and wading the Port in chapter 14 have nearly the same beat, and when chapter 14 does change, it changes to telegram fragments ("Torch up. Right arm across her side."), which sound like a different narrator.

3. **Below both in trust.** Crouch states a reversal once, at the end of a chapter, and cuts. The Good Place never explains a joke or a rule straight: the rule comes out of somebody's wrongness. This narrator shows a thing and then says what it showed. The reversal that the visitor brings the flood is given three times in two chapters. Maylis's silences are footnoted with her reasons. Lesca's change of heart at the wheel is argued in four paragraphs. Chapter 4 proves the book can do it the other way: Lesca chairs a meeting with one chair and nobody in it, and every rule of the premise comes out as her vanity. That scene is level with The Good Place. The narrator's glosses are below it.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The central reversal is delivered as a sum and then told twice more. (7, 9) Structural.**

> "Yesterday morning Bordenave had found the river a metre under the line and the road dry. Lesca's file had said seventy, so he had drawn his cross. Today the post said fifty-five." (7)

Four paragraphs of recap follow, of facts the reader met one page earlier, ending in a stated conclusion: "A flood was coming on the 11th because Lesca had brought it over with her." Then Maylis says it to Coralie over a three-column chart ("'She's the flood'... 'When she comes over into me she brings her river with her'"), and in the same chapter Lesca says it in the chair ("'Now I know. It comes with me.'"). This is the turn that re-reads everything before it, and it reaches the reader as bookkeeping, three times.

What I would do: in chapter 7, cut the recap. Keep the marker post, the wet cellar, the cross, and one line of realisation that stops short of the full statement. Let "You're the water", shouted across the bingo hall and misheard by everyone, be the first time it is said. Let Coralie's chart in chapter 9 be the only place it is laid out, because there it is a scene: she is deciding which woman to believe. In the chair, cut Lesca down to the one thing that is new, that only Friday night matters.

**2. Lesca's decision at the wheel is argued where it should be done. (13) Structural.**

> "Twelve years ago she had let his phone ring once and then done what she had come to do. This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel."

This is her whole arc, and it happens between the eighth ring and the ninth in four paragraphs of reasoning: "She had told Maylis there was no both. She had wanted both herself, the bed and Castagnet." The reader made the comparison with the prologue two pages ago, when the headlights went down the track. The ring count promises speed and the prose stops to think.

What I would do: keep what she sees. The dead engine, the hand locked on the brake, 02:53, and "the reflector was red, and no nearer than it had been twelve years ago". Keep "There was no man in it", which is the one sentence that tells me something I did not know. Then "I'm letting go." Cut the rest.

**3. The narrator footnotes every one of Maylis's silences. (1, 2, 7, 9, 12)**

> "Maylis wanted the duck. But the duck meant an hour at the stove for Coralie, after a day's work. So she wrote *Either* across the bottom and went out to the van." (1)

The same three steps (she wanted, but, so she didn't) come three times in chapter 1 alone: the wrist, the duck, the car window. They return for the eels ("If she looked, she would know. Then she would have to do something about it"), for the landing ("Until she could, she wouldn't ask her back to lie beside that"), for Bayonne, for Kévin at the handle. Her silence is the character. Each footnote does the reader's work and makes her smaller, because a reason given by the narrator is an excuse. The proof is on the platform in chapter 12, where she says "Either" to Jeannot with no footnote at all, he answers "Then it's shut", and it is the hardest moment in the first two-thirds of the book.

What I would do: keep the first, the wrist, because it teaches the reader how to read her. From then on keep the act and cut the reason.

**4. In the last chair scene the two women summarise their own motives. (17)**

> "'At the gate I thought I could do better than you,' she said. 'I drove past the handle to be the one who did.'"

And Lesca before her: "'It was my own night I was putting right.'" Both were shown. Chapter 14 already has "Maylis had let it ring nine times, and Jeannot was alive. She drove past", and chapter 13 has the motion with no man in it. The same chapter has also had Maylis tell Kévin "I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me." Three accounts of one motive in the final chapter.

What I would do: the scene is "Forty-one." / "Is yours in the house?" / "Across the landing. Four steps. Yours?" / "She took the pillow." / "Don't come again." / "I won't." Those six lines are the scene. Cut the two speeches between them. Keep the line to Kévin, since he is owed it.

**5. One rhythm for everything. (throughout; 14)**

> "Mud to the ankle at every step. The torch in her left hand, her right arm pressed across her side." (14)

Short declarative sentences, subject first, are the voice, and I would not change the voice. But with the average under ten words and so many sentences beginning "Then" or "She", calm and danger read at the same speed. The book's one answer is the verbless fragment in chapter 14, and that is the wrong one: it is the only place the narrator drops verbs, so it reads as borrowed. The right answer is already in chapter 15: "She watched her sentence travel back through the square and down the road, row by row, at walking pace." A long sentence that carries a whole movement.

What I would do: restore the verbs in chapter 14. Give the wading of the street and the corridor two or three long sentences of that kind. Halve the "Then" openers across the book; most can simply go.

**6. Rules of the premise stated by the narrator, and certified. (8, 9)**

> "Lesca hadn't finished the words, so Inguma had nothing to count and would take her home." (8)

Five paragraphs later: "Inguma had gone to count the corn, so nothing would take Lesca home tonight." Both sit inside the funniest scene in the book and explain its mechanism while it runs. Madame Etcheto's "'That's till morning'" already does the second one. In chapter 9 the narrator stamps a speech as true: "Nothing had stopped a word of that." The reader learned in chapter 4 what the room does to a lie, from watching a word stick.

What I would do: cut all three sentences. Nothing else needs to change.

**7. Refrains struck too many times. (throughout)**

> "A voice was calling numbers, each one whole and then in parts." (9)

Repetition with a turn is this book's method, and it is why the prologue and chapter 14 can share a paragraph and the second one hurts. But the count has run away. "Whole and then in parts" comes five times. Inguma's sound is a sack five times, twice in nearly the same words ("the sound a full sack makes when it is lowered down a step", 5; "the sound of a sack being lowered down a step", 7). The laugh that stops at the rib comes six times before Coralie's hand gets there first in chapter 10. The word "rib" appears 46 times and "hand" about 300, one in every 150 words.

What I would do: for each refrain keep the first statement, the turn and the payoff. The rib-and-laugh run would be chapter 1, chapter 10 and chapter 9's oven, no more.

**8. Significance announced with "never", "nobody" and a count of years. (throughout)**

> "Nobody in Peyrebarthe had ever heard Maylis's mouth forbid anything." (5)

> "Maylis had never smiled at Jeannot Castagnet in her life, and the whole café was watching her do it." (5)

By my count: "nobody" 60 times, "nothing" 56, "never" 43, and a number of years ("for eighteen years", "in twelve years", "eleven Decembers") about 35 times. It is the narrator's standard way of saying that a moment matters: it had never happened, and now it did. Where the count is a fact the plot uses, as with 2:51 every night for twelve years, it is right. Elsewhere it is a drum roll before a moment that was already landing. The window going "all the way down, into the door" needs no help from "Maylis hadn't known it could", though that one is funny and can stay.

What I would do: go through every "never" and every count of years and keep those that give information.

**9. The narrator steps forward. (3, 5, 6, 9, 14)**

> "The other Maylis Lesca was the one whose village had its flood in 2014 and made her mayor. Call her Lesca." (3)

This is the only sentence in the book addressed to the reader, and it opens the chapter with a summary of what the chapter then shows very well. Smaller cases of the same hand: the one-sentence identity card dropped after a name ("She ran the care home down in the Port", 3, stuck on after a pulse reading; "He kept the café's score pad", 5; "Sarthou was Jeannot's cousin", 6; "Her mother lived on the other side of the yard and kept her ponies on the flood fields", 9, six chapters after we met her). And in chapter 14 a "you" that appears nowhere else: "the kind you turn by hand", "the way you carry a child".

What I would do: open chapter 3 on Lesca waking under the wrong roof and let the name arrive in the second paragraph without the instruction. Fold each identity card into something the person does. Take the "you" out of chapter 14.

**10. Slips of usage and consistency. (various)**

> "Josiane took a loaf out of the box" (17)

The bread has stood in a crate on the counter three times before this. The rest, in one list:

- **Figures.** Pulses are in figures everywhere (112, 130, 64, 34) except "'Ninety, and coming down'" (3).
- **Days.** "Somebody had been under there with her since Tuesday" (2): the first time was 2:51 on Wednesday morning. The book is strict about dates everywhere else, so this shows.
- **The land.** "flood fields" (ten times), "the village field" (3, 5), "the low fields" (2). One place, three names.
- **The river gauge.** "the red line on the pillar of the old gate" (3, 15), "the marker post" (5, 7, 9, 14), "her father's line" (9). I could not tell whether Maylis's village has a pillar, a post or both.
- **"the hunters' huts"** (12), plural. Everywhere else there is one hut and one man, and the whole sum depends on that.
- **"gate" doing two jobs.** "she came down and locked the platform gate behind her" (12), at the moment the floodgate matters most.
- **Council time.** "Council is at 6:30" (6); "met on Tuesday at 6" (16).
- **British and American.** The spelling and most of the vocabulary are British (torch, pavement, windscreen, rubbish bin, mum, colour, metre). Against that: "dish towel" (seven times), "truck" (three), "baby carriage" (twice), "grass trimmer".
- **The driven body.** The convention is "the mouth", "the legs", "the eyes" for a body somebody else is driving, and it is a good one. It slips: "Maylis felt her legs turn towards the café" (5), "Maylis's mouth spoke without her" (5), "her mouth hadn't said another word" (8). In the same chapters "Lesca" is the subject of sentence after sentence, 42 times in chapter 5: "Lesca didn't know there was another half. Lesca filled the spoon."
- **Screens.** Messages, the pad and the file are in italics. Caller names are not ("The screen said Castagnet, house", 14).
- **Unclear sentences.** "No job on that bank had ever taken her the time she gave it" (14): longer or shorter? "She wrote the motion in" (3). In the prologue, "She held it for three breaths. Then she climbed back down to the van and turned it around": the first "it" is the handle and the second is the van.
- **Names.** Maylis, Maïté, Marie-Jo, Madame Etcheto and Madame Darrigrand; Jean, Jeannot and Josiane. Chapter 4 puts Marie-Jo and Maïté in consecutive scenes with Maylis.

What I would do: one pass with this list. Pick "flood fields" and one gauge. Decide British or American for the four nouns.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Jeannot's numbered speech**, and above all the six points down the phone at 5 in the morning, ending "'And six. Bugger off.'" (10). It is why the reader wants him alive.
2. **The shopping pad**, from "'I don't cook Either. There's no recipe for it'" (1) through the two ticked boxes (4) and "me, as a distance" (9) to "'I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it'" (17).
3. **The square after the sentence** (15): the deputy turning the sheet over to see if there is more on the back, the sentence travelling "row by row, at walking pace", "A child asked what the lady had done. Somebody told the child", and Marie-Jo's "'Somebody has to stack them.'"
4. **The corridor** (14): Monsieur Daguerre's "'Here'", the bins, the man "who weighed nothing", Kévin counting five fingers in his own headlights, and its answer in 17: "'I wanted six, Maylis.'"
5. **Maylis measuring the world in her jobs**: the trailer of leaves (8), "She had worked the roadsides out at the speed of the village tractor. Inguma was doing them on foot" (6), "It wasn't much. She took more than that to the dump in a morning" (17). This is the narrator's voice at its best, and it is the model for how the rest should trust the reader.
