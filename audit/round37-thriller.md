# Round 37 — critic, lens: Thriller

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. About 43,600 words.

Overall, as a thriller: **7**. Good, and one notch under the bar. It frightens me for a marriage as well as Dark Matter does, and it prices its central choice harder than Crouch ever does. It does not drive like him between the Sunday kitchen (ch. 9) and the Friday vigil (ch. 12), and the scene the whole catastrophe hangs on is 150 words long.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes — 7.5.** The private stake is the one that frightens: "It's a whole life with one hand. You eat at my table, and I tip the spoon." The public stake stays a figure that gets recited ("forty houses, and the care home, with forty-two people in their beds").
- **Tension — 7.** Peaks at 9: "Lesca took the body up one stair. Maylis's arm pulled it back down." Troughs at 5: "'Then it should have gone last Monday.'"
- **Progression — 6.** "'On Friday night I'm coming across with you, and I won't be sent home.'" That is said at the end of ch. 9 and done in ch. 13. About 10,000 words lie between, and the double is in the body for none of them.
- **Comedy — 7.5.** "'It's adultery whichever she's after, and it's in that house.'"
- **Characters — 8.5.** "'I know what you picked. I was the other one.'"
- **The switch between dark and comic — 8.** In the flooded corridor: "'Here,' he said. 'I know you are.'" The bingo-night joke comes back as the thing that hurts.
- **Prose — 7.5.** At its best: "the car door shutting on a pillow". At its worst it explains its own climax: "This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel."
- **The ending — 8.** "She heard the passage, then the front door, then the car door shutting on a pillow." It costs. It also takes ten farewells to get there.
- **Clarity — 6.5.** "'The count tells it when to take the visitor home,' Lesca said. 'It doesn't keep it off. 2:51 is my minute.'" A rule the climax depends on, arriving past the halfway mark, the ninth or tenth the reader has been handed.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. From ch. 5 I read to the end of ch. 8 without stopping, and again from the 5 a.m. phone call in ch. 10 to "Forty-one".

**Would I press it on a friend?** On the friend who loves The Good Place, yes, this week. On the friend who reads Crouch for the speed, no: I would have to tell them to push through the Tuesday of ch. 9, and a thriller you have to apologise for is not being pressed.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch as a thriller. Level with him on the marriage. Above him on the choice.**

- **Premise speed: level.** The prologue puts the dilemma on the table in 338 words and ends "It rang once." The double speaks through the mouth at the end of ch. 2, about 4,000 words in; Dark Matter has its abduction no sooner. Chapter last lines are Crouch's kind and nearly all work: "'Come back across,' her mouth said. Maylis hadn't said it." / "She hadn't told them to." / "'Are you coming up?'" / "LESCA, MAYLIS." / "The arms of the chair were warm."
- **The premise stops escalating: below.** Crouch makes the device itself worse as the book goes on: one Jason becomes a hundred, one rewritten timeline becomes the end of the world, again and again. Here the device does its worst work by ch. 9 (the double at the table, on the stairs, on the landing, out of the body with Coralie's weight on her wrist). After that the premise is a date in a diary. The last third runs on High Noon's engine, not Dark Matter's.
- **Fear for the marriage: level.** Jason2 in Daniela's kitchen is the scene Crouch readers remember. Ch. 5's stairs and ch. 8's landing are that scene, and better built, because the wife can feel both women in one wrist: "'That's her,' she said. 'In this hand.'"
- **The reversal: level in conception with The Good Place, below in staging.** "This is the Bad Place" is said by a person to a room. The book has that line, "You're the water", and stages it superbly as a misfire at the bingo. But the reader was given the reversal a chapter earlier, as subtraction on a marker post.
- **The choice: above.** Crouch lets his man keep the family. The Good Place made Chidi's inability to choose both the joke and the damnation; this book does the same with "Either" and then sends the bill: "That was Either, with the river in it." A heroine who avoids pulling the lever so that "it wouldn't be me", saves a man who "was in no danger", and loses the wife for it: that is a harder finish than either comparison risks.

**Where the reader is afraid, and of what.** Of the thing's back in ch. 1 (not after: by ch. 5 Inguma is a bailiff). Of Lesca getting up those stairs. Of the pulse chart, 64 down to 34. Of the rib ("Her mouth tasted of coins"). Of Coralie "down there" under the gap. Never of Larrère, and never really for the forty houses.

**Where a Crouch reader puts it down.** Ch. 9, from the association voting through a car window to Arlette at the tap. Second place: the first 600 words of ch. 12.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The hinge of the whole plot is played in 150 words and decided by a tic. (Ch. 12, 5 p.m.)**
"'Either,' Maylis said. / 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. 'That's how the vote left it.' / He was off the platform and down the steps before she could take it back."
Since ch. 4 the book has told us the answer is this exact moment: "opened on Friday afternoon. In daylight, while the tide is going out … with the fields cleared and nobody on them." At 5 p.m. she has the handle fitted, daylight, and the one man standing above ground on the boards beside her. This, not 3 a.m., is the central choice, and the book knows it, because Madame Darrigrand says so in ch. 17: "You had the key on your belt then." But on the platform nothing equal to the stakes is pushing back. Her job is lost in January anyway. Kévin released her that same afternoon: "Don't keep a gate shut for me." Her bet that she can stay awake and keep the bank whole, which is the only good reason to spare the fields, is not on the page. So she reads as folding for no force, and for the rest of the night a thriller reader is annoyed with her instead of afraid for her.
*Do:* give this scene the length of the stairs scene. Put the forces on the platform: Larrère's car a hundred metres off, the folder, her wager on staying awake said in so many words. Let her turn it, click by click, with him watching, so that "Either" stops a hand that was moving. And move Kévin's "Don't keep a gate shut for me" from the sand bin to the net scene after dark. Then at 5 p.m. his name on that sheet is still live, and his release arrives when Jeannot is already under his trapdoor and it can buy nothing.

**2. The engine is switched off for a quarter of the book. (Ch. 9 after the kitchen, to the end of ch. 12.)**
Lesca is expelled on Sunday morning with Coralie's weight on her wrist, announces "I won't be sent home", and is not in the body again until 2:52 on Friday night. What fills the gap is procedure: a petition, a vote, a list, a form from Hontaube, a rehearsal with tape. Each scene is well made; in sequence the reader is waiting for an appointment. The two scenes that do hold (Coralie's "2:51", the doorstep "Come back across the landing") are two-handers about the past and the marriage, not the threat.
*Do:* take 1,500 words out between the vote and the vigil. Arlette at the tap ("She hasn't got stamps, she's got the count") goes. The failed words on Tuesday night are reported in the box anyway; cut the bedroom half. Halve the Pau rehearsal down to the tape strips and "Eight." Replace the Hontaube call with fault 3's scene.

**3. The village refuses her offstage, in one sentence. (Ch. 12.)**
"She had tried the café first, but the nine who had signed on Tuesday had watched Jeannot's hand go up. A vote was a vote."
By Friday this village has watched a prophecy come true at its own café door ("I wrote that eight days ago, Guy"), has water in its cellars and a mattress going upstairs. That it lets her sit on the bank alone on the named night is the least credible link in the chain, and the book steps over it. It is also the scene that would make ch. 17 bite: the people who blame her are the people who would not come.
*Do:* play it. Friday noon, in the café, with the pad open at the cross. Each of the nine says no for a reason of their own. It costs no more words than the Hontaube call and it has opponents in the room.

**4. The rule that kills is waived for Lesca, and the bill is shown too late to frighten. (Ch. 13, ch. 15.)**
"She knew what that cost. She had seen the card with her name on it." Then: "'You can put that in the minutes as apologies received.'" She walks out and wakes in Bayonne. The dread built since "The one it cannot take home dies in her own bed" is refunded with a quip, and the price ("'Twelve. One, two.' Somebody pulled a chair in to a table") is not called until the top of ch. 15, after ch. 14 has already shown us the bed.
*Do:* keep the quip, move the call. End ch. 13 on the voice dropping her name and calling twelve, and the chair being pulled in. The reader then goes into ch. 14 knowing a seat has been taken by room twelve, and Nadine's line "she went back down for twelve" turns Coralie's absence behind the fire door into the most frightening stretch in the book.

**5. The book's one great reversal arrives as a sum. (Ch. 7.)**
"In one day the river had made up forty-five centimetres, and now it was back where the file said it should be." Then the narrator says it flat: "The rain came when Lesca came. When she was kept out, it stopped. A flood was coming on the 11th because Lesca had brought it over with her."
A metre, seventy, fifty-five, forty-five: the reader is checking arithmetic at the moment the floor should go. And once the narrator has stated it, "You're the water" in ch. 8 is a repeat.
*Do:* keep three facts (the dry road, the cross, rain by noon) and the two nights she pushed. Drop the centimetres. Let her reach the thought without the narrator finishing it, on the trapdoor, in Lesca's rain. The first time the sentence is said whole is then at the bar, to a hall that thinks she is thirsty.

**6. The rule the climax rests on is given by fiat, and stated two ways. (Ch. 9.)**
"'One night matters. If I'm in your village at 2:51 on Friday night, your bank goes, as mine was going. If I'm not, it holds.'" Lesca has no means of knowing this; the book certifies it with another rule ("Nothing had stopped a word of that"). And ch. 7's evidence says something different: rain follows any night Maylis reaches the chair, which is why it rains on Wednesday the 2nd with Lesca at home, and all the week of the 7th with Lesca never in the village. The Friday plan (stay out of the chair) and the Friday rule (Lesca in the village) are not the same rule. Then ch. 14 shrugs: "it had come too late: the river was through the bank already." The reader was given a hope to hold through the vigil and cannot say what would have counted as winning.
*Do:* one rule, said once, and earned from what Maylis has seen: the nights she reaches the chair, it rains. Count the rules in the box scenes and cut whichever the climax does not use.

**7. Both peak chapters stop to explain themselves. (Ch. 13, ch. 14.)**
Ch. 13, at the eighth ring: "But the motion she had written at her own kitchen table was for a seat and a bed. There was no man in it … This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel." Ch. 14, at the handle: "Lesca had let his phone ring once and then turned the handle. Maylis had let it ring nine times, and Jeannot was alive." And the job sheet at full sprint: "Three minutes to get there. Two to get him up the ladder, if he climbed. Three back. That made eight."
The image already did the work: "the reflector was red, and no nearer than it had been twelve years ago."
*Do:* in ch. 13 go from that reflector straight to "'I'm letting go.'" In ch. 14 keep the clock stamps and "She wanted it not to be her", which is the book in seven words, and cut the comparisons and half the sums.

**8. The last chapter is a roll-call. (Ch. 17.)**
Larrère and Sarthou, Madame Darrigrand, Kévin, Maïté's bus, the café, Marie-Jo's chair, Coralie, the box, the priest, Jeannot. Ten farewells in 2,800 words, after a climax that ended three chapters back. "'I said that house,' she said. 'I only had the one name wrong.'" is a good line that the momentum cannot afford.
*Do:* Darrigrand's indictment, Kévin's "I wanted six", Coralie, the box, the grave. Fold the bus into one sentence seen from the café door, and let Marie-Jo's chair go.

**9. The same answer is withheld four times, and the reader has had it since the prologue.**
Ch. 3: "'Did you know he was out?'" Ch. 6: "'When you stood on that platform, did you—'" and the bell. Ch. 8: "What is he to you?" and the mouth stays shut. Ch. 9: "'That's not a no.'" / "Lesca said nothing." We watched her look down the track on page one. The first silence is irony; the fourth is stalling.
*Do:* in ch. 6 let Larrère finish the question before the hand rings him off. A whole council hearing the mayor's own hand silence "did you see his van" is an event with consequences. Cut the ch. 8 repeat.

**10. The box is a doorway, not a mechanism, and the fork is never accounted for.**
"'I don't know why it comes. That I can say.'" The book later answers that for Maylis's village and never for Lesca's: why did one have its flood in 2014 and the other a dry winter? The card that says *left* rides in the chest pocket to the last page ("with the card from the steel room") and does nothing. A Crouch reader expects the device to be squeezed.
*Do:* one exchange in ch. 9. Maylis asks "Then who brought yours?" and the truth rule either passes Lesca's answer or stops it. The gap becomes the book's, not the reader's.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The owner's right hand**, everywhere it acts: the stair post in ch. 5 ("cut and all"), the wrist on the landing in ch. 8, the handbrake in ch. 13.
2. **The pulse chart under "Back at 2"** and its cross-cutting in ch. 6: 64, 58, 47, 36, 34.
3. **The trap that every rescue tightens**: "I forbid you to go" → "On Friday the 11th I'll be in my hut" → the 5 a.m. call that scares the ducks → "My phone stays in the van" → "don't come down in that van". And Bordenave's cross, which is at once the clue and Jeannot's reason to vote Shut.
4. **The reflector.** "Then the water rose up the door and covered it, and the red light went out." And its return: "It hadn't changed. She was awake, and this night was hers."
5. **Ch. 14 from "Where's Coralie?" to "Forty-one"**: the empty chair going over in the water, "Pick that chair up", Kévin's five fingers at 4, the hatbox on the wardrobe.
