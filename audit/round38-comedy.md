# Round 38 — critic's report, Comedy lens

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** "It's a whole life with one hand. You eat at my table, and I tip the spoon." (9) The loss is put in kitchen terms, so it is felt.
- **Tension: 7.** "The gate was six hundred metres behind her. The red reflector was four hundred metres in front. The bank had six minutes left." (14) That is a 9. Chapters 7 and 9 are a 5, and they sit in the middle of the book.
- **Progression: 6.5.** "So I'm asking. Don't come on Friday." (9) It is the third time she has asked, with the same answer.
- **Comedy: 7.5.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (6) At its best it is as good as the comparison. It is not at its best in 2, 7 and most of 9.
- **Characters: 8.** "It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you." (17)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.5.** "'Who's dead?' said Jeannot." (10), one paragraph after the torch finds the sole of his boot.
- **Prose: 7.5.** "It wasn't much. She took more than that to the dump in a morning." (17) Against that: "Inguma had gone to count the corn, so nothing would take Lesca home tonight." (8)
- **The ending: 8.5.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" (17)
- **Clarity: 6.5.** "'The count tells it when to take the visitor home,' Lesca said. 'It doesn't keep it off.'" (9) A rule the reader needed in chapter 4 is handed over in chapter 9.

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. From chapter 10 I did not stop.

Would I press it on a friend? Yes, and I would tell them it starts being itself in chapter 4. That sentence is the problem.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Half a step below The Good Place as comedy. Level with it on the moral problem. Above it at the climax.** Crouch is not funny, so on this lens there is nothing of his to beat; where he is ahead is the chapter-end, and chapters 7 and 9 here do not end the way his do.

What puts it there:

- **Level: the flaw is the joke and the joke is the plot.** Chidi cannot choose, and that is why he is damned. Maylis cannot choose, and the book walks one word from a shopping pad ("Eggs is what Either gets in this house") to a gendarme ("I cannot summon Either") to the platform ("'Either,' Maylis said. 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said") to the phone ("Both") to the kitchen ("That was Either, with the river in it"). The show resets its damage every season. Here a woman of ninety-four stays dead. On this one thing the book is the harder of the two.
- **Level: the moral sum is made funny without being made small.** The show stages the trolley problem as slapstick. This book stages it as paperwork: a stone that needs a verb ("It reads that she opened him"), a form that should have gone last Monday, "It's four when you're the one counting. When it's your house it's one, and they knock it down." None of it shrinks the death.
- **Above: jokes left standing in the room with the dead.** "'Here,' he said. 'I know you are.'" "'Then they'll want the bins out.'" The deputy mayor turning the sheet over "to see whether there was more on the back." The show cuts from pain to a gag. This book makes the gag carry the pain, and it does it five or six times in chapters 13 to 15 without a miss.
- **Below: people who are funny in themselves.** The show has six people who are funny about anything. This book has four: Coralie, Jeannot, Arlette, Madame Etcheto. The two leads are funny as mechanisms (one has a word, the other has a procedure), and the second rank of villagers has one tic each.
- **Below: speed.** The show's premise is a joke in its first minute. Here the comedy the premise exists for, a mayor in a roadmender's mouth, starts in chapter 5, a quarter of the way in.
- **Below: the reversal.** The show delivers "this is the Bad Place" as a scene, in a line of dialogue, with a laugh in it. This book delivers "she is the flood" as a sum done silently in the rain (fault 1).

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. The book's reversal is delivered as arithmetic. (7)

> "Yesterday morning Bordenave had found the river a metre under the line and the road dry. Lesca's file had said seventy, so he had drawn his cross. Today the post said fifty-five. In one day the river had made up forty-five centimetres, and now it was back where the file said it should be."
>
> "The rain came when Lesca came. When she was kept out, it stopped. A flood was coming on the 11th because Lesca had brought it over with her."

This is the moment everything before it gets re-read: the rescuer is the disaster. It has no scene. Nobody says anything. There are four paragraphs of working, then the answer, in the narrator's voice. It sits in the one chapter where nobody is good company: two nights of pushing, coins in the mouth, pink in the sink, a walk with a watch. I stopped smiling at "No sacks" and did not start again until Marie-Jo asked about the toothache.

The instrument to do it as comedy is already on the table: the café's pad. Bordenave has a cross against Friday the 4th and a wet cellar on Saturday. Play it on his cellar steps. He or the dealer says the innocent thing that does the sum ("She was right every day she was in you. The two days you were yourself, the road dried"), as a point about betting on the mayor. Maylis hears it with Lesca driving and the jaw not hers. Keep "She had nobody to say it to and no mouth to say it with." Cut the working to two sentences. The reversal then lands the way the comparison lands its own: out loud, from somebody who does not know what they have said.

### 2. Coralie leaves the room on a reproach seven scenes running. (4 to 10)

> "Coralie picked up the bowl of coffee and poured it down the sink." (9)
>
> "'I asked where you'd be,' Coralie said. She went up to the spare room." (10)

Chapter 4: she tears off the page and takes the envelope. Chapter 5: she waits on the fourth stair. Chapter 7: she takes the pillow. Chapter 8: she shuts the spare-room door. Chapter 9: the coffee, then the pencil left across the pad. Chapter 10: the spare room again. Each one is good. Seven in a row is one beat played seven times, and she says "sixteen years", "never once", "you'll have to ask" or "for once" in nearly every one of them.

Coralie is the funniest person in the book. The oven door is the best running joke in it and she gets it three times in seventeen chapters. From chapter 4 she is a wit used as a grievance, and she stops being company. The comic scenes with her stop; they do not build.

Chapter 10's exit is the one to cut. It repeats chapter 9's pad scene ("And then there's me, as a distance") two days later with the same joke ("The van will be at the gate from dusk"). End the bandage scene on her hand flat on the rib while Maylis laughs. That gives the reader one whole warm scene before the flood, and it makes "Come back across the landing" on the doorstep and the bag in chapter 17 cost more. Then take the stated reproach out of two more scenes and let the exit do it.

### 3. The comedy the premise is for starts a quarter of the way in, and chapter 2 is carrying freight. (1 to 4)

> "'It's a thing you can learn, waking up inside your dreams. The phone goes off on the hour and you count. Five, and you're awake. In a dream the hand comes out wrong.'" (2)

Chapter 2 has to plant eels, finger-counting, the way into the dream, Madame Etcheto's words, the bingo of the dead, two more pictures in the head and the envelope, in 1,700 words. Only the room-twelve scene is funny ("She reported him. She said he had put it off its count, and it came at 1"). The Kévin scene is an explainer given to a likeable boy. The sash picture and the grey-water picture earn nothing the stone has not already earned.

The first line that could only be in this book is "There's nobody in the chair." / "Next item," in chapter 4. The first scene of two women in one mouth is chapter 5.

Cut the second and third pictures. Let Kévin count on the hour without the lecture and have her ask one question. Bring "Come back across" forward by a third of a chapter. The reader should be in the steel-room meeting by word 7,000, not 9,000.

### 4. Chapter 9 runs the same argument three times, and the chair scenes stop building. (9)

> "'Then stay in your own bed on Friday,' Maylis said." (Sunday night)
>
> "'So I'm asking. Don't come on Friday.'" (Wednesday night)

"Then stop coming" was chapter 4. Chapter 9 has the kitchen fight over the mouth, then a chair scene, then the association, the yard, the stove, the pad, then a second chair scene. The first chair scene exists to let Lesca say standing up what she denied sitting down. The second does the real work ("To stay. That's the one that comes out") and ends on the card with the name. The steel-room meeting was the funniest scene in the book in chapter 4. In chapter 9 it comes back twice with the comedy drained and the form unchanged.

Merge the two chair scenes into the Wednesday one. "It comes with me" can be the first thing that comes out whole when she stands up. The chapter loses 300 words and a repeat, and the hall of the dead arrives as the only thing that room does that night.

### 5. The bingo-night climax needs two footnotes, because the rule of the count is not settled. (8, 9)

> "Lesca hadn't finished the words, so Inguma had nothing to count and would take her home."
>
> "Inguma had gone to count the corn, so nothing would take Lesca home tonight."

The scene is the comic peak of the book: a woman holding her own jaw shut while a hall decides she has toothache, then that she is thirsty, then that she is in love. Its sting is that a kind old woman finishes the prayer for her and undoes the whole evening. The narrator explains that sting twice in the middle of the farce. A joke with a gloss on each side is half a joke.

The cause is upstream. For Madame Etcheto the count delays the thing's arrival. For Lesca it delays her removal. Maylis then tries the words in chapter 9, they fail, and Lesca corrects the rule. The reader has been unsure since chapter 4.

Say the rule once, in the meeting in chapter 4, as one of Lesca's points: it comes at its minute whatever anyone says; the count only decides when it takes the visitor home. Then cut both narrator sentences in 8. "'That's till morning,' Madame Etcheto told Coralie, and settled her hands on the duck" is the gloss, and it is the funnier one. Chapter 9's failed attempt can go, or stay as two lines without the lecture.

### 6. The second rank has one tic each, and Larrère on Maylis's side never gets his egg-timer speech. (throughout)

> "'Your father slept on that platform with the gate locked shut, and he never rang me once.'" (12)
>
> against "'Eleven years I've been timed like an egg. I have sentences in that register with no ends on them.'" (6)

Sarthou speaks in reports. Daguerre says "Here." Bordenave asks how to put it. Josiane has adultery. Marie-Jo calls numbers. By chapter 12 the reader can say the line before the character does. That is fine for Daguerre, because the tic is the point and it pays twice. It is thin for the café.

The clearest case is Larrère. In Lesca's village he surprises: off the timer he turns out to have eleven years of unfinished sentences, and he is right about the three hundred hectares. In Maylis's village he is a window, "I'll be brief", "your father" and a folder. He is the obstacle for twelve chapters and is funny in one posture. The comparison's people are each wrong in their own way; this Larrère is only wrong in the author's way.

Give him one moment through the gap where he is right and it stings, the equal of the egg speech. Chapter 12 is the place: he has an orange warning, not a red, and a man on the fields. Let him say what a president who opens on orange gets called in the café by Easter. And give Josiane or Bordenave one line each that is not their line.

### 7. In chapter 6 the joke does not feel the danger. (6)

> "Maylis had never kept anybody waiting in her life, so she took her time over it."

Maylis knows by now that her own body is dying in her own bed. Coralie has her thumb over the doctor's number at 36. The council scene around those cuts is the funniest ten minutes in the book, and it plays as leisure: she pins the timer, she lets Larrère run. I smiled all the way through and was not afraid once. The three cuts to the bedroom read as a clock on the wall, in summary, from outside ("She had made a joke of it then").

Put the failing body into the comic business. The left hand that pins Lesca's wrist is the one she has had to tell twice. Let it fail in front of the table: she cannot raise it for the vote, and the secretary counts the one hand that is up, which is Lesca's. The two-hands joke gets a harder version and the bedroom gets into the room.

### 8. The stuck word is used seven times and broken sentences are everywhere. (4, 5, 9)

> "'It's the ti—' Lesca said. She started again, and it stuck on the same word." (9)

Chapter 4 has Either stuck, "It's—" stuck, "because no—" stuck. Chapter 5 has "Ei—" in the square, where the rule of the room does not apply, so the reader wonders whether it does. Chapter 9 has "the ti—", "I w—" and "to get—". Add Maïté, who breaks every other sentence ("In his— In his minute"), and the secretary ("Madame le—"), and the device that should be saved for a revelation is the book's commonest punctuation. The last one, Coralie's "The—" at the oven, is the best line in the book and deserves a clearer run-up.

Keep three: Either in 4, "because no—" in 4, "to get—" / "To stay" in 9. Cut the "Tell me it's Tuesday" demonstration, "the ti—" and "I w—". In chapter 5, have Lesca refuse the word, not choke on it. Maïté keeps hers; it is how she talks.

### 9. Coralie is used as a leaflet. (7)

> "'He said his mouth tasted of coins. He coughed, and it came out pink on the sheet. His breath stopped halfway down, and then at his collar.'"

Two pages later: "Her mouth tasted of coins." "It was pink." "It stopped halfway down." Three symptoms planted, three collected, in order. She says it in the doorway with a pillow under her arm, straight after "But you'll have to ask", which is the line the scene should end on. The reader hears the plot being loaded.

Plant it in chapter 1, where she straps the rib before she allows the laugh. One line there ("I've seen one go through. He was reaching for his glasses") is a joke with teeth and does the work. In 7 she then needs only "No sacks. And you don't push."

### 10. The narrator explains the gag after the scene has made it. (4, 5, 7)

> "She had written that word on the shopping pad on Tuesday, when she wanted the duck. It had always come when she needed it. It had never been true, and it wasn't true now. She did want one of them, badly." (4)
>
> "She had lain still for the village's sake. It was going to cost her Coralie." (5)
>
> "Lesca had walked her down the track with a watch because she wanted to save Jeannot. But he would only need saving if Lesca was here on Friday night." (7)

In each case the scene has just shown it. The first follows the word sticking in her throat. The second follows Lesca's foot on the stair. The third follows a whole chapter of it. Cut all three. The narrator's commentary earns its place when it is itself a joke: "For sixteen years Maylis had sat down and said nothing, in the wrong kitchen" and "Maylis had crossed from one village to the other, and Josiane had still got there first" stay.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The steel-room meeting in chapter 4**, from "A meeting needs somebody in the chair" to "There's nobody in the chair." / "Next item."
2. **The line of Either**: eggs, "I cannot summon Either to the station", "'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said", "Both", "I know that word. I've cooked it." Not one use to be added or removed.
3. **The oven door, three times**, and above all the third, which she cannot finish.
4. **The phone call at the end of chapter 10**: the drowning told flat, then "'Who's dead?' said Jeannot", the six points, "And six. Bugger off", and "Jeannot had just sworn at her in six points, and he was alive."
5. **Every joke in chapters 13 to 15**: "apologies received", the man with his arm around his card, "'Here.' 'I know you are.'", the bins, Kévin's "Five", the deputy mayor turning the sheet over, the man who clapped, "I've got nothing on the sheet for this", "Somebody has to stack them." Nothing here weakens the dark. Change nothing.

Close behind: the stone's verb ("she opened him") and its answer in 16 ("*Left*"); "Good night, Maylis"; Hontaube's "You'll belong to us"; letter forty-two.
