# Critic's report: Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. 44,549 words, about 4,575 sentences. Counts below are mine, taken from those files.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "The trapdoor was twenty-five minutes away, on foot, in the dark, with the dip under water. The bank had ten." Exact, but the one stake aimed at the heroine herself, her name on a stone dated eighteen days off, is cancelled in ch. 4 by "It isn't a grave. It's for a night."
- **Tension: 7.** "On the sixth ring the breath wouldn't come." Chapters 10 to 14 are at the bar. Chapters 6 and 9 run to a timetable.
- **Progression: 6.** "'It comes with me. What one of us has in her head, the other gets, through that chair.'" That is the fourth statement of one discovery in three chapters.
- **Comedy: 7.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" It works wherever a fixed manner meets a fact. It fails where the narrator explains the joke first: "the only form she had for it was a motion, the kind the council votes on."
- **Characters: 8.** "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'"
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'It's Saturday.' / 'Then they'll want the bins out,' he said." Said in a flooded corridor, while Coralie is still somewhere in the dark wing.
- **Prose: 7.** "It had been higher. It had left a line on the wallpaper a metre above the pillow." is what it can do. "Lesca tied the handkerchief around the palm, using the left hand and the teeth." is what it does for five chapters.
- **The ending: 8.** "She heard the passage, then the front door, then the car door shutting on a pillow."
- **Clarity: 6.** "Lesca kept the eyes on the patch and didn't answer him. Then she walked her up onto the bank, to the platform of the floodgate." The reader has to work out whose eyes, and who "her" is, in a sentence that only moves a body up a slope.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. From ch. 10 I would not have stopped. In ch. 5 I nearly did.

**Press it on a friend?** On a patient one, yes. Not on the Crouch reader the bar names, until chapters 5 to 9 read as easily as chapter 14.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, by one revision.** Level on chapter endings, above on economy, below on ease, and ease is what the comparison is for.

- **Below Crouch on ease.** A Dark Matter page never asks the reader who is acting. It has one "I", and the grammar is invisible. Here the premise, once in play, is carried by a two-subject sentence: "Lesca" does the verb, "the left hand" or "the mouth" is the instrument, and Maylis is "her". In ch. 5, "Lesca" appears 42 times in 2,968 words. The book's best comic day is told in its hardest grammar.
- **Below both on rules.** Crouch states a rule once, in a character's mouth, then lets it operate. The Good Place states a rule as a joke and never again. This book does that in ch. 4 ("All those in favour." "There are two of us."), then has the narrator restate each rule every time it fires: "Lesca hadn't finished the words, so Inguma had nothing to count and would take her home."
- **Level on endings, above on economy.** Almost every chapter ends on an image or a line that pulls ("'Forty-one,' Coralie said."). There is one "very" in the book and one "really", no "suddenly", no semicolon and no ellipsis, and no sentence about a pounding heart. Crouch has many.
- **Below The Good Place on voices.** Cover the name and its leads can be told apart from one line. Here Larrère, Jeannot, Sarthou, Josiane, Maïté and Arlette pass that test. Coralie, Maylis, Lesca, Marie-Jo, Darrigrand and Madame Etcheto speak the narrator's own clipped closing line.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The grammar of the driven scenes (ch. 5, 7, 8, 9; structural).**
"Lesca unlocked the box with the key from the belt, fitted the handle and began to turn it with the left hand. The chain came up wet. Lesca counted the clicks of the catch as it came."
Every routine movement costs a proper name and a definite-article limb. Across the book "Lesca" stands directly before one of a dozen plain verbs (walked, kept, held, took, put, turned, pulled, got, had, let, waited, said) 118 times, and "the left/right hand" occurs 50 times beside 39 of "her left/right hand". The convention also slips. Ch. 8: "By then Maylis's left hand had served eleven coffees and her mouth hadn't said another word", when Lesca is driving that hand. Ch. 5 has "Maylis felt her legs turn towards the café" and then "The legs stopped inside the door". Ch. 7: "Lesca kept to the grass between them. Her mother's ponies stood along the fence", where "her" reads as Lesca, whose mother's ponies went up a ramp years ago.
What I would do: keep one consciousness per scene, and one contested part. Chapter 13 already does this from the driver's side and reads cleanly. In chapters 5 to 9, narrate what Maylis feels her body do ("Her left hand took the key off her belt"). Name Lesca only when she speaks, decides or wants something. Keep "the mouth", "the jaw" for the instant of a fight over it. That halves the name count and removes the parsing.

**2. The narrator referees the rules (ch. 5 to 13; structural).**
Ch. 8: "Lesca hadn't finished the words, so Inguma had nothing to count and would take her home." Twenty lines on: "Inguma had gone to count the corn, so nothing would take Lesca home tonight." Then Madame Etcheto says it a third time: "That's till morning." Ch. 9: "Nothing had stopped a word of that." Ch. 13: "Inguma hadn't taken her home. She had held the wheel against it for a whole ring, and this was where that put her."
There are seven rules (stay seated and you drive; the owner keeps the right hand; standing, you cannot lie; the count sets the return; knowledge passes through the chair; the rain follows Lesca; resist the return and you are seated among the dead). Each is restated in narration at each use, usually on a ", so" (47 in the book).
What I would do: after ch. 4, delete every narration sentence that states a rule. Let one person say it once, in their own manner, as Madame Etcheto does. The ch. 13 sentence is the clearest case: she set that rule up in ch. 2 ("The one it cannot take home dies in her own bed"), Lesca repeated it in ch. 9, and the hall is in front of the reader.

**3. The two decisions are explained after they are made (ch. 13, ch. 17; structural).**
Ch. 13: "She had told Maylis there was no both. She had wanted both herself, the bed and Castagnet. But the motion she had written at her own kitchen table was for a seat and a bed. There was no man in it." And: "Twelve years ago she had let his phone ring once and then done what she had come to do. This was the same thing, done with a steering wheel."
The hinge of the book is argued by the narrator on Lesca's behalf, in a chapter otherwise counted in rings. Ch. 17 then gives Maylis's choice three glosses: to Kévin ("so that it wouldn't be me"), to Coralie ("I had to pick one. I picked"), to Lesca ("I drove past the handle to be the one who did"). Chapter 14 had already written it once, correctly: "She wanted it not to be her."
What I would do: in ch. 13 keep "Then she saw what she was holding. It was the wheel of a van with a dead engine and its brake on", the reflector "no nearer than it had been twelve years ago", and "I'm letting go." Cut the two reasoning paragraphs. In ch. 17 keep the Kévin line and cut the other two.

**4. One discovery, delivered four times (ch. 7, 8, 9; structural).**
Ch. 7: "The rain came when Lesca came. When she was kept out, it stopped. A flood was coming on the 11th because Lesca had brought it over with her." That is three sentences for one fact, after three paragraphs of sums in the pluperfect. Then ch. 8, "You're the water." Then ch. 9, Maylis to Coralie in three columns. Then ch. 9 again, Lesca in the chair.
The weakest version comes first and takes the surprise from the best one, which is three words at a bar.
What I would do: in ch. 7 give the data (Bordenave's cross, fifty-five on the post, rain by noon) and one sentence of conclusion. Keep the bar. Keep Coralie's chart, because she is the one who tests it. In the chair, keep only what is new: "One night matters."

**5. One rhythm for everything (whole book).**
Mean sentence length is between 8.7 and 11.4 words in every numbered chapter. Half of all sentences are eight words or fewer. About 2% reach twenty-five. The committee farce (ch. 3), the bedroom confession (ch. 11) and the evacuation (ch. 14) run at 9.9, 9.7 and 9.6. In narration, 17% of sentences begin "She" and well over half begin on the subject. Ch. 6: "She breathed in. The breath went all the way down and nothing caught. This body had all its ribs. The bed had one pillow."
The clipped line is right for chapters 12 to 14. Used everywhere, it leaves them nothing to accelerate into. Crouch gets his speed from contrast: a one-word paragraph against a forty-word run.
What I would do: let the comic scenes and the looks across a room run long, as ch. 15 does once: "She watched her sentence travel back through the square and down the road, row by row, at walking pace." Save the short line for the night.
The backfill adds to it: 607 uses of "had", 70 of them in ch. 3. Ch. 10: "Lesca had had forty houses to open the gate for. She had also had Coralie's car in that yard."

**6. Sentences that tell what the scene has just shown, and recaps at the joins.**
- Ch. 5: "Maylis knew what Lesca wanted. She had lain still for the village's sake. It was going to cost her Coralie." The foot on the stair has said it.
- Ch. 5: "She was still waiting for the woman who had ticked the box." Cut; "'Are you coming up?'" does it.
- Ch. 4 ends "She would rather it was Lesca, who signed things." Ch. 5 says in its third paragraph "she would rather the village was told by the one who signed things."
- Ch. 9: "She had seen them work twice. Lesca had said them through the kitchen door... Madame Etcheto had said them across the village hall..." A recap of two scenes the reader was at.
- Ch. 9: "You eat at my table, and I tip the spoon. You answer Coralie, and I've got the wrist she holds for my pulse, to pull away with. You pour the coffee, and I shut the tap." The character lists the book's own images back.
- Ch. 10: "She knew now why Inguma always came at 2:51, first to Lesca and then to her." The screen said 02:51 a page earlier.
- Ch. 12: "But she might not get another chance, and that frightened her more than asking did."
- Ch. 14: "She had put Lesca out, and it had come too late: the river was through the bank already."
- Ch. 4: "She believed the date. She wanted that gate opened on Friday afternoon as much as Lesca did. What scared her was Monday."
What I would do: cut all nine. Nothing is lost.

**7. The narrator's voice changes six times.**
- Ch. 0: "She was scared." The only named emotion in a page of pure action.
- Ch. 3: "The other Maylis Lesca was the one whose village had its flood in 2014 and made her mayor. Call her Lesca." The narrator turns to the reader, once, and hands over the premise as a briefing. The paragraph also gives away what the chapter then dramatises well ("That village hadn't had its flood yet").
- Ch. 6: three inserts from Coralie at the bedside ("The pad said 2. She would wait until 2.").
- Ch. 8: one from Arlette ("From the chair she couldn't hear the breath any more").
- Ch. 10: a scene from Coralie at the care home.
- Ch. 14: verbless fragments used nowhere else ("Torch up. Right arm across her side.").
What I would do: cut the ch. 3 opening paragraph and start on "She lay on her back with rain falling on the roof", letting the yellow coat and the single pillow name her. Cut "She was scared." Keep the bedside inserts, which earn their place by the pulse falling, but mark them the same way each time.

**8. Coralie almost never misses a line.**
Ch. 17, after two days in the same uniform: "I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it." That is three closers in a row. Also: "Eggs is what Either gets in this house." "I've had sixteen and a shopping pad." "And then there's me, as a distance." "I was the other one." And one construction three times in two chapters: "Don't give me the minutes." "Don't tell me what he was." "Don't give me the measurement."
Each line is good. Together they make her the book's wit, which is the narrator's wit, and it is the same from both Coralies.
She breaks once, in ch. 14: "and then the torch went and I couldn't—". It is her best moment, and it shows what the finished lines withhold everywhere else.
What I would do: let her break once more, in ch. 17 at the table. Keep one of the three "Don't give me". In ch. 17 keep "That was Either, with the river in it" and cut the two before it.

The with-tail and the clock opener are how nearly every scene starts: a person, a doorway, a time, a prop. What I would do: put the day, the hour and the village in a dateline over every chapter, as Recursion does and as ch. 1 already does, and strike the in-prose datelines ("It was 9 on Tuesday morning, and"). Cut one with-tail in two.

**10. Words and numbers doing two jobs; usage.**
- **Forty-one and forty-two.** Jeannot's letters and the care home's residents. Ch. 5 has "Put it in forty-two" and "what to do with forty-two people" forty lines apart. If the rhyme is meant, nothing on the page points to it. If not, renumber the letters.
- **Nineteen.** Days of rain, turns of the handle, sandbags, Coralie's age at her first shift: 19 uses, four meanings. Keep the first two.
- **Twelve.** Room, date, years, seats on the minibus, Jean's letters, the bingo call. Ch. 16's "they called twelve" has to be sorted from all of them.
- **Left and right.** Ch. 16: "'*Left*. He was left out there.'" "'It would have been right for both lines.'" Next to two cards reading *left* and *right*, and 89 left and right hands, this lands as a pun the scene does not want. Maylis's card is *left*, and she is the one who did not leave him.
- **Box.** The steel room, the handle's box, the tick boxes, the confession box, the cool boxes, the first-aid box. Ch. 5's "Lesca unlocked the box" comes a page before "Still in the box, were you?"
- **British base, American nouns.** Colour, metres, pavement, torch, windscreen, handbrake, pyjamas, programme; but "truck" (ch. 11 twice, ch. 14), "baby carriage" (ch. 17 twice), "dish towel" (7), "dump" (2). Ch. 14's "the lift they used for getting a resident out of it" reads as an elevator; it is a hoist.
- **Times.** "a.m." appears three times (ch. 1 dateline, ch. 5, ch. 14) and nowhere else, which leaves "At 5 on Friday it was light" (ch. 17, afternoon) against "At 5 she pressed Jeannot's number" (ch. 10, morning).
- **Pulse.** Figures everywhere ("112", "130", "34") except ch. 3: "'Ninety, and coming down.'"
- **Rank.** "Sarthou, the gendarme" (ch. 5) becomes "Adjudant Sarthou" once (ch. 6) and never again.
- **Ch. 6.** "Maylis had never kept anybody waiting in her life, so she took her time over it. She couldn't have stood as long as they could." The two sentences give opposite reasons for the same pause.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Room twelve, ch. 14**, from "Twelve was shut" to "'Forty-one,' Coralie said." Including the hatbox: "There was a hatbox on it. Nothing else."
2. **The oven door, three times** (ch. 1, 9, 17), and the third stopping at "'The—'".
3. **The square after the sentence, ch. 15.** The deputy mayor turning the sheet over to see whether there is more on the back, and "A child asked what the lady had done. Somebody told the child."
4. **Larrère at the garden gate, ch. 16.** "'I'll be brief,' he said." Then: "He said nothing."
5. **Coralie leaving, ch. 17.** "It wasn't much. She took more than that to the dump in a morning." through "the car door shutting on a pillow." (Change "dump" if the usage is settled as British. Change nothing else.)
