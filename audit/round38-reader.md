# Round 38 — Reader

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes — 7.** 'I'd have had you. You answer. But what I'd have had is a river over forty-two beds.' (9) One man against forty-two beds, and a bed against a village: always concrete, never abstract. Small beside Crouch, where the hero's own life is at risk from the first chapter.
- **Tension — 6.5.** 'So on Friday I won't lie down. I'll sit up in the van, awake, until 4, when it gives up.' (9) By then it has sat on her on a kitchen floor, at a council table and standing at a stair post. I was not asking whether the plan would fail, only when.
- **Progression — 6.5.** 'The door of the box opened and Inguma came in over the step.' (9, and again nine paragraphs of plot later in the same chapter.) Chapters 6 to 9 run the same three moves several times each.
- **Comedy — 8.** 'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.' (6)
- **Characters — 8.5.** 'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.' (8)
- **The switch between dark and comic — 8.** 'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—' (17) The joke I had smiled at three times is the thing that breaks.
- **Prose — 7.** 'Lesca had gone out of her the way warmth goes out of a chair, and those words were what she had left behind.' (14) Never once hard to read. Flat in places: 'She was scared.' (00)
- **The ending — 8.** 'I know what you picked. I was the other one.' (17)
- **Clarity — 5.5.** 'The count tells it when to take the visitor home,' Lesca said. 'It doesn't keep it off.' (9) I learn in chapter 9 what the words have been doing since chapter 2, and it is not what room twelve showed me.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. I was held from the last line of 2, slowed in the first third of 6 and the middle of 9, and did not put it down from 10 to 15.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who likes village comedy, yes, today. On a friend who reads Crouch, not yet: I would have to warn them about the rules.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Overall: below the bar on grip and clarity, level or above on people and comedy. About a 7.**

- **Below Crouch: one rule against thirteen.** Dark Matter gives me one box and one rule and then spends the book on consequences. Here I was still being handed rules at the two-thirds mark (list under fault 1).
- **Below Crouch: fear.** I was afraid three times: the pulse falling to 34 (6–7), the taste of coins (7), and 'And she hasn't come up' (14). Between chapters 3 and 9 I was curious and amused, not afraid.
- **Above Crouch: the wife.** Lesca's motive is the motive of the other Jason in Dark Matter exactly: the one who took the career and lost the marriage comes for the other one's bed. A reader who knows Crouch sees that by chapter 3. The book beats him on it at the landing, because his Daniela is fought over and Coralie judges: 'Which of you is that?' … 'That's her. In this hand.' (8)
- **Level with The Good Place: the moral problem as a comic habit.** Maylis is Chidi, and the gate is the trolley lever the series staged as a lesson. Here the lever sticks at nineteen turns and the inability to choose is a word on a shopping pad that later kills someone. That is heavier than the series and as funny.
- **Below The Good Place: reversals.** One reversal re-reads what came before ('You're the water'). The later reveals (the van was seen, Coralie knew, Larrère rang from that kitchen) change 2014, not my reading of the present.

**What I did not understand**

- What the green nets under Jeannot's floor are for, and why they are a threat.
- 'That's what you'd keep' (17).
- 'That was how long she had to find out whose stone it was' (1). Her own name is on it.

**Where I skimmed**

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The rules arrive in instalments and one of them contradicts what I was shown. (2, 4, 9 — structural)**
'She rings at 3.' (2) against 'The count tells it when to take the visitor home … It doesn't keep it off.' (9)
By chapter 9 I was carrying these:
- it comes at 2:51;
- lie still and you reach the chair, push and it leaves at 4;
- the chair passes knowledge;
- whoever stays sitting wakes in the other's body;
- the owner keeps the right hand;
- the words set when the visitor is fetched home;
- with no words it is twenty minutes;
- nobody can say an untruth standing in the steel room;
- the empty body cools;
- the one who cannot be taken home sits with the dead;
- the rain follows Lesca;
- long stays wear out the owner's hand;
- only one night counts.

Room twelve and Arlette's mother both show the words keeping it away. For the two women they do not, and I am told why seven chapters later, after Maylis has tried it and failed.

*Do:* chapter 4 already has Lesca reading out 'three points, and I'll say them once'. Make that list complete and final: it comes at 2:51 whatever is said, because that minute is hers; the words only decide when the visitor goes home. Then cut the failed experiment with the roadsides in 9. The Friday plan stops looking hopeless by accident, because Maylis and the reader both know exactly what sitting up can and cannot do.

**2. The climax rests on a rule nobody learns; it is announced. (9)**
'One night matters. If I'm in your village at 2:51 on Friday night, your bank goes, as mine was going. If I'm not, it holds.'
She cannot lie standing up, so I believe her. I do not know how she knows. It also cancels what chapter 7 made me work out, that every night she is kept out buys a dry day.

*Do:* derive it from what is already on the page. The gendarmes' sheet says '02:50. Water through the bank'. One line: she arrives each night carrying that minute, and on the 11th the minute is the bank.

**3. Chapters 6 to 9 loop. (structural)**
Two chair scenes in chapter 9 both end 'Inguma came in over the step'. Coralie comes three steps across a landing in 8, is asked in 12, and leaves in 17. The track is walked in 7, 12 and 14. Lesca drives for a day (5), Maylis drives for a day (6), Lesca drives again (7–8).

*Do:* merge the two chair scenes in 9 into one (the admission, 'To stay', 'Both', the hall with the card). With the roadsides experiment and the tap scene gone, 9 loses a third and Friday comes sooner.

**4. The one turn that re-reads the book is delivered as subtraction. (7)**
'In one day the river had made up forty-five centimetres, and now it was back where the file said it should be.'
Four figures in four sentences at the moment I should be feeling the floor go. I reread it to check the sum.

*Do:* the picture is already there: Bordenave's cellar, 'This floor was dry yesterday'. Let her get it from the dry road, the wet cellar and the hour the rain began, and drop the centimetres.

**5. Maylis risks dying in chapter 6 for a reason the book then makes unnecessary. (6, 10)**
'Maylis wanted to read the page with her own eyes: how much time there had been, and what was found at the hut.' Four chapters later 'the night of the flood came to Maylis anyway', free, through the chair.
The true reason is the next sentence: 'She also wanted one day as the woman who signed things.'

*Do:* make that the reason she goes. The file is what she finds once she is there.

**6. Chapter 1 hands me the plot's equipment as an inventory before I care. (1)**
'The bank was her job too: the care home at one end, then the patch she repaired every spring with four wheelbarrows of clay, then the floodgate.'
I came back to this paragraph four times in later chapters. That a raised gate drains the fields when the river is low and floods them when it is high I had to work out from Jeannot's complaint.

*Do:* say once, plainly, what the gate does to a high river and to a low one. Let her pass these places on her round, in order, instead of listing them.

**7. The eve of the flood opens with a rehearsal. (11)**
'He wanted the front row for the councillors and the emergency services, because it looked better on camera.'
I wanted Friday night and got tape. The second half of this chapter (the phone, 'Because you'd have told me') is the best scene in the book, and I reached it impatient.

*Do:* keep Darrigrand's step and Coralie's eight strips. Cut the kiss, the medal and the tent to a line each; chapter 15 plays them without help.

**8. I could not tell what Coralie's last question is worth. (17)**
'Stay.' / 'That's what you'd keep,' Coralie said.
I do not know what that reply means. Then 'What do you want, Maylis?' is answered with the grave, but she has already said 'Come back across' (12) and 'Stay' a page earlier. It reads as the third want, not the first, and I could not tell whether Coralie hears it as the answer she waited sixteen years for or as one more miss.

*Do:* let Coralie say in plain words why 'Stay' does not count and why the grave does.

**9. The paper in chapter 6 outruns me, and the nets are never explained. (6)**
'There are green nets under the floorboards in your hut, folded wet.'
A signed letter with no date, a blank sheet, a complaint against persons unknown and a threat about nets, in two pages. I never learned what the nets catch or whom Jeannot should fear. The scene matters: it is why he votes 'Shut'.

*Do:* one clause saying what the nets are for, the same offence Kévin could go to prison for.

**10. Forty-one and forty-two each mean two things. (1, 14)**
'I've paid for forty-one registered letters' and 'Forty-one,' Coralie said.
At the hardest line in the book I heard Jeannot's stamps.

*Do:* change the number of letters.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The landing (8).** Coralie taking the right wrist before she takes the fourth step.
2. **The phone (11).** '2:51. I've known that for twelve years.' and 'Because you'd have told me.'
3. **'Either' on the platform at 5 in the afternoon (12)**, with the handle fitted and Jeannot above ground, and its return as 'That was Either, with the river in it.'
4. **The corridor (14).** 'Here' / 'I know you are', the man who wants the bins out, Kévin counting to five.
5. **The confession and what follows it (15).** The sentence going back through the square at walking pace, the man who claps, 'until things are clearer', 'Somebody has to stack them.'

Close behind: the window going into the door (5), Larrère without his bell (6), 'You're the water' at bingo (8), 'apologies received' (13), and 'Call her Lesca' (3), which is the sentence that kept me from getting lost.
