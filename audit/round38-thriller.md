# Round 38 — critic, Thriller lens

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** Named, counted and personal: "'The gate, or Castagnet? Say which, Maylis. For once.'" They stay the size of one village, and a night that threatens forty houses and forty-two beds sends a bill for one.
- **Tension: 6.** At its best it is as good as anything in Crouch: "She sent nine, ten and eleven up the ramp to me in their chairs, and she went back down for twelve. Then the lights went out." At its worst the reader is nine hours ahead of the heroine: "There was still time to go down the track and knock. She didn't go."
- **Progression: 7.** Every chapter changes the board, and the middle turns it over: "The rain came when Lesca came." Then the double goes idle from Sunday the 6th to Friday the 11th, and three chapters follow the breach.
- **Comedy: 8.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'"
- **Characters: 8.** "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'"
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** In the flooded corridor: "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'" And at 4 a.m., a boy checking whether he is dreaming: "'Five,' he said."
- **Prose: 7.** Plain, fast and exact when it matters: "The street lamps of the Port went out one after another, starting at the bank end." It keeps one temperature from the café to the breach, so fear is reported more often than felt.
- **The ending: 8.** "'I know what you picked. I was the other one.'"
- **Clarity: 6.** The ground is clear from the first page. The rules are not: the one the climax turns on is a decree, "If I'm in your village at 2:51 on Friday night, your bank goes, as mine was going. If I'm not, it holds."

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. Chapters 12 to 14 in one go.
**Press it on a friend?** On one who loves The Good Place, yes. On one who wants the next Dark Matter, no.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, on this lens.** Good, not yet as good.

- **Below: the hero's last day.** In Dark Matter the ampoules run out in front of the reader and Jason spends every one on an attempt. This book names its ampoules, "Your rib will tell you how many nights you've got", spends them by chapter 7, and then sends its heroine into the last night with a plan that is to sit in a van. Crouch's people fail by acting. Maylis's worst hours are waiting.
- **Below: the rules.** Crouch gives one rule and shows what it costs the first time a door is opened. Here I count ten (the chair shares, the sitter drives, the owner keeps the right hand, the count delays, nobody lies standing up, the body left behind cools, words do not keep it off, the river comes with her, one night matters, the one who resists goes to the hall). Half arrive as an agenda read out by the antagonist.
- **Above: the marriage.** Daniela is deceived by the double. Coralie knows there are two and says which she prefers: "'I'd have this one again.'" I was more afraid for this marriage on the fourth stair than I ever was for Jason's.
- **Level: the reversal and the chapter ends.** Chapter 7 does what the first-season finale of The Good Place does: the rescuer is the disaster, and chapters 3 to 5 re-read. Larrère's kitchen clock does it again for "No one came to tell him." Fourteen of eighteen chapters end on a line that turns the page.
- **Above: the cost.** Dark Matter lets the family drive away. This one takes the wife, the job, the old woman and the village's good opinion, and leaves the heroine a grave to dig.

**Where a Crouch reader puts it down:** Tuesday 1 December (chapter 6, first three scenes); the middle of chapter 9; chapter 10's replay; the bus in chapter 17.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The hinge of the plot is one unweighted word. (Ch. 12)**
"'Either,' Maylis said. / 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. 'That's how the vote left it.'" At 5 p.m. she has daylight, the handle fitted, the catch in its first tooth and Jeannot above ground an arm's length away. She takes the handle off because he asks a question. Kévin has cancelled the eels an hour earlier, so nothing on the "shut" side has weight, and the reader spends the next nine hours annoyed with her and not afraid for her. She has a good reason and the page never gives it: if she keeps Lesca out tonight the bank holds, and an open gate drowns his hut and three hundred hectares for a flood she means to stop. Put that in her head at the handle. Let Jeannot's cross on the pad say it aloud. Then "Either" is a bet she cannot call, the reader cannot call it either, and the wait is dread.

**2. The Friday plan has a hole the book does not see. (Ch. 9, paid in ch. 12)**
"I can't push, and the words don't keep it off. So on Friday I won't lie down. I'll sit up in the van, awake, until 4, when it gives up." Inguma has already come to her awake ("She pushed until the church clock struck 4"), and Kévin said so in chapter 2: "I've had that twice, wide awake." Lesca's objection, "if you nod off at 2", makes sleep the danger. Then at 2:49 she is awake, counts five, and it lands anyway. So the first beat of the climax surprises nobody and is not her failure. One line mends it: the plan is to push sitting up, for an hour and nine minutes, and find out which goes first, the thing or the lung. Then the six rings are a bet lost in the open, with her life as the stake.

**3. The rule the climax turns on is announced, not found. (Ch. 9)**
"One night matters. If I'm in your village at 2:51 on Friday night, your bank goes." The rain following Lesca is shown, with Bordenave's cross for evidence. This is not: Lesca cannot know it, and the room where nobody can lie is used to certify it. It also empties five nights. Once only Friday counts, Lesca stops driving and Maylis lies still for free from Sunday to Thursday, and the antagonist who wrote "and Coralie Mounet in it" does nothing for a third of the book. Let Maylis get the minute from the gendarmes' page she read in chapter 6 ("02:50. Water through the bank"), as her inference, and keep Lesca trying for the kitchen between the 6th and the 11th.

**4. The most frightening minute in the book is cut short. (Ch. 14)**
"She was back at the doorstep with his empty chair when Coralie came up through the fire door." Coralie is missing in the dark wing for six wheelchairs. The rule that Maylis must push the chair in her hand and not run is the best pressure the book invents, and it is released before it has cost anything. Then "'It won't open. The water's pushing on the door from inside the room'" tells the reader that Madame Etcheto is dead forty paragraphs before the door. Bring Coralie up when the corridor is nearly empty. Let Nadine keep the list until then.

**5. The reader has the secret on page one, and it is staged as a mystery four times. (00, 3, 6, 8, 9, 10)**
The opening shows her turn the van, look down the track and ring. After that: "'Did you know he was out?'" (3), "did you—" and the bell (6), "'What is he to you?'" (8), "'That's not a no.'" (9). Each is cut off by a device. Chapter 10 then replays the night in the opening's own sentences ("she turned the van around on top of the bank so that its headlights pointed down the track"). Two cut-offs are enough. Cut chapter 10's replay to what is new: the reflector, the four minutes, Coralie's car in the yard, the boot.

**6. Chapter 9 holds two chair scenes that make the same request. (Ch. 9)**
"'Then stay in your own bed on Friday,' Maylis said", and silence. Two days later: "So I'm asking. Don't come on Friday." Seven scenes, two visits to the steel room, one negotiation done twice. Merge them: the rule, "to stay", "Both" and the card with her name in one sitting, as the chapter's end.

**7. The book promises a fight at the handle and the gate goes up offstage. (Ch. 5, paid in ch. 14)**
"So one arm won't do it. On the Friday I'll need both of yours, and you'll have to give me the right one." That sets a climax: two women, one body, one handle. The fight comes at a handbrake, which works. But the gate itself, the object the book opens on, is raised by the man it would have drowned, and the reader gets it as a report: "'It's up,' he said. 'Your gate. I counted the clicks, the way you do, and at nineteen it stopped.'" Give Maylis the sound of it from the street, or the sight of the lamp, at 3:20, so that the gate goes up inside the scene.

**8. Tuesday 1 December is three scenes of paper leverage in a row. (Ch. 6)**
"He held a cardboard folder up to the window until she had read LESCA M. on the label." Then Sarthou's form. Then the nets. Each matters later (the nets cost her Jeannot's vote), but run together they are the slackest pages in the book, and the threat they build, "That's prison, for eels", is withdrawn by its victim before it is tested: "'Don't keep a gate shut for me.'" Fold Sarthou's complaint into the café scene with the nets and get to the crossing a page sooner.

**9. Chapter 3 spends chapter 2's cliffhanger in its first sentence. (Ch. 3)**
"The other Maylis Lesca was the one whose village had its flood in 2014 and made her mayor. Call her Lesca." The next paragraph does the same work by evidence, and better: two pillows, the yellow coat on the door, a rib that is not hers. Open on "She lay on her back with rain falling on the roof" and let "Madame le maire" name her.

**10. The aftermath is ten scenes long. (Ch. 16–17)**
Each settles an account, and none carries a question. The bus, the café and Marie-Jo's chair are three verdicts from the village after Madame Darrigrand has already given the one that counts: "You had the key." Keep the dealer's line and Marie-Jo's chair, cut the rest of that scene, and reach Coralie's bag sooner.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The warning that makes its own fate.** "'I hadn't given the 11th a thought until you handed it to me.'" Then the two dry nights that discredit the prophet: "'If she can be out on Friday the 4th, she can be out on Friday the 11th.'" Then his vote. This is tighter plotting than Crouch's.
2. **The stair post and the landing.** One hand holding the body off the stairs (5), and Coralie two steps from the bed with a pulse under her fingers (8).
3. **The rings.** "It rang once." Nine rings and "'Who's dead?'" Eight rings across the van fight. And the clock behind Larrère's voice, "the kind that hangs in a kitchen."
4. **The corridor.** The chair in her hand, "Here", the list changing necks, Kévin counting his fingers in his own headlights, "'Forty-one.'" Also "'You'll count the fingers of my right hand. Then you'll take her home'", the one rule that pays off by fair play.
5. **The call log and the confession.** "'2:51,' she said. 'I've known that for twelve years.'" And the sentence going back through the square "row by row, at walking pace."
