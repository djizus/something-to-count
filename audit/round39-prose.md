# Critic's report: Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. About 43,000 words, 4,400 sentences. Counts below are mine, taken from the text.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the care home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the fields there was one man, at his duck hut, and no one came to tell him." The sum is exact from chapter 3 on. For the two chapters before it the stake is a date on a stone.
- **Tension: 7.** "The gate was six hundred metres behind her. The red reflector was four hundred metres in front. The bank had six minutes left." Chapters 12 to 14 are a 9. In chapters 6 to 9 the question on the page is which rule applies tonight.
- **Progression: 6.** "On Sunday night Maylis could have pushed, but she lay still." Between chapters 4 and 12 she decides about ten times whether to push, lie still, stay sitting or stay awake, and most of those decisions are followed by a rule being restated.
- **Comedy: 8.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'"
- **Characters: 8.** "'I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it.'"
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" The joke taught in chapter 1 fails to finish in chapter 17, and nothing is said about it.
- **Prose: 7.** "The legs stopped inside the door. The eyes had gone to the back of his neck, and Lesca left them there." The sentences are exact and made of things. Long stretches of chapters 5 to 9 ask the reader to work out whose body part is moving.
- **The ending: 8.** "'I know what you picked. I was the other one.'"
- **Clarity: 5.** "Maylis walked her across the square from the church." In this sentence "her" is Lesca's body. Seven other times in the book "walked her" means Maylis's.

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. From chapter 10 I did not stop. I read chapters 7 and 9 on trust.

Would I press it on a friend? On one who reads slowly, yes. On a Crouch reader, no: they would read the first page of chapter 3 twice and resent it.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Level with Crouch as prose: above him sentence for sentence, below him on ease.**

- Above. Crouch declares feeling and describes in stock. Here nothing is abstract and every measure is an object the character has handled: a sandbag pad "as fat as a pillow", a leak "no wider than a shoelace", the gate "no bigger than a nail", a mayor "timed like an egg". There is no sentence in the book that could be moved to another novel.
- Below. A Crouch reader never asks who is doing what. Dark Matter has two Jasons and keeps them in two bodies and one first person. This book puts two women in one body in a close third person where "she" cannot be used, and leaves the sorting to the difference between "the left hand" and "her right hand".
- Below. Crouch changes speed. Panic gets one-line paragraphs and calm gets long ones. Here the mean sentence is between 8.7 and 11.4 words in every chapter. The kitchen and the flood are written at the same pace, so the flood cannot accelerate.

**Level with The Good Place on the comic line, below it on excess.** Each person is funny through their own syntax, as Chidi and Tahani are: Sarthou never contracts, Jeannot numbers his grievances, Lesca moves items, Maïté cannot finish a sentence, Larrère will be brief. But the show's people talk too much and that is half the comedy. Here almost nobody wastes a line.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The grammar of the shared body. Chapters 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 13.**
"Two words were all Maylis could take before Lesca had the jaw back. By then it was no use to Lesca." (3) "Lesca kept the eyes on it longer than a cut deserved." (5)
The convention is that "the hand" is driven by the visitor and "her hand" belongs to the owner. It is never taught, and it is broken: "Her legs swung out of the bed" (4) and "Maylis felt her legs stand her up" (5) against "The legs stopped inside the door" (5). Because the pronoun is unusable the name does the work: "Lesca" 36 times in chapter 5, 13 per thousand words there and in 7 and 13; in chapter 7 three paragraphs running open on it. The narration falls back on denial to assign each movement: "Lesca wasn't the one pulling. Maylis was." "Maylis wasn't the one holding it." "Maylis hadn't told it to." "Maylis hadn't said it." And the viewpoint slips inside these scenes: chapter 5 is Maylis's, yet "Lesca had the rest ready" and "Lesca didn't know there was another half."
What I would do: one viewpoint per scene, and in a driven scene it is the passenger's. The driver has no reported thoughts and is not the subject of bodily verbs. The body is: "Her legs took her down through the Port." The last two sentences of chapter 4 already do this and need no decoding. Every part stays "her". Keep the hand-against-hand contest for the four scenes that are about it: the stair post, the bar, the kitchen table, the handbrake. This removes all eight "walked her" and a third of the "Lesca"s.

**2. The paragraph that explains the scene just shown. Chapters 4, 5, 7, 8, 13, 17.**
"Lesca had walked her down the track with a watch because she wanted to save Jeannot. But he would only need saving if Lesca was here on Friday night." (7, after the walk and "The rain came when Lesca came.")
"Twelve years ago she had let his phone ring once and then done what she had come to do. Holding this wheel was the same thing." (13)
Others: "It had never been true, and it wasn't true now. She did want one of them, badly." (4) "Maylis knew what Lesca wanted. She had lain still for the village's sake. It was going to cost her Coralie." (5) "she knew why. Where Lesca came from, he was a name cut in a stone." (5) "Inguma had gone to count, so Lesca was still there. Maylis had kept her teeth shut all evening to stop that." (8) "She had held the wheel against it for a whole ring, and this was where that put her." (13)
The worst case is the book's own verdict on its heroine, given four times in chapter 17: to Kévin ("I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me"), to Coralie ("I picked"), to Jeannot ("At 3 I never tried the handle"), to Lesca ("I thought I could do better than you"). Chapter 14 said it once and best: "She wanted it not to be her."
What I would do: cut every sentence quoted here. In chapter 17 keep the line to Jeannot, because it refuses a gift, and cut the one to Kévin, who did not ask.

**3. Chapter 9 administers the rules. Chapter 9, and the end of 7.**
"The words keep it off the old woman in room twelve. They won't keep it off you … It sits on you at 2:51 whatever you say to it. The count only decides when it comes back to take the visitor home."
"It comes with me. Keeping me out for a night or two changes nothing, because the river catches up the day I'm back. One night matters."
At the midpoint the book is still amending how it works, in speeches. The chapter has six scenes and 3,200 words. Its kitchen scene repeats to Coralie what chapter 7 gave the reader, and it visits the steel room twice.
What I would do: settle every rule in the chapter 4 meeting, where itemising is the joke. After that nobody states a rule; people are caught by one. In chapter 9 fold the first steel-room scene into the second, keep the three-column chart but give it three answers of one line each, and keep "It couldn't count Either."

**4. One rhythm. The whole book.**
"At home her name wasn't even on her raincoat. Here it was cut in granite. She liked seeing it there. She didn't like what it was under." (6)
68 sentences in 4,436 run past 25 words. The median is 8. About a third of all sentences open on She, He, It, Then or a name; "Then" opens 93. The one paragraph that lets a sentence travel is the crowd in chapter 15, and it is the best paragraph in the book.
What I would do: in the calm scenes join cause to effect inside one sentence. Save the short line for the bank.

**5. The first page of chapter 3.**
"The other Maylis Lesca was the one whose village had its flood in 2014 and made her mayor. Call her Lesca."
The only time the narrator addresses the reader, at the moment the premise arrives. A briefing where the book needs its best page. A second world, a second heroine, a shared body and a separated marriage are delivered in 300 words.
What I would do: start in the bed with the rain and the two pillows. Let Coralie's "Madame le maire" at 06:20 name her, and use the surname only after that.

**6. Everything returns, and some of it word for word.**
"each one whole and then in parts" (8, 9, 13 twice, 15). The hut is "a low bump in the tall grass, with a chimney pipe" in 7 and again in 12. "three rows of tables", "waxed floor", "strip lights" three times. "pressed down just as hard" three times. A sack for Inguma's weight or sound five times. "rubber boots" ten times, six as "in his rubber boots". Coralie's pillow goes "down on the single bed" to end a scene in 7 and in 8.
The numbers are worse. Twelve is the years, the room, the date, Jean's letters, the seats and the commemoration: more than forty uses. Forty-one is Jeannot's letters and the survivors. Forty-two is his next letter and the beds. Nineteen is the days of rain, the turns of the handle and the sandbags. Coralie comes down "every stair … all eleven" after eleven years.
The echoes that carry plot pay: the reflector, the sheet of water "as wide as a door", the kitchen clock behind the voice, the taste of coins. The rest show the hand that arranged them.
What I would do: describe a thing differently the second time unless the repetition is the point. Change the number of letters or of beds so that forty-one means one thing when Coralie says it. Cut "all eleven" and the count of the bags.

**7. Firsts are stamped as firsts. Chapters 5 and 6.**
"Maylis had never smiled at Jeannot Castagnet in her life, and the whole café was watching her do it."
Chapter 5 alone: "had never got as far as one tick", "the same word for eighteen years", "Maylis hadn't known it could", "for eighteen years without once looking at it", "Nobody in Peyrebarthe had ever heard Maylis's mouth forbid anything." In the book: "never" 40 times, "twelve years" 16, "eleven years" or "eleven Decembers" 12, 89 sentences opening on a name or pronoun plus "had".
What I would do: one per chapter. The café already marks the first: "The cards went down."

**8. People are narrated by what they don't do.**
"Nothing came at 2:05. Nothing came at 2:30." (6) "Coralie didn't touch her." (11) "He didn't look up from his numbers." (13, twice)
"didn't" 65, "nobody" 57, "nothing" 49. In chapter 15 the square's failure to react is the event and the negatives earn their place. Elsewhere they stand in for an action the writer has not found.
What I would do: replace half with what the person does instead. The book knows how: Maïté "held her handbag in front of her with both hands and looked at the radiator."

**9. Every scene lands.**
"Coralie picked up the bowl of coffee and poured it down the sink." (9) "Coralie picked the slippers up and stood them on the radiator." (10)
28 of 81 scenes close on a paragraph of twelve words or fewer. Coralie leaves six of hers by doing one silent thing to one object. Darrigrand, Arlette, Marie-Jo and Madame Etcheto each exit on an epigram: "It's four when you're the one counting." "Nobody emptied hers." By chapter 11 the reader can feel the closing line coming.
What I would do: end a third of the scenes a beat early, in the middle of business. Let Bordenave and Kévin say something that is no use to anybody.

**10. Usage.**
- British spelling throughout (metre, colour, grey, towards, windscreen, pavement, torch) with three American nouns: "dish towel" (six times), "baby carriage" (twice), "truck" (three times).
- "said Jeannot", "said old Bordenave", "said Madame Etcheto", "said Monsieur Daguerre" (twice), "said her sister", against "X said" everywhere else.
- "a.m." twice only: the dateline of chapter 1 and "It was 4 a.m." in 14. Every other hour is bare.
- Chapter 1 alone has a dateline.
- "you" in the narration three times: "A motion is supposed to say what it is for" (3), "the kind you turn by hand", "the way you carry a child" (14).
- "Council is at 6:30" (6); "The village council met on Tuesday at 6" (16).

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 15, from "She looked up from the sheet" to "Somebody told the child."** Four plain sentences at the microphone, then the sentence travelling back through the square "at walking pace".
2. **Chapter 14, room twelve to the count.** The two fingers on the wrist "for the full half minute", the line through the last name, "There was a hatbox on it. Nothing else.", and "Forty-one."
3. **Chapter 11, the call list.** "Incoming, a landline number, no name. Then outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. 02:51." and "'Because you'd have told me.'"
4. **The voices made by syntax.** Sarthou's "I am reporting a line." Jeannot's "And six. Bugger off." The woman at Hontaube: "Then it should have gone last Monday." Larrère's last "I'll be brief" in chapter 16, followed by nothing. Coralie's oven door in chapter 17.
5. **The measures.** "as fat as a pillow", "no wider than a shoelace", "no bigger than a nail", "the way mud comes home on a boot", and the opening of chapter 10: "A pad was made to hold a litre. Coralie had never seen one beaten."
