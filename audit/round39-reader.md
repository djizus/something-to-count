# Round 39 — Reader

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** One man against forty houses and the woman she lives with, and the sum fits in two sentences: "The trapdoor was twenty-five minutes away, on foot, in the dark, with the dip under water. The bank had ten."
- **Tension: 7.** From chapter 10 I could not stop ("02:49. She was awake."). Chapters 7 and 9 let it go slack, because I was working out rules when I should have been afraid.
- **Progression: 7.** The pivot costs one word and I felt it: "'Either,' Maylis said." / "'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said." But the middle goes Lesca in, Lesca out, Lesca in, and each turn arrives with a new rule.
- **Comedy: 8.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'"
- **Characters: 8.** Coralie, in one line that is hers and nobody else's: "'I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it.'"
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** In water to the wheels: "'Here,' he said." / "'I know you are.'" The joke from chapter 2 comes back as a man who is still alive.
- **Prose: 7.** I never met a word I had to look up, and the best lines are plain ("the car door shutting on a pillow"). I did stop over who was moving which limb: "Lesca walked her down through the Port."
- **The ending: 8.** It costs both women the same thing, and nobody is let off: "'I know what you picked. I was the other one.'"
- **Clarity: 5.** In chapter 9 of 17 I was still being taught how the crossing works: "'The count only decides when it comes back to take the visitor home.'"

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. From chapter 10 to the end in one sitting.
**Press it on a friend?** Yes, with one instruction: get to the last line of chapter 2.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, by a short distance.** Level or above on people and on what the ending costs. Below on how much a reader has to carry in their head.

1. **Rules.** Dark Matter gives its box one rule, says it once, and every later scene uses it. Recursion's chair is the same. This book has, by my count: stay sitting and you cross; the owner keeps her right hand; the count sets when the visitor goes home; standing in the steel room you can't say what you know is false; the body left behind cools; the visitor weakens; pushing keeps the visitor out and costs the rib; the rain comes with Lesca; only 2:51 on the Friday matters; the one who can't be taken home dies and gets a bingo card. Ten, and the last three arrive after the halfway mark. A Crouch reader spends that effort on fear.
2. **The reversal.** The Good Place's "this is the Bad Place" is said aloud, in one sentence, and re-reads a whole season. This book's equal, that the rescuer is the flood, is first worked out as a table of wet and dry days in chapter 7. When it is said aloud in chapter 8 ("You're the water") the hall mishears it, which is funny and is the better scene, but I already had the news.
3. **The marriage.** Above. In Dark Matter the fear is that the other man is in the house. Here the fear is that the wife prefers him, for a reason the reader agrees with: "'I'd have this one again.'" And the villagers are each wrong in their own way (the gap in the car window, the numbered grievances, the timer), which Crouch's supporting casts never are.

**What I did not understand**

- What exactly would keep Lesca out on the Friday: being awake, sitting up, or pushing. The plan is "I'll sit up in the van, awake, until 4, when it gives up," but I had already watched it land on her standing on the stairs and sitting at the kitchen table.
- How Lesca knows that only 2:51 on Friday counts. She says it and the book proceeds as if it were proved.
- What Larrère is guilty of. His order was to keep the gate shut, which keeps Castagnet dry. The book treats the kitchen phone as an unmasking and I could not say what it unmasked.
- Whether the rib was going to kill her.
- Why the fields in Lesca's village are a lake. I got "gravel pit" on the second pass.
- "It takes a small m." I did not get the joke.
- The stone in Maylis's picture has a date on it. The stone Lesca walks round has none.

**Where I skimmed**

- Chapter 1, the square: Jeannot's account of which way the water ran.
- Chapter 9, the second scene in the chair, where the rules are restated.
- Chapter 11, the opening paragraph of ponies, contract and lake.
- Chapter 12, the layout of the care home ("The old wing came first. At its far end a ramp went up to a fire door"). I skimmed it and needed it in chapter 14.

**Where I could not stop**

- The last line of chapter 2 through the stair post at the end of chapter 5.
- Chapter 6, the pulse chart falling on one side while Larrère talks untimed on the other.
- Chapter 10 to the end of chapter 15.

**What I will remember in a month**

- The dead playing bingo, a card with her name on it, and "Twelve. One, two," then a chair pulled in to a table.
- Larrère through a gap in his car window, and Coralie doing him through the oven door.
- "Either."
- Jeannot's numbered points, ending in "And six. Bugger off," and letter forty-two.
- "Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him," and the sentence going back through the crowd at walking pace.
- Kévin in the water counting five fingers and wanting six.

I will not remember the association, the merger with Hontaube, the gravel company, or any of the ten rules except the right hand.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The rules are still being issued in chapter 9, and the Friday plan rests on one I could not state.** (Structural. Chapters 4, 6, 7, 9.)
"'The words keep it off the old woman in room twelve. They won't keep it off you,' Lesca said. 'It sits on you at 2:51 whatever you say to it. The count only decides when it comes back to take the visitor home.'"
More than halfway through, the book is correcting what I thought I knew. Then the climax depends on Maylis staying awake in a van, and I did not know whether that could work, or whether I was meant to know it couldn't. I was confused through the nine hours on the bank when I should have been afraid.
*Do:* settle every rule by the end of chapter 6. Cut the failed attempt with the words from chapter 9 or move it to chapter 7, where she is trying to keep Lesca out anyway. Give chapter 9 one sentence a reader can repeat about Friday: to keep her out Maylis must push at 2:51, and one more push puts the rib through the lung. Then the van is a bet the reader understands and can dread.

**2. The book's one reversal is delivered as arithmetic.** (Structural. Chapter 7.)
"The two days that followed, Thursday and Friday, had been the only dry ones. Last night she had lain still and Lesca had come across. By noon it was raining again."
This is the moment everything before it changes meaning: the woman who came to save Jeannot is what will drown him. I got it by checking days against nights. Chapter 9 then does it a third time as a three-column chart, which is the funny version and the one to keep.
*Do:* let it happen in front of her. Bordenave's cellar, dry on Friday and ankle-deep on Saturday, is already there. Put the first rain on her hood as Lesca takes the legs on Saturday morning, cut the recap paragraph, and let "The rain came when Lesca came" stand alone.

**3. Chapter 9 is three chapters.** (Structural.)
It holds the kitchen chart, the fight for the mouth, a scene in the steel room, the petition, the vote from the car ("'I'm heating the whole square,' Josiane said"), the oven door, the job list, Coralie's box, the night of the words, a second scene in the steel room and the hall of the dead. By the time the wall came down and I saw the card with her name on it, the best image in the book, I was tired.
*Do:* end a chapter on "Jeannot kept his hand up until the car had left the square." Start the next with the pad. The hall of the dead then closes a short chapter and gets the room it has earned.

**4. Only one date line in the whole book.** (Structural, and cheap. Throughout; worst in chapters 7 and 9.)
Chapter 1 opens "Tuesday 24 November 2026, 2:51 a.m." Nothing like it appears again. After that I get "On Thursday night", "At 10 on Friday 4 December", "By noon on Saturday", across two villages that share a calendar and differ in weather. In chapter 7 I lost which night was which, and that is the chapter where the nights are the evidence.
*Do:* head every section the way chapter 1 is headed, with the village when it changes. A countdown to the 11th that the reader can see is tension for nothing, and it is exactly how Crouch does it.

**5. The rib is promised as a death and then forgotten.** (Chapters 7, 12, 14, 17.)
Coralie, chapter 7: "Push on it and it goes through into the lung... He was dead before the doctor had his coat off." Maylis then pushes in the van until "Her mouth tasted of coins," hauls a heavy man up a ladder ("Something gave in her side. She heard it go."), pushes wheelchairs through water for an hour, and on Sunday gets "'I'll strap it first.'" On Monday she digs a grave.
I spent chapter 14 waiting for her to go down. She never does, and nobody mentions the lung again, least of all the woman who described it.
*Do:* pay it. She goes down in the hall at "Forty-one" and wakes in Bayonne beside the forty-one, or Coralie puts a stethoscope to it on Sunday and says what gave. Or take the man of eighty-one down from a death to a warning.

**6. I could not say what Larrère did.** (Chapters 10, 14, 16.)
"'You stood in my kitchen that night and rang her from my phone, with his van gone down to the fields, and you never told the woman you'd sent to the gate.'"
The clock ticking behind his voice is a good clue and I caught it. But his order was "shut", and shut keeps the husband dry. So his silence cost what? I think the answer is that she would have gone down the track before she ever went up the bank. The book never says it, and he walks away from the gate without my knowing what he had been caught at.
*Do:* give Maïté one more clause that names the cost, the twenty-five minutes Lesca would have had.

**7. Lesca's turn at the wheel is argued when it should be seen.** (Chapter 13.)
"But the motion she had written at her own kitchen table was for a seat and a bed. There was no man in it."
This is the other lead's whole moral turn, in the middle of a scene counted out in phone rings, and I had to read the paragraph twice to follow the reasoning from motion to wheel to "the same thing". The paragraph before it has already shown me everything: a dead engine, a hand on the brake that won't open, a reflector "no nearer than it had been twelve years ago".
*Do:* go from the reflector straight to "'I'm letting go.'" Keep one line of the motion at most.

**8. The square in chapter 1 loads the village before I have a reason to carry it.** (Chapter 1.)
"'Two. That gate lets water through both ways. On Monday the river was lower than my fields. You put the gate up, and my water ran out through it, down into the river.'"
The prologue and the thing on the wardrobe had me. Then in one scene I am given the association, its president, registered letters, the handle, the duck water and which way a gate drains. I read Jeannot's speech twice to see where the water went, and this is the first time I meet the man the whole book is about.
*Do:* keep his counting, which is him. Cut the hydraulics to one line: he had water on Sunday, she raised the gate on Monday, he sat over mud. Leave the association until chapter 4, where the order matters.

**9. Too many names, and four of the main ones start with L.** (Throughout; worst in chapters 3 and 14.)
Lesca, Larrère, Labat, Lassalle. Then Darrigrand and Daguerre. One man is Jean, Jeannot and Castagnet. Residents are named for the first time in the flood: "'Madame Hirigoyen. Frame.'" In chapter 3 I mixed up Larrère and Labat at the council table, in the scene that introduces both.
*Do:* change Labat and one of the D names. The names read off the list in chapter 14 are fine, because there the name is the point.

**10. The eighteen days are counted in and never counted out.** (Chapters 1, 2, 4.)
"She opened her pocket knife and cut eighteen marks down the rake, starting level with her shoulder."
I waited for the rake. It never comes back, and neither does her fear of her own date once Lesca says the stone "isn't a grave". A hook from page one is dropped in a subordinate clause in chapter 4.
*Do:* cut the marks, or let her see the rake once more on the Friday with one notch left.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie and the oven door.** Chapter 1, chapter 9, and the time it fails: "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'"
2. **The hall of the dead.** The card, "Lesca. Maylis." called like a number, "'The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies,'" and in chapter 15 "Twelve. One, two," and the chair.
3. **Jeannot whole.** The numbered points, the 5 a.m. call, "'One. It's Jeannot. Two. Jean's for my gravestone,'" letter forty-two, and the second shovel.
4. **Chapter 14 from the glass doors to "Forty-one."** "Here." The bins. Kévin's five fingers. The hatbox on the wardrobe.
5. **Chapter 15 from "I knew he was at his hut" to "Somebody has to stack them."** Above all the man beyond the last loudspeaker who starts to clap.
