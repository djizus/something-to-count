# Critic's report: Characters (round 4)

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `22.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

In this report "Maylis" is the keyholder in the wet village and "Lesca" is the mayor in the dry one, as the narrator has it.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." (ch. 2) The village's stake is exact and stays exact. The stake between the two women is cancelled by the book itself: "Staying added the other life to hers, and took nothing of her own away." (ch. 15)
- **Tension: 6.** "It's one chair. Somebody sits and somebody gets up. It never was an exchange." (ch. 11) From that line until ch. 15 neither woman can lose anything to the other. Each visit ends when Inguma has finished counting, and the reader knows it.
- **Progression: 7.** "That village had not been spared December 2014. It had not had it yet." (ch. 4) After that the gauge does the work, a figure a chapter. The premise takes four chapters to be in play.
- **Comedy: 8.** "At six he said hydraulic and finished the sentence it was in, which nobody at that table had heard him do." (ch. 10)
- **Characters: 7.** "Because you'd have told me." (ch. 15) The supporting cast is an 8 or a 9, each woman by herself a 7 or an 8, the two of them as a pair a 5.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." (ch. 19)
- **Prose: 7.** "Then the water came up the door to it and put it out." (ch. 16) Held down by the repetitions counted in faults 6, 9 and 10.
- **The ending: 7.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (ch. 22) The last image pays. The two women do not.
- **Clarity: 6.** "To have Friday, Lesca had to be the one still sitting when the other one woke. Then she went home with her, and the two of them stood on that dyke in the one pair of arms." (ch. 11) I read that paragraph three times. The hall in ch. 18 I understood only from ch. 20.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. Caught at the last line of ch. 4, would have drifted in ch. 13 and 14, pulled back by ch. 15 and not let go after.

**Pressed on a friend?** On a friend who likes village comedy, yes. On a Crouch reader, no, not as it stands.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**For this lens: above Crouch on the people, below Crouch on the doubles. Level with The Good Place on the ensemble, below it on the central pair. Net: just under the bar.**

What puts it there:

1. **The ensemble is better than anything in Crouch and level with The Good Place.** Crouch has leads and functions; nobody misses a secondary character in Dark Matter. Here each villager is wrong in a way that has its own grammar, which is The Good Place's method. Larrère: "So there's the question of the winch. It isn't settled, and you're not to worry about it." Sarthou: "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade." Madame Ducasse: "There's nothing here that lets me un-receive it. There's no column." Jeannot numbers his grievances. Marie-Jo addresses the wall.

2. **The marriage is set up as well as Crouch sets one up, and then never put in danger.** Crouch shows the marriage whole in chapter one and then puts the wrong man in the bed; the book is the fear of that. Ch. 1's kitchen, with the eggs and Larrère done through the oven door, is a better first half than Dark Matter's family dinner. The second half is announced in writing ("That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it") and never happens. Lesca gets as far as the foot of the stairs in ch. 8 and is fetched. Coralie is never made to choose between the two, which is the scene Dark Matter exists to reach.

3. **The two versions hardly speak.** The Good Place changes its people by shutting them in a room to argue for whole episodes. These two share about 2,200 words face to face in 57,000 (ch. 7, 11, 15, 18), and the last thing either says to the other is "You rang once." The book's reversals are at The Good Place's level of craft: 02:51 turning out to be the minute of the call, Maïté's verb being the word on the card, the kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice. They re-read what came before. The pair they happen to has no scene in which to take them in together.

**The lens's questions, answered.**

- *Distinct?* Yes. One line of dialogue is enough to tell them apart. "Which will you have first? What I've come about, or what happened at six?" against "Either."
- *Wanted-for?* Lesca from ch. 6. Maylis not until ch. 17 (fault 5).
- *Alive?* Lesca from her first page. Maylis is alive in what she does (the blackmail in ch. 9, the collar in ch. 19) more than in what she says.
- *Softened or excused?* Both, in the last three chapters (fault 3).
- *Described from outside as a woman?* Never by the narrator. The village does it ("little Lesca", "the commune's girl", "Is the man from the commune about?", "Monsieur le maire") and the book makes it count: "He had left the little off. She heard it go."
- *Who would be missed?* Jeannot Castagnet first. Madame Etcheto. Kévin Labat. Then Marie-Jo and Madame Ducasse. Nobody would miss Tastet, Nadine, Bixente or six of the seven men on the dyke.
- *Which relationship carries the book, and does it pay?* Not the one the premise names. The book is carried by each woman and Jeannot Castagnet (one drowned him, one hauled him out by the collar) and by Coralie's question. Jeannot pays, in the last line. Coralie pays once in full (the pillow in ch. 21) and once in a loss that is under-lit (the pillow in the car, ch. 22). The two women do not pay at all.
- *Does the reader care who lives?* Jeannot, yes, by ch. 5. Madame Etcheto, yes. I was never once afraid for either woman.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. The two women never finish with each other (ch. 18 to 22)

> "You rang once," she said. (ch. 18)

That is the last line either speaks to the other. After it the book holds two accounts of the night and never sets them side by side. Maylis: "The words told her nothing. She had been to the tonne first." (ch. 19) Lesca: "Last night I sent it to her." (ch. 21) Maylis never learns that Lesca said "I drowned him" into a microphone. Lesca receives the hall as "one more thing had crossed" and never has to stand in front of the woman she advised. Ch. 22 then shuts the door in its first paragraph: "Nothing sat up there, and nothing came down."

This is the relationship in the title position, and it has a beginning (ch. 7), a middle (ch. 11) and no end.

**Do:** one more meeting, after both Saturdays. Put it at nine minutes to three on Sunday in place of the bare wardrobe, with the chair empty and Inguma gone, and let each arrive with the other's day in her head. Six lines. Maylis: you told me the tonne first. Lesca: I did. Maylis: I'd gone twice already. Whatever the last line is, it should settle whose the track was. Then the wardrobe is bare, and it means something that it is. If a meeting is refused, the minimum is that the square crosses to Maylis as the hall crossed to Lesca, so that "I had the key" in ch. 22 is said by someone who heard "I drowned him".

### 2. The marriage is never threatened, and Coralie is never made to choose (ch. 6, 8, 11, 13)

> "I don't know who you've been today. [...] I'd have this one again." (ch. 8)
> "I'd keep that one in the house." (ch. 11)

Each Coralie prefers the other woman. It is the best idea in the book and nothing comes of it. Maylis reads a line in her own hand with Coralie's surname in it (ch. 13) and answers it with a line about a sash. She is given no jealousy and no fear. When she reports the soup in ch. 11 ("She says you answered everything she asked. She liked it." "So did I.") it goes by flat. So when Coralie carries the pillow to the car, the reader has not been shown that Maylis would have fought for her.

**Do:** let Lesca get up the stairs in ch. 8. One night in the bedroom, with Maylis underneath and unable to get the jaw, and let Coralie be the one who sends her back down. She is the person to do it: "I know when one's holding and I know when one wants to be let go." That gives the book its Crouch scene, gives Coralie the choice, and stops Lesca being excused by an interruption. Then have the preference come back once, where it costs: in ch. 22 Coralie should say what she said in ch. 8.

### 3. The guilt is shared out as soon as it is owned (ch. 15, 20, 21, 22)

> "02:40. WHITE TRAFIC, END OF TRACK." (ch. 20)
> "He rang you from here. Guy. From that telephone." (ch. 21)
> "That's mine. I'll have it now for as long as I have anything." (ch. 22)

Lesca says "I opened the gate, and I drowned him" and within two chapters has three people beside her: Coralie ("Then he's mine as well"), Sarthou, who drove past the van, and Larrère, who gave the order from the dead man's kitchen. Maylis loses one life for her choice, and it is a woman of ninety-four who asked to be put back in her room, behind a door that would not open, on a list that Coralie drew up walkers-first. Both women are softened by the same method.

The ledger of Lesca's Saturday is also too kind. What she loses is a medal against a receipt, an appointment on Monday and a sitting on Tuesday, all after the last page. What she is given on the page, the same day, is Marie-Jo's "Maylis", Maïté's ladder and Coralie's pillow.

**Do:** cut Sarthou's notebook page. He is the third sharer and the weakest, and the rubber band snapping behind his door is better left as it is. Keep Maïté's telephone; that scene is about Maïté. Put one bill on the page: the Tuesday sitting, or her mother (fault 7). For Maylis, remove one cushion from room twelve. The stuck door or the being put back at ten, not both.

### 4. Coralie wants nothing of her own (throughout)

> "I'd have settled for anything. A holiday. A dog." (ch. 12)
> "So that's where I am, in this. There's a key, and a Trafic, and Guy, and then there's me, as a distance." (ch. 12)

She has the best voice in the book and is given only responses. "I've waited" or "I waited" seven times. "What do you want" five times, always put to Maylis. Even the application to the hospital is a question to Maylis: "I put it where you'd see it." She is the object of a written motion, and the second quotation shows the book knows it. Knowing it is not the same as mending it.

**Do:** one thing she wants and goes after without showing it to anyone first. The hospital post will serve if it is hers. Cut "I put it where you'd see it" and let Maylis learn of it at the yellow box. And let somebody put her own question to her once, Arlette or Madame Etcheto, and let her have an answer ready.

### 5. Maylis's want for the other life is asserted, and its surrender is narrated (ch. 10, 15, 17)

> "I want your side. The desk, the timer, people getting up when I come in." (ch. 15)
> "she no longer meant to sit in anybody's chair that night." (ch. 17)

Her one day as mayor shows her failing at it: Either to three councillors, the wrong signature, a timer she cannot turn. What she gets is two telephone calls and being thanked. The book itself weighs the prize and finds it light: "It came to less than the key on her belt at home." Yet on the strength of that she tells Coralie that what she wants is not in the house ("Is it here?" "No."), which is the gravest thing she does before the flood. The cause is too small for it. Then the want is given up in a subordinate clause. "Meant to" appears 27 times; the narrator announces an intention at the head of a scene where a decision should have been played.

**Do:** give her one act in ch. 10 that succeeds and that only she could have done. The material is on the table: Castagnet's twelfth letter, unanswered "since 2014", in front of the one person who has signed for forty-one of them. Let her answer it. Let that be what she wants to keep. Then the nine rings in ch. 16 take something from her that the reader has seen her hold.

### 6. The shared body is a pantomime horse by its fourth outing (ch. 8, 10, 13, 14)

> "So the knees had one order to bend and another to walk, and they locked." (ch. 14)

"The jaw" 14 times, "the mouth" 19, nearly all in four chapters. It is excellent in ch. 8 ("'Either,' the mouth said.") and mechanical by the loto. Worse for this lens: in four chapters of living in one coat they speak to each other once, at the top of ch. 14 ("How long?" "I gave it the grass."), and that half page is livelier than any of the seizures.

**Do:** let the passenger talk. Halve the jaw business in ch. 13 and 14 and spend the room on what each thinks of the other's village, said to the other while it is in front of them. That is also where fault 1's missing conversation can begin.

### 7. Arlette is dropped from both endings (ch. 12, 20, 21, 22)

> "I know what you're called," Arlette said. "I'm the last that uses it." (ch. 10)

She is the fixed point in both worlds: the mother who voted three times against her daughter, the only one who says the name. In the dry world her whole response to the confession is "Her mother came out from the church wall, as far as where the lettering could be read." Nothing follows. In the wet world ch. 12 plants "If that gate were ever opened with the river up, I'd want an hour's warning. It takes an hour, with the old mare", and she and the ponies are absent from ch. 19 and ch. 22.

**Do:** a short scene for the dry-world Arlette in ch. 21, between Maïté's kitchen and the landing. It can be the bill that fault 3 asks for. And one sight of the wet-world Arlette on the Sunday road, with the old mare accounted for.

### 8. Larrère is a catchphrase for twenty chapters and a man for one beat (ch. 9, 21)

> "Nothing came." (ch. 21)

The beat is right, and it is almost the first inside he has been given. The only preparation is one sentence in ch. 15: "He had telephoned an order that night from somewhere, and in twelve years nobody had asked him where." Meanwhile "I'll be brief" comes eight times and "through the gap" twelve, six of them in ch. 5. His other motive is planted and abandoned: "That hut had no box." (ch. 9) The unmetered pump never returns.

**Do:** pay the hut or cut it. Give him one unguarded line before ch. 21. Ch. 8 is the place, when the window goes all the way down; what he says there now ("Guy. From the commune's girl, in my own square.") keeps him inside the mask.

### 9. "Either" is one joke told to three officials (ch. 9, 10, 17)

> "I can't put Either in a tray. Shall we begin again?" (ch. 17)

The word appears thirty times. Sarthou cannot summon it, Tastet minutes it as consent, the woman at Hontaube cannot tray it. By the third the scene is known before it starts. And it contradicts the turn: by ch. 17 Maylis has let a telephone ring nine times and decided to be at the gate herself. She should not still be saying it to a stranger about a form. The Good Place finds a new shape for Chidi's indecision each time; this finds a new desk.

**Do:** cut the Either from the Hontaube call and keep the form, the tray and the thirteen kilometres. The word then goes silent from ch. 10 until Coralie's "That was Either, with the river in it", which is where it should land.

### 10. Props do the feeling (throughout; ch. 21)

> "Lesca's right hand was shut." (ch. 21)

The shut right hand eight times, the seam, the shopping pad, "lower than she used it" eight times. Each is good once. By ch. 21, at the moment Lesca tells Coralie that Madame Etcheto is dead, the reader is handed the hand again where a sentence was owed. In the whole book the narrator says once what either woman feels about the other: "They were of a height, and neither of them liked it." (ch. 7)

**Do:** three plain sentences, no more. What Maylis thinks of the woman who rang once (end of ch. 16). What Lesca thinks as she lets go of the arm (ch. 18). What Lesca thinks when forty-one crosses (ch. 20).

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie at the oven door and the shopping pad.** "Eggs is what Either gets in this house." (ch. 1), and the same impression breaking off at "The—" in ch. 22.
2. **The telephone scene in ch. 15**, whole, and Lesca's refusal ever to know which. "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." through to "I can't find which part of it turned." (ch. 21). Do not let her find out.
3. **Jeannot Castagnet's voice.** The numbered points, the nine rings and "And six. Bugger off." (ch. 16), "It's Jeannot. Jean's for the stone." (ch. 13), letter forty-two that "doesn't thank you", and the second shovel.
4. **Madame Etcheto, all three scenes and room twelve**, to "Forty-one." Including Monsieur Daguerre's "Here" to the roof of the van.
5. **Kévin's fingers.** "Five." in the yard (ch. 19) and "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything as much." (ch. 22).
