# Round 4: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `22.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." (ch 2). Stated early and never unclear. Held at arm's length until chapter 16, because one village has already paid and the other spends a dozen chapters on a staff file.
- **Tension: 6.** "Under the motion she ruled two columns, as the commune's accounts were ruled" (ch 15). Three days before the river, the book does its books.
- **Progression: 6.** "At six he said hydraulic and finished the sentence it was in" (ch 10). Told once, then reported back to the reader in chapter 11 (twice), chapter 14 and chapter 15.
- **Comedy: 7.** "'I don't cook Either,' she said. 'There's no recipe for it.'" (ch 1). Constant, exact and built to pay. One register, low volume, and no full day of the wrong woman in the wrong village until chapter 8.
- **Characters: 7.** "'I am not hearing a statement,' he said. 'I retire on the eighteenth, with no file open.'" (ch 8). The village is an 8: each of them has a private way of not answering. Maylis as a comic presence is a 5: she has one word.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Who's dead?' said Jeannot Castagnet." (ch 16), nine rings after the sole of a wader under black water. One slip, in chapter 20.
- **Prose: 7.** "as a dog gets up" (ch 1, ch 4, ch 7). Dry enough to carry a joke without leaning on it. It reuses its best comparisons and reports the fight for the jaw more than a dozen times in the same words.
- **The ending: 8.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the neck. 'The—'" (ch 22). The funniest person in the book can no longer do the voice.
- **Clarity: 5.** "Whoever wakes first goes home, and if you're still sat there you go home with them, and it isn't your home." (ch 7). A swap comedy needs the reader to know at a glance who is driving. Here it is worked out like a sum at the top of most chapters from 6 to 18.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. Chapters 2, 4 and 6 are where I would have put it down. Chapters 3 and 5 are why I did not.

**Press it on a friend?** On one who likes village comedy played straight, with the instruction to get to chapter 8. Not on somebody because they loved The Good Place. They would be promised a laugh rate the book does not have.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below The Good Place as comedy, taken whole. Level with it in one thing and above it in one.** Crouch is not a comic comparison. The one point that carries over is that his reader has the premise in play in the first chapter, and here it arrives as a joke in the eighth.

- **Below: speed of the comic premise.** The Good Place has the wrong woman in the wrong place in its first scene and the first complication inside the half hour. This book's version of that scene is Lesca in a yellow waterproof telling the mayor "You have three minutes" through his own car window. It is chapter 8 of 22. Before it, the laughs belong to a village (good) and to a council table (thin).
- **Below: range.** The Good Place runs four or five registers at once: the selfish one, the anxious one, the snob, the idiot, the cosmic clerk. This book runs one: understatement, from people who will not answer the question. Nobody is silly. Nobody is happily stupid, except Kévin for one line ("'I'd fly,' Kévin said. He was up in a loader bucket with a string of lamps round his neck"). Only Jeannot is allowed to be loud, and the one page where he is let go (ch 16) is the biggest laugh in the book.
- **Level: everybody is wrong in their own way, and it is the heroine's way.** Larrère hides in bodies ("I was in the room, yes. I was in the room as mayor"). Sarthou closes files. Tastet minutes a shrug as consent. Madame Ducasse cannot un-receive a letter because "There's no column". Maïté abstains. Arlette says "I'm not asking". The priest has a seal. Coralie keeps a landing. Each is a different make of Either. That is the Good Place's ensemble principle, and it is done as well here.
- **Above: what the running jokes turn into.** The Good Place pays a gag off with warmth. This book pays it off with the bill. The hourly finger count, set up as a boy's hobby in a loader bucket, comes back in a flooded yard: "'Five,' he said. He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." Monsieur Daguerre's "Here" comes back as "I know you are." The old woman the two of them giggled outside is the one who is not got out. The Good Place does not do this to its jokes.
- **On reversals.** They re-read what came before: 02:51 is the minute of a telephone call, the kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice re-reads two bowls on a drainer, and Maïté's verb re-reads a card. None of them is funny. The Good Place's great reversal makes the viewer laugh at what they believed. These make the reader wince. That is a fair choice, and it is where the comparison is lost on laughs.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The half of the opening that carries the premise is the half with the comedy switched off. Chapters 2, 4, 6.**
"'That's a pity. I'd have taken both. One for the commune, and one for the washing-up.'" (ch 4). That is the one joke in chapter 4, and it is a flashback to 2014. The dry village is, by design, the place where the fun stopped: Coralie has "done nobody's voice in that kitchen since", Larrère is under a three-minute bell, Marie-Jo talks to a wall, Jeannot is dead, Kévin has been to prison. Every comic engine running in chapters 1, 3 and 5 is off in 2, 4 and 6. Those are the chapters that deliver the card, the warm chair and the first crossing. So the reader gets the high concept from the unfunny half, and the first full day of swap comedy lands 36 per cent in.
*Do:* get her across two chapters sooner. The opening of chapter 6 ("Come back across. I've left you on that landing eleven years." / "Where did you get eleven?") is the first scene where the premise is funny and painful at once. It should close chapter 4. Pay for it by cutting chapter 4's walk through the gauge sheets to its last four lines and halving chapter 6's dyke committee. Then the Monday in the waterproof arrives as chapter 6. And put Tastet and Madame Ducasse to work in chapter 2: they are the dry village's own comic people and they do not come alive until chapters 10 and 11.

**2. The farce is told once and then minuted back to the reader. Chapters 8, 9, 11, 14, 15, 18.**
"'Wednesday the second. She raked the square, signed three letters in her father's hand and told three members Either. The same evening, at this table, she let a member speak for eleven minutes and thanked him. I was the member. The third, at the budget. She slept in the chair for twenty-two minutes.'" (ch 15). The reader watched all of it. The eleven minutes happen in chapter 10 and are then recited by Madame Ducasse (ch 11), by Lesca in the steel room (ch 11), by Larrère (ch 14) and by Larrère again (ch 15). "Three minutes" and "Guy" happen in chapter 8 and are recited by Lafitte's pad, by Lesca at supper, by Larrère in the square, by Larrère at the blotter and by Maylis in the steel room. Add Lesca's *Received* column (ch 15) and Arlette's "On the Sunday before last… On the Monday… On the Wednesday" (ch 18). A village that files everything is the joke, and Lafitte's pad makes it once, well, because the pad gets it wrong. After that each list is a joke the reader already has, said more slowly. This is why the middle feels like double entry.
*Do:* one retelling per event, by the person it costs most, and only where the retelling changes it. Keep Lafitte's pad. Cut Madame Ducasse's replay in chapter 11 to the two objects (three letters with a tall L, a page of Tastet's diary). Cut the opening exchange of invoices in the chapter 11 steel room to what is new (the undated letter, Castagnet going down). Cut Larrère's chapter 14 summary. Keep his chapter 15 reading at three items. Cut the *Received* list to the Castagnet line and *Nil*. Cut Arlette's list.

**3. The two women never become a double act. Their scenes together are an exchange of bills. Chapters 7, 11, 15.**
"'On Wednesday you sat in my chair and let Guy Larrère speak for eleven minutes. Eleven. I made a rule in 2015 so that he should never have four. You thanked him. You said Either to three of my councillors in my mother's kitchen.'" (ch 11). A book about two versions of one person owes the reader the scene where they are in a room and it is funny. Chapter 7 starts it ("'Good,' the mayor said. 'You can get up. That saves a week.'" and "They were of a height, and neither of them liked it") and drops it after ten lines for a briefing. Chapters 11 and 15 open on accounts. The proof of what these scenes could be is in chapter 11: "'You had soup with her.' 'Yes.' 'She says you answered everything she asked. She liked it.' 'So did I.'"
*Do:* let the chapter 7 meeting run as a meeting. The mayor tries to chair a sitting of two, with one chair. Maylis prices each item. Give them something small and shared to disagree about that is not the plot: the L, their father, what Coralie's laugh sounds like. Start chapters 11 and 15 where the soup exchange starts, not with the list.

**4. The loto is the set piece and the double is kept quiet at it. Chapters 7 and 13.**
"Maylis worked it from underneath. Inguma was still counting signatures, and she stood behind that bar as a passenger in her own arms." (ch 13). The whole village is in one hall, two women are in one mouth, and the collisions happen one customer at a time, at a bar, overheard by nobody: Kévin, then Coralie, then Marie-Jo. The loudest thing the mouth does in public is put the wine to a vote. And a thread is dropped. In chapter 7 Marie-Jo rings Coralie ("She said, 'Chrysanthemums.' She said, 'To the house.' Then a chair scraped on the tiles"). Coralie never raises it. At the loto the mouth stares at Jeannot all evening ("Lafitte had it on the pad twice") with Coralie twenty metres away, and "She did not ask again." A farce plants a partner who has heard the rumour in order to put her in the room when it seems to come true.
*Do:* make two of the three bar exchanges public. "I'd have stood" should be said to Marie-Jo with the interval queue listening. Have Coralie arrive for her coffees while Lafitte is reading his pad aloud. Let the rumour about Castagnet, the one about the mayor and the real thing land on her in the same minute. Keep Monsieur Daguerre's "Here" as the top of the scene.

**5. Maylis is the straight woman in her own chapters. She has one comic note, and by chapter 17 it repeats. Chapters 1, 5, 9, 10, 17.**
"'I can't put Either in a tray.'" (ch 17). This is the fifth time somebody files the word: eggs (ch 1), "The keyholder has no preference" (ch 5), "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade" (ch 9), "I have that as consent" (ch 10), a tray (ch 17). The first four climb. The fifth is the same step again. It is also said on the morning she let a telephone ring nine times and five hours before she asks Coralie back across the landing. The woman is ahead of her own gag. Meanwhile her second comic habit is used half a dozen times and never as a joke except once: she prices things. "She read it through. It was a night's work for one woman who had done it before, and Maylis priced it as the commune did, at forty euros for the month. She put that at the foot, and *paid*." (ch 12). That is the funniest thing she does in the book.
*Do:* cut "Either" from the Hontaube call and keep the rest of it ("Then it should have gone last Monday"). Give Maylis pricing from chapter 1 as her voice: trailers, mornings, turns of a handle. Then she has two notes, and the second one is the moral problem. Twenty-five minutes against ten is a price.

**6. In the minute after the confession the comedy lets Lesca win twice. Chapter 20.**
"'It was given by decree, and it isn't taken back by clipboard,' Lesca said. 'He'll need a decree to keep it. He can hold it until he has one. You can tell him the mayor said so, and that she'll have a receipt.'" Then, a page on: "'Then write it in. I can't sign a summons to nowhere.'" Then she corrects the lieutenant's appointment. She has just said "and I drowned him" to six thousand people, and for the next two pages she is the wittiest and best-informed person on the stage. Two gags of one shape, both at the expense of smaller people, tell the reader she is all right. This is the place where a joke weakens the dark.
*Do:* keep the summons. She corrects the paper that removes her, and that joke is on her. Take the medal back in silence, or let the young man with the clipboard have the last line. Let the first funny thing after the confession be Marie-Jo's: "Somebody has to stack them."

**7. A bell stops a revelation twice. Chapters 2 and 15.**
"'…did you—' The timer rang." (ch 15). In chapter 2: "She opened her mouth to give it. The timer rang." The first is a good joke. The second is the same device used to hold back the same kind of answer, and the reader sees the hand on the dial. The text even concedes it ("without a thought of the day she would be glad of it").
*Do:* in chapter 15 let Larrère stop himself with time on the clock. The next paragraph already says "he did not altogether want it said". The man who has never finished inside three minutes dries at two, and the timer rings into the silence afterwards. It is funnier and truer, and it sets up "Nothing came" at the garden gate in chapter 21.

**8. The physical comedy of two in one body is narrated from inside, as mechanics. Chapters 8, 10, 13, 14.**
"Maylis was at the jaw by then, and pulling. Lesca kept it." (ch 8). "Maylis shut the jaw on the next word. She held it shut until her ears sang." (ch 13). There are more than a dozen of these. From inside it is a sensation, and from outside it is funny. The book knows it: the three times the fight has a witness are the three times it gets a laugh. "Madame Ducasse looked at the chair's two hands, one of which was sitting on the other" (ch 10). "'Shall I do that for you?' Madame Ducasse said" (ch 14, the sash). "four men watched the mayor of Peyrebarthe stand at the stone in her sash, on a Monday, with one of her hands holding the other by the wrist" (ch 14). The same inwardness makes the reader stop to work out who has which limb.
*Do:* halve the reports from inside. Give each one that stays a witness and a line. Open each swap chapter by saying in plain words whose village it is and who is driving.

**9. The saying that is retired at the end was heard once, twenty-one chapters earlier. Chapters 1 and 22.**
"'For twelve years, when a man came in late, they told him he was still in the box. I've said it to you myself, in this hall. This morning they told one he'd had the key and gone for a walk.'" (ch 22). "Still in the box" is spoken once, by Marie-Jo, in chapter 1. The replacement of a village's joke about her by a worse one is the last comic turn in the book, and the reader has to be reminded what is being replaced.
*Do:* have the village say it at least twice more before chapter 22, to people other than Maylis. Kévin comes late to the loto. Somebody comes late to the café.

**10. Footnotes are put in characters' mouths in the first two chapters. Chapters 1 and 2.**
"'For anyone who has managed not to see one, it's a frame in the shape of a bull with fireworks tied along it, and a man carries it through the crowd alight.'" (ch 2). The mayor is explaining the bull to the committee that voted it last year. In chapter 1 a postman asks which building is the mairie so that Larrère can define one. Both are there for a reader who does not know the words. Both cost the scene its rhythm, and the second makes the sharpest person in the book say something she would never say.
*Do:* give each gloss to the narrator in a clause. If the mairie line stays in Larrère's mouth, cut it to the part that is a joke: the mayor, in his car, saying where a village keeps its mayor.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The life of "I'll be brief".** Coralie at the oven door in chapter 1 ("'In my tray,' she said, and shut the door on him"), the eleven minutes in chapter 10 ("At four minutes he glanced at the timer, and went on with more confidence"), the garden gate in chapter 21 ("Nothing came"), and the oven again in chapter 22 ("'The—'"). No line of it.
2. **Either, from the eggs to the river.** "Eggs is what Either gets in this house" (ch 1). "'That's Either in a good coat'" (ch 11). "She had said both, and it had been a van's answer" (ch 19). "'I know that word, Maylis. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it'" (ch 22). Fault 5 trims one repeat and leaves the spine alone.
3. **Jeannot's answer to the telephone, and where it stands.** "'And six. Bugger off.'" (ch 16). It comes straight after the night of 2014, with no paragraph between to prepare it.
4. **Kévin's fingers.** The loader bucket (ch 3), the yard ("'Five,' he said", ch 19), the van step ("'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything as much'", ch 22).
5. **Room twelve in chapter 3.** "'She said it had been told twice and given two lots of stars, and it got through them early and came at one.'" and "'It's in the plan now. Staff to wait. Staff not to join in.'" The giggling outside that door is what chapter 19 costs.
