# Critic report, round 4: Prose

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `22.md`, in order, in full (57,300 words). Nothing else.

Counts below are across the whole manuscript.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." (ch. 2) The sum is exact and it is given early. It stays a sum until ch. 16.
- **Tension: 6.** "She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it went." (ch. 19) That sentence is a 10. Chapters 10 to 14 have nothing like it, and the prose there keeps stopping to take stock.
- **Progression: 6.** "'Monday the thirtieth of November. Prevented from eight, with Monsieur Tastet as mayor from nine.'" (ch. 15) This is the sixth time the reader is given the events of that Monday.
- **Comedy: 7.** "'There's nothing here that lets me un-receive it. There's no column.'" (ch. 12) Very funny, in one key. The Good Place has four or five keys.
- **Characters: 7.** "'She says which. Then she says why. Then she tells you which you ought to have had.'" (ch. 10) The supporting cast each have a way of speaking. The two leads are told apart by one word and one shut hand.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Here,' said Monsieur Daguerre, to the roof of the van." (ch. 19) The running joke is carried into the flood and makes it worse.
- **Prose: 7.** "She had said on Thursday night, standing, that she would sit." (ch. 12) The sentences are disciplined and observed, and too many of them are in code. Without the code it is an 8.
- **The ending: 8.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (ch. 22)
- **Clarity: 4.** "That chair had never been hers. Somebody had sat down in it behind her, and she knew now which door that person had slept behind." (ch. 20) I worked out what this meant. A reader should not have to.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes, with a real risk of stopping between ch. 10 and ch. 14.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who reads Penelope Fitzgerald, yes. On the reader the bar names, no, not yet.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below the bar as it stands.** Sentence for sentence it is better written than Crouch. It is delivered much less well than either comparison.

1. **Crouch states his rules in plain words, once, early.** The box in Dark Matter and the chair in Recursion are explained by someone who understands them, and the reader spends the rest of the book afraid of what the rules allow. The Good Place does the same with its points and its orientation film. This book gives its rules as a riddle read off a forum by a man who has not understood them: "'Whoever wakes first goes home, it says, and if you're still sat there you go home with them.'" (ch. 3). The narrator never restates them. The central bargain in ch. 11 ("'Have Friday,' the other one said. 'I'll have yours.'") is conducted in that riddle language, and the choice in ch. 18 depends on it.
2. **A Crouch chapter ends on a danger that has just got nearer.** This book has four endings of that kind (ch. 4, 5, 17, 19) and they are as good as his. Most of the others end on a tableau, or on a bystander asking who she is.
3. **Above both in one thing.** The object work is better than Crouch's and as good as the best of The Good Place's callbacks: the reflector "screwed on crooked", the two fingers on a wrist, the pad with its boxes, the pillow, 02:51. Each is planted plain and paid in a different chapter.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

One pattern lies under the first four. **The narrator is coded where it should be plain, and plain where it should trust.** How the crossing works, who is in whose body, and what each woman plans are given in shorthand. What a scene meant is then spelled out. Reverse the two and most of this list goes.

**1. The mechanics and the plans are written in private shorthand.** (ch. 3, 7, 11, 12, 13, 18, 20) "She had said on Thursday night, standing, that she would sit. She had been told what that left, which was a van on a dyke with her laid back in it and nobody at the gate. But there was one chair, and the other one meant to have it too." (ch. 12) "Standing" means "in the steel room, when both were on their feet". "Sit" means "ride in the other woman's body and leave my own empty". "The relief" means Lesca. None of this is ever said. Nor is the rule for who moves the shared body: in ch. 8 the visitor has the legs and the left hand, in the first scene of ch. 13 the host walks out and counts signs, and by evening the host is "a passenger in her own arms". The reader cannot fear the choice in ch. 18 without knowing what each outcome does.
*Do:* give the narrator one flat paragraph in ch. 6, after the first crossing, that says what happened in ordinary words: who stayed seated, whose body she rode in, what became of her own, what brings her back. Say it again in two sentences where the bargain is struck in ch. 11. Replace "standing", "sit", "the relief" and "was given" with what they refer to.

**2. "The other one" is used 62 times and changes referent every chapter.** (throughout; worst in ch. 8, 13, 18) The book already has a perfect device: the narrator calls one woman Maylis and the other Lesca. Then it throws it away. "At six on Monday an alarm rang in the wet house, the other one got up, and Lesca went with her." (ch. 8) "The other one read the slate." (ch. 13) In ch. 13 "the other one" is Lesca; in ch. 8 and 18 it is Maylis. Add "the mayor", "the mouth", "the right hand" and "its owner", and a page can have five names for two people.
*Do:* in narration, always Maylis and Lesca. Keep "the other one" for speech. Open every crossing chapter with one plain line: whose village, whose body, who is riding.

**3. The same events are retold six times.** (ch. 8, 9, 11, 15) Lesca's Monday in Maylis's body is shown in ch. 8, then listed by Lafitte on his pad (ch. 8), by Larrère from his folder (ch. 9), by Madame Ducasse from the register (ch. 11), by the two women to each other in the steel room (ch. 11), by Larrère again from the register (ch. 15) and by Lesca in her *Received* column (ch. 15). The narrator adds its own: "She had gone over on Monday to tell a village. She had told a mayor, a gendarme, a café and a woman with a list, and the café had elected her for a joke and gone back to its cards." (ch. 11) Each list is well made. Together they are why the middle third does not move.
*Do:* keep Lafitte's pad, because it is the funniest, and Larrère's reading in ch. 15, because it ends on "did you—". Cut the narrator's recaps. Cut the steel-room exchange in ch. 11 down to the trade.

**4. The narrator tells the reader what the scene meant, and that it mattered.** Two forms.
*Explaining:* "*Nil.*" is the best single word in the book, and it is followed by "Staying added the other life to hers, and took nothing of her own away." (ch. 15) The night at the gate is shown whole, then: "She had been at the gate in good time, with a telephone in her hand, and she had chosen." (ch. 16) The hall: "She saw what the chair was. [...] She would have the quiet for good, with a card in front of her, and no morning to get up to." (ch. 18) The grave: "On the first morning she had read a stone as an eighth grave, and thought it was her own." (ch. 22)
*Flagging:* a turn is marked by saying it had never happened before. "which nobody at that table had heard him do" (ch. 10); "which nobody in that hall had seen her do" (ch. 13); "In all her years beside that car Maylis had never seen it done" (ch. 8); "It was the first question the association had ever put to Maylis." (ch. 5); "which she had never done since she ran it" (ch. 6). "Nobody" appears 89 times.
*Do:* cut the four explaining sentences outright. Keep the "never before" marker for the car window in ch. 8 and Marie-Jo's uncounted coins, and drop the rest.

**5. "She meant to" is the book's only way of pointing forward.** 27 uses; four in ch. 14, three in ch. 18. "Maylis meant to stay sitting in the morning, and go home with her, and see how that was done." (ch. 9) Lesca's plan for the Friday is announced in ch. 11 ("She meant to be at that gate on the Friday night herself"), repeated to Maylis in the same chapter, and announced twice more in ch. 18. The scene that follows then does what was announced.
*Do:* keep the intention only where the next scene breaks it (ch. 17, "She meant to be awake when the telephone rang"). Elsewhere end the section on the last thing done.

**6. Three chapters in four end on the same question.** "'Is it you? And where have you been, Maylis?'" (ch. 10). "'What have you been turning, Maylis?'" (ch. 12). "'Which of you is that?'" (ch. 13). The same beat also falls inside chapters: "'Who is that?'" (ch. 6), "'Was that you?'" (ch. 7), "'Who is it wants her at nine?'" (ch. 8), "'Which of you was that?'" (ch. 13). The reader has known the answer since ch. 6, so the question pulls nothing.
*Do:* keep ch. 13's, since Kévin is the one who can understand the answer. End ch. 10 and ch. 12 on the river: a gauge reading, a day nearer.

**7. Every paragraph has a number in it, so the four that matter do not stand out.** Roughly one number word every 45 words. Nine strings, seven buckets, fourteen churches, ninety-one players, thirty-eight votes, thirty-one euros, fifty-four sandbags, eleven drops before Bayonne. Both women count, and that is character. But the book rests on 02:51, ten minutes against twenty-five, ninety over, and forty-one, and the reader has been trained to let figures go by.
*Do:* cut the figures that are only texture, a third of them at least. In ch. 16, 17 and 19 leave no number on the page that is not one of the four or a clock.

**8. Signature phrases are reused word for word.** Inguma's descent in ch. 1 ("the size of a ewe", "the sound of a sack let down off a trailer", "as a dog gets up") is copied into ch. 4 nearly entire, and "as a dog gets up" again in ch. 7. "With its back to her" and its variants 14 times; "lower than she used it" 5; "glass of white" 6; "tails to the river" 5; "hand on the frame" 5; "dressed for the night" 4; "turned on his stool, the whole of him" twice. Two refrains pay and must stay exact: 02:51, and the two fingers on the wrist, which ends on Madame Etcheto in ch. 19. The others are description said again.
*Do:* in ch. 4 let Lesca see the descent in her own words. Elsewhere give the phrase once.

**9. One sentence shape, one paragraph ending.** 108 sentences open "It was"; 124 open "She had"; "had" appears 955 times; "stood" 203; "looked at" 81; a trailing ", with ..." 276 times. A new figure is introduced by "It was" plus a name at least a dozen times ("It was Monsieur Darrigrand." "It was Kévin Labat." "It was Jeannot."). There is no semicolon, no exclamation mark, no colon outside a clock time and no dash outside three broken-off speeches, in a book with a flood and a fire bull. Paragraphs close on a short pluperfect turn: "After the third letter she had bought grease and stopped writing." "He had left the little off. She heard it go." Each is good. As the standard ending they flatten the page. The book can do otherwise: "She had not run since the rib, and it let her have a third of each breath, and the mud took her boots to the ankle, and she ran with the torch in her left hand" (ch. 19).
*Do:* let syntax follow the event in ch. 8, 17 and 18 as it does in ch. 19. Halve the "It was" openers. End one paragraph in three on something other than a turn.

**10. Glossary put in mouths, one dialect for everyone, and period diction.** A French mayor defines a mairie to a French postman: "'The one with the flag. It's where a village keeps its mayor, its council and its registers.'" (ch. 1) Lesca explains the bull to the committee that has run it for years: "'For anyone who has managed not to see one, it's a frame in the shape of a bull'" (ch. 2). The glass-eel paragraph in ch. 3 stops the scene for a guidebook entry. Everyone in the village shares one idiom ("I've a", "I'll not" 22 times, "you'd a"), so Kévin, in his twenties and on a forum, says "'I'll not put it on the forum unless you say'" exactly as Jeannot and Arlette would. Smartphones are "telephones" 84 times and Jeannot, born about 1968, hears the forecast "on the wireless". The job-drawn simile ("as she would have asked the prefecture for the name of a form", "as she would have read it off a chart", "as a man tries a door he hopes is locked") appears about twenty times and is the only kind of simile the narrator has.
*Do:* move each gloss into one narrator clause. Give Kévin, Bixente and the clerks a present-day register and keep the idiom for those over sixty. Keep one job-simile per character.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 5, the winch meeting, entire.** All twelve "through the gap", "It'll have sunk", the motor killed by "Then the State has the key", and "'Either.'" as the answer to the only question she is ever asked.
2. **Chapter 19 from "02:59." to "'Forty-one,' Coralie said."** The clock times, the one long running sentence, Kévin's "Five", Jeannot's three points in a flooded doorway, the two fingers on the wrist in room twelve.
3. **The sentence at the microphone in ch. 20 and its walk through the crowd.** "she watched her sentence go back through six thousand people at a walk", "A child asked what the lady had done, and was told", and Marie-Jo's "'Maylis'" followed by the chairs.
4. **Jeannot's numbered voice, wherever it appears.** Letter seventeen (ch. 14), "'And six. Bugger off.'" (ch. 16), *No. 42* and the last line of the book.
5. **Coralie's pad, oven and pillow.** The oven impression in ch. 1 and its "'The—'" in ch. 22, the two boxes in ch. 7, "'That was Either, with the river in it'", and the eleven stairs.

## Usage and consistency

Spelling is British throughout with no slips found (metre, grey, colour, towards). Roman type for *mairie*, *tonne*, *loto* and the Basque is consistent. "The Port" and "the State" keep their capitals. "Madame le maire" and "Monsieur le maire" are consistent and used as a joke. Jean and Jeannot are kept apart by village, correctly.

To fix:

- **Letter numbers.** Figures in ch. 1 narration ("letter 41", "Letter 17"); words everywhere after ("number forty-one", "numbered seventeen", "number six"). Use figures only inside the italic *No. 41* and *No. 42*.
- **Dates in narration.** "Tuesday 24 November 2026" (ch. 1), "From 1 January" (ch. 5), "12 December" (ch. 15) against "the eleventh of December 2014" (ch. 7, 16). Pick one. Words in narration and speech, figures in documents, would match the rest.
- **Ch. 19, the key.** "She hooked it on her belt. It had not been off it since she had it from her father." She threw it to Jeannot twenty minutes earlier. It needs "until that night".
- **Ch. 12, the count of days.** On Tuesday the 1st Maylis has "ten days to it" (ch. 9). On Friday the 4th Jeannot has "eight days". By the same count it is seven.
- **Ch. 19 and 22, the call.** Ch. 19 opens "At eight minutes to three"; Sarthou reads the call as "Seven minutes to three". Both are right (she wakes at 02:52 and answers at 02:53), and it reads as a slip. Give the opener the call's time.
- **Ch. 13, "the jacket".** Kévin's leather jacket is described, and six lines later "Nobody had said who would have the jacket" means the commune's waterproof. Say "the waterproof".
- **Ch. 5.** "Seven men turned to hear." Larrère, who asked, is one of the seven and is in his car. Six turned.
- **Ch. 17.** "on the wireless", from a man of fifty-eight in 2026.
