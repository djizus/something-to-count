# Round 4: Reader

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `22.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else opened but the brief.

Lens: an ordinary reader with no special knowledge of anything in the book.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Behind it are forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. [...] And out on the meadows there is one man." (ch. 8) I can count what is at risk and I know the people. But I could not have said this sentence before the last line of chapter 4.
- **Tension: 6.** "'That has been stated to me twice,' Sarthou said. 'It has not occurred.'" (ch. 13) The warning is given five times between chapters 8 and 13 and absorbed as a joke each time. After the second time I stopped being afraid and waited for the date.
- **Progression: 6.** "So telling was finished with." (ch. 11) That is 3 December. The next thing either woman does about the flood is a telephone call in chapter 16, a week and five chapters later.
- **Comedy: 8.** "'Opened what? There's a name over it. It reads that she opened him.'" (ch. 2)
- **Characters: 8.** "'I don't cook Either,' she said. 'There's no recipe for it. Soup or duck.'" (ch. 1) Coralie is a person from her first line. So are Jeannot, Madame Etcheto and Larrère.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Five,' he said. He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." (ch. 19) The fingers were a joke in chapter 3 and are the worst moment of the flood.
- **Prose: 7.** "She looked at that chair as at a bench in the shade." (ch. 15) Every word is plain and I did not know what the sentence meant. The sentences are clean, and too often they keep back the one thing I needed.
- **The ending: 8.** "He fetched it. Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (ch. 22) It costs: one old woman, a job, a marriage, a village's regard. Coralie's leaving is done in a page and I did not believe it at that speed.
- **Clarity: 5.** "'It's one chair. Somebody sits and somebody gets up. It never was an exchange.'" (ch. 11) The third act hangs on this rule and it is delivered as a riddle.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. I would have put it down for a few days somewhere between chapters 11 and 13 and come back for Coralie.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On the friend who reads village comedy, yes, with the words "it starts at chapter 6". Not on the friend who reads Crouch. Having to give that warning is the measure of what is wrong.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, for this lens.** An ordinary reader asks two things: do I know what is going on, and can I stop. For roughly the first fifteen chapters the answers are "not always" and "yes". From chapter 15 to the end it is level with the comparisons or above them.

What puts it there:

1. **I cannot state the rules.** After one reading of Dark Matter anyone can say what the box does in a sentence. After one reading of this I can say that two women meet in a steel chair at 02:51, and I have to stop and work out the rest: who goes home with whom, what happens to the body left behind, why they cannot swap, what the empty chair in the hall is. The Good Place states each new rule in one plain line and then makes the joke. This book makes the joke (a forum's pinned list of don'ts) and leaves me to derive the rule.
2. **The clock starts late and then stops.** Crouch has his man taken by the end of the first chapter. Here the threat is first stated at the end of chapter 4, the first crossing is chapter 6, the women meet in chapter 7. Then from chapter 10 to chapter 15 the date is fixed, the warnings have failed, and I am shown meetings.
3. **What it does better.** Crouch makes me afraid for a marriage by telling me the man loves his wife. This book gives me a shopping pad with boxes on it, and a pillow that crosses a landing. The discovery that Inguma comes at 02:51 because that is the minute of an unanswered call re-reads the whole book in the way a Good Place reversal does. Neither comparison has a scene I will keep as long as chapter 15's telephone or chapter 19's corridor.

**The reader's four questions.**

*What I did not understand:*
- The rules of the chair (fault 1).
- In the van in chapter 18, which woman wanted the gate and which the track, and whose decision put Maylis on the track (fault 3).
- The hall with no walls, the people who are "a little more than one", and what sitting in the empty chair would mean (fault 5).
- Why Larrère wants the gate shut on the night. The breeze-block hut with no meter in chapter 9 seems to be the answer and is never mentioned again.
- The café talk in chapter 11 about cameras, a grant and a boy in prison.
- Why the rain of 2026 follows the file of 2014 to the centimetre. The book asks this in chapter 7 and answers that nobody is to blame. I accepted that. A Crouch reader will not.

*Where I was bored, lost or skimming:* the well and the kitchen at the head of chapter 4; the glass-eel paragraph in chapter 3; the café and the chair in chapter 11; the middle of the loto in chapter 13 (the wine vote, the cash box); the first page of each chapter in which one woman is inside the other, while I worked out whose village it was.

*Where I could not stop:* chapter 7 from "Tick one" to the chrysanthemums; chapter 8 entire; chapter 15 from the landing light on; chapters 16 to 21 without a break.

*What I will remember in a month:* the thing on the wardrobe made of leaf and rivets; Either, and eggs; a mayor talking through three centimetres of car window; seven men and a winch that stops at nineteen; a care plan that says staff are not to join in; an old man calling "Here"; "Not answered. Two fifty-one."; a hand counted in floodwater and coming out five; a sentence going back through six thousand people at a walk; a man who has said "I'll be brief" all book standing at a garden gate with nothing.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The rules of the chair are never said in plain words. (Chs. 7, 11, 12; structural.)**

> "Don't be the one still sitting. Whoever wakes first goes home, and if you're still sat there you go home with them, and it isn't your home. Don't count on staying. It fetches you, as soon as it's done counting whatever you gave it. Don't get up in there. And don't leave it the chair." (ch. 7)

> "'It's one chair. Somebody sits and somebody gets up. It never was an exchange.'" (ch. 11)

This is the whole mechanism and I got it second-hand from a poacher reading a forum off his telephone, as five prohibitions. Two of the five ("don't get up", "don't leave it the chair") are broken with no consequence I could name. The paragraph in chapter 11 that explains why the women cannot trade nights took me three readings, and chapter 12 opens on a decision ("she would sit") whose cost I had to reconstruct. Because I was unsure of the rules, I was unsure what to be afraid of on the Friday, and the tension of the middle chapters leaked away.

What I would do: keep Kévin's list as the joke, and give the rules once more straight. The place is chapter 7, when the two women first stand face to face. The mayor is a woman who states motions; let her state this one, with an example: whoever stays sitting when the other wakes goes home inside her; the body left behind lies with its eyes open; she stays until Inguma has finished counting what she set it. Then, before chapter 12, one sentence each: what Maylis means to do on Friday night and what it leaves undone, what Lesca means to do and what it leaves undone.

**2. The countdown stops between chapter 10 and chapter 15. (Structural.)**

> "So telling was finished with. She meant to be at that gate on the Friday night herself, in the other one's arms." (ch. 11)

From here to chapter 16 the plan does not change and nothing is done towards it. Six chapters show what happens when the wrong woman is at a table: Either taken as consent, eleven minutes, asleep at the budget, a pawl ordered, a vote at a bar, a laugh in the minute, a sash tied and untied. Each scene works. Together they are one idea played seven times while a river I have been told to fear rises off the page. This is where I would have put the book down.

What I would do: get from chapter 9 to chapter 15 in four chapters. The budget sleep (ch. 11) is the third "mayor prevented" beat and the wine vote (ch. 13) is the third three-minutes gag; one of each can go. Keep the laugh in the minute, the un-receiving of letter 41, Arlette's thumb on the weal, Sarthou at the microphone. And give the stretch one thing that cannot be minuted: something on the wet side that visibly gets worse or is lost before the Friday, so that I am counting days with them.

**3. At the moment of the central choice I cannot tell who is choosing. (Chs. 17, 18, 19.)**

> "The hand came off the key. It went down to the belt, where the key of the handle box hung, and shut on that. [...] The other one was going to the gate, and she was going for her." (ch. 18)

> "The words told her nothing. She had been to the tonne first." (ch. 19)

> "She thought she knew what had put a woman on a track at three in the morning. She had let go of that woman's hand to do it." (ch. 20)

Chapter 18 shows Maylis reaching for the gate key. Chapter 19 says she never meant anything else but the tonne. Chapter 20 has Lesca believing she sent her. I had waited seventeen chapters for this and came out not knowing whether Lesca misread the hand, or Maylis changed her mind, or the five words did it. If the point is that Lesca takes credit for a choice that was not hers, I need to be certain of what Maylis intended before she fell asleep, and the end of chapter 17 does not tell me: the hatch is bolted, she gets into the van, she puts her hand on the telephone. The women may be in doubt. The reader must not be.

What I would do: one sentence at the end of chapter 17 saying what she will do when it rings. In chapter 19, show what the hand on the gate key was for, from the inside.

**4. The threat is not stated until the end of chapter 4. (Chs. 1 to 4; structural.)**

> "That village had not been spared December 2014. It had not had it yet." (ch. 4)

This is the line that starts the book, and it arrives a fifth of the way in. Before it I have a creature, a second woman, forty letters against twelve, and a great deal of village. I liked the village. I did not yet know why I was being shown it. Two things slow the approach. Chapter 1 stops on its third page for the history of Jeannot's letters ("Most of the forty before it concerned the water in the low meadows..."), and chapter 3 for a paragraph on glass eels I did not need until chapter 9. And Inguma is named and the words taught twice in consecutive chapters, by Madame Etcheto in 3 and by Arlette in 4. The second time I was being told what I had just been told.

What I would do: Lesca has everything she needs to say it at the end of chapter 2 ("It was the twenty-fourth [...] she had woken under dry tiles to the sound of rain on a roof"). Bring the realisation to the end of chapter 3 at the latest. Open chapter 4 at the envelope and cut or move the well. Let Arlette confirm the name in two lines, since I already have it.

**5. The hall and its empty chair. (Chs. 15, 18, 20.)**

> "At each place somebody sat with their head down, and each of them was a little more than one, like a photograph of a person who had moved." (ch. 18)

> "A woman who sat down there was nobody's to fetch. She would have the quiet for good, with a card in front of her, and no morning to get up to." (ch. 18)

I took this to be death, or a place where all the versions sit, and I am still not sure which. It first appears in chapter 15, when a steel wall is suddenly not there, with nothing before it but a loto. Lesca's knees bend for it, and I had not been shown that she wanted to stop living. Who then sits down in it is given to me sideways in chapter 20: "she knew now which door that person had slept behind". I worked out that it was Madame Etcheto. I should not have had to work it out; it is the book's price.

What I would do: let the hall be heard through the wall of the steel room earlier (a voice calling numbers, in chapter 7 or 11), so that it is a known thing by chapter 15. Give Lesca one earlier moment of wanting the quiet. In chapter 20, name who sat down.

**6. Coralie leaves in a page. (Ch. 22.)**

> "'Stay.' It was out before Maylis had weighed it."

> "She heard the passage, and the front door, and the car door shut on a pillow."

In chapter 6 Coralie says "I'd have come for half that sentence, from the far end of the Port." For twenty-one chapters I have been taught that she is waiting to be asked. Maylis asks, in one word, unprompted. Coralie counts it, asks her question again, gets the grave for an answer and goes. The reasons are present (room twelve is "mine", "both" was "Either, with the river in it", nobody was in the house at seven) but they go by in twelve lines, in a chapter that also has to settle Larrère, Sarthou, Kévin, Madame Darrigrand, Lafitte, Maïté, Marie-Jo and Jeannot. The other Coralie's return in chapter 21 gets a landing, an hour and two church bells.

What I would do: give this scene the room chapter 21 gives its twin. Let Coralie say why "Stay" is not an answer to "What do you want". Take two of the walk-through-the-Port beats out of the chapter to pay for it.

**7. Larrère's reason, and two threads I could not follow. (Chs. 9, 11.)**

> "That hut had no box. Jeannot Castagnet's tonne stood three hundred metres from it, and on a still night in August he would have heard it run." (ch. 9)

> "'And what it cost. A boy from here in the prison at Bayonne for six months, over a bucket of worms.'" (ch. 11)

The first is, I think, an unmetered pump, and the reason a man keeps a gate shut on forty houses. It is shown once and never spoken of. At the end I can say why he rang from that kitchen. I cannot say why he gave that order. The second is the mayor's history with Kévin, given as overheard talk with the nouns missing; I understood it only when Kévin said "You sent me to Bayonne" a page later.

What I would do: one line from Jeannot, at the hatch or in letter 42, saying what Larrère has standing on those meadows. In chapter 11, let somebody in the café say "cameras on the gates" and "Kévin" in the same sentence before I walk in.

**8. Too many names that look alike, and too many numbers that might mean something. (Throughout.)**

> "The two Lassalle sisters. Madame Hirigoyen. Old Lafitte's brother. Madame Dufau, who doesn't know what year it is and knows that one. And Monsieur Castéra, who was carried." (ch. 18)

A reader sorts people by their first letter. This book has Lesca, Larrère, Labat, Lafitte and Lassalle; Darrigrand, Daguerre, Ducasse and Dufau; Castagnet and Castéra. I confused Darrigrand (the swept step, the baby) with Daguerre ("Here") until chapter 17, and Lafitte with Tastet for longer.

The numbers do the same. Nineteen is the turns of the winch, the days of rain and the sandbags at the home. Twelve is the years, the letters in the dry village, the room, the seats in the minibus. Forty is the letters and the houses; forty-two is the beds and the last letter; eleven is the years on the landing, Larrère's minutes, the ponies and the stairs. I could not tell which echoes I was meant to hear, so I stopped listening for them, and then nearly missed the one that matters (02:51).

What I would do: no two speaking characters with the same initial. Keep the numbers that carry weight (02:51, nineteen turns, forty-one and forty-two) and change the accidental ones.

**9. I lose my footing at the head of each crossing chapter. (Chs. 8, 10, 13, 14.)**

> "On Saturday the other one came home with Maylis at six, and stayed. On Friday night she had given Inguma every signature she had put to a paper as mayor, and a mayor, it seemed, signed a great deal." (ch. 13)

"She" in the second sentence is not the woman named last in the first. For the next page I am asking: which village, whose body, who is steering, who is underneath, how long has she got. The book has a perfect signal (rain or frost) and a clear naming rule (Maylis, Lesca) and at these openings uses neither soon enough.

What I would do: the first three lines of every chapter with two in one body say the weather, whose bed, who has the legs, and what Inguma was given to count. In that order, every time.

**10. People are told what they already know. (Chs. 1, 2.)**

> "'For anyone who has managed not to see one, it's a frame in the shape of a bull with fireworks tied along it, and a man carries it through the crowd alight.'" (ch. 2)

The mayor is explaining the village's own festival to the committee that organises it. I needed the information and was glad of it, but I could see whom it was for. The same with Larrère telling a postman what a mairie is, though there it passes as his way of not saying who he is.

What I would do: give the bull to the narrator in one sentence and let Lesca say only where the money goes.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The shopping pad.** The boxes, "Either gets eggs", "Tick one", the tick Lesca leaves and Coralie wants to look at again that night, and the last use of it: "That was Either, with the river in it."
2. **Chapter 15, the landing.** "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." And everything Coralie says after it, including that she does not want to hear which.
3. **Madame Etcheto and Monsieur Daguerre.** The care plan ("Staff to wait. Staff not to join in."), the duck held on her knees, "It counts quickly", every "Here", and room twelve exactly as it is found, down to the hatbox on the wardrobe.
4. **Chapter 19 as it is paced.** The clock times standing alone, "Both", the van on its axles, the bank going with the sound of gravel tipped, the corridor of chairs, the buzz at four o'clock, "Forty-one". And its echo in 22: "I wanted six, Maylis."
5. **Chapter 21.** Maïté's verb, and that it is the word on the other woman's card. "I'll be brief", and then nothing. The pillow.
