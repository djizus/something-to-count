# Round 4: Thriller lens

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `22.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else was opened.

**Verdict against the bar: below.** Chapters 16 to 19 are as good as Crouch at the same job. Chapters 1 to 15 are not, and a Crouch reader is most likely to put the book down in 13 or 14.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "It was twenty-five minutes on foot to that hatch. The bank had ten." (16) They are numbered and concrete. Until 16 they belong to Lesca's past, not to Maylis's Friday.
- **Tension: 6.** "The screen said 23:58. She had not seen it change." (17) Chapters 16 to 19 stay at that level. Chapters 10 to 14 have none of it.
- **Progression: 5.** "So telling was finished with." (11) That has been true since the end of 8. Nothing else is tried until 16.
- **Comedy: 7.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade.'" (9)
- **Characters: 8.** "'And six. Bugger off.'" (16) Jeannot alive is why the flood frightens.
- **Switch between dark and comic: 7.** "'Five,' he said. He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." (19) That is the switch at its best. At its worst it is the quip that defuses the hall in 18.
- **Prose: 7.** "Out on the meadows the dark began to shine." (16) The refrains (*Either*, *I'll be brief*, *in the minutes*, *lower than she used it*, *as a dog gets up*) are leaned on until they slow the reading.
- **The ending: 7.** "She heard the passage, and the front door, and the car door shut on a pillow." (22) It costs. It does not close the two women.
- **Clarity: 5.** "'Whoever wakes first goes home, it says, and if you're still sat there you go home with them.'" (3) The plot runs on this rule, and it is given once, as forum hearsay.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes, from the end of 4, but I pushed through 13 and 14.
**Press it on a friend?** On a friend who reads slow comic novels, yes. On a Crouch reader, not as it stands.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below**, for three reasons.

1. **The premise is in play late.** Crouch has his man abducted in chapter 1 and in the wrong world a chapter or two later. Here the second woman is named a third of the way into chapter 2, the first crossing is in 6, and the two first speak in 7, a third of the way through the book. Chapter 1 spends its first page on the wardrobe and the next 170 lines on leaves, a postman and trestles.
2. **The chapter ends do not get paid.** A Crouch chapter ends on a reversal that the next page has to deal with. Here 8, 10 and 12 end on a question from someone who loves her ("Are you coming up?", "where have you been, Maylis?", "What have you been turning, Maylis?"). The next chapter is in the other woman's head, and the answer is never given on the page.
3. **The marriage is not in danger where the reader can feel it.** Crouch makes the wife the reason for every act and puts the other self in her bed as the horror. Here the other self has soup, the wife likes her better, and the woman whose bed it is says "Have Friday. I'll have yours." The reader fears for Jeannot and hardly at all for Coralie.

**Level or above** in two places.

- **The twists are fair.** The kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice (16, 19, 21), the car in the back lane (9), the two bowls and the suitcase (7), and "Castagnet, house" on the screen are all planted. Crouch plays less fair than this.
- **The reversal re-reads the book, as The Good Place's do.** Coralie's call log in 15 turns 02:51 from Inguma's hour into the minute of the call. It also turns "No one came to tell him" into a true sentence that lies.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

### 1. The middle stops the clock (10 to 14, and the missing week)

> "'The wine is under-priced,' she said, to the first six in the queue. 'At that the fête clears nothing once the ham is paid for. I propose to put it up. Those in favour?'" (13)

This is the woman who came over to stop a flood, on one of her two days there, six days before it.

- **The swap-days repeat.** There are four after the first (8, 10, 13, 14), all with the same shape: the wrong woman in the body, the village baffled, Lafitte writing it down, Inguma fetching her.
- **Friday's odds do not change.** After 8, the only new fact about the flood is Jeannot's vow to go down, and that is made twice (8 and 13).
- **The rising river is offstage.** The wet village is not seen from Sunday 6 to Thursday 10 December. The river crossing the red line on the Tuesday was promised aloud in 8 ("Pau will send you a yellow, and you'll tell me the gate stays shut"). It happens in a subordinate clause in 16. Sarthou's fifty-four sandbags, the first sign that anyone believed her, get half a sentence in 17.

**What I would do:**
- Cut one swap-day. Chapter 14's sash on a Monday is the one to go; keep the laugh in the minute.
- Give Tuesday 8 December a chapter in the wet village. The line is crossed, the yellow arrives, and Larrère says word for word what the "mayor" told the café he would say. Then show who starts to believe her and what they do.
- Make every crossing after 8 leave Friday measurably better or worse.

### 2. The central choice is not on the page as a choice (18 and 19)

> "She had said both, and it had been a van's answer. She got out, left the telephone ringing, and went on." (19)

This is the moment the book exists for, and it gets two sentences. Three things blur it.

- **The two chapters disagree about who chose.** In 18 her hand goes to the gate key: "The other one was going to the gate, and she was going for her." In 19, from her own side, that reach does not exist and "The words told her nothing." In 21 Lesca says "I told her with her own mouth to go to the tonne first. She went."
- **Her reasoning in the dip cannot be followed.** She tells herself going on means "the river had the Port". At the hatch she says "I'm opening the gate." So she is either choosing Jeannot or still trying for both, and the reader cannot tell which.
- **The cruellest fact is thrown away.** "She had given it ten minutes. It had held sixteen." With sixteen minutes, both was possible. What stopped it was her own van across the track in front of the Trafic. The reader has to do that sum alone.

**What I would do:**
- Decide who chose and make 18 and 19 agree.
- Give the dip a full scene, with the gate lamp visible behind her and the red disc in front.
- Move Coralie's "The gate, or Castagnet? Say which, Maylis. For once" from the crown to the dip. On the crown "Both" is still a plan. In the dip she would have to answer in Coralie's ear, and the pillow in the car in 22 would have its cause on the page.
- At 03:06, with the Trafic stopped a metre short of her van, let the reader see that those were the minutes.

### 3. A fortnight's foreknowledge, and she falls asleep (17)

> "It had found her at a bar, with a hall looking on, and she did not mean to be held to a seat at nine minutes to three with a telephone ringing." (17)

The chapter plants exactly the right fear and then does not use it. She nods off at midnight: "Then she had the telephone in her hand, and her eyes shut."

The catastrophe then depends on the heroine dozing through the three hours she has known about for two weeks.

The same chapter says "A man who would not open could not be carried." In 19 she takes him "by the collars of both jackets with her left hand" and has him out in six lines. If she can do that at 03:03 she could do it at 18:00, or sit on the hatch all night.

**What I would do:**
- Keep her awake to 02:50 and let Inguma come down in the van at its minute. She said the words and "It was not true", so it comes. She is pinned with the telephone ringing in her hand. That is the fear the chapter set, and it puts her in the steel room by the book's own rule rather than by tiredness.
- Shut the dusk door properly. Either she does get him out and he drives back down past her at eleven, or the hatch at 03:03 opens for a reason it did not have at 17:30 (see fault 9).

### 4. The impostor threat has no teeth (6, 8, 11)

> "'You had soup with her.' 'Yes.' 'She says you answered everything she asked. She liked it.' 'So did I.'" (11)

Chapter 6 ends on the best threat in the book: "and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it." Nothing follows from it.

- Lesca gets as far as the fourth stair once, and never tries again.
- Maylis feels nothing about it that the reader is shown.
- Nobody is ever at risk of being locked out of her own life.

**What I would do:**
- Let Lesca get further once: up the stairs, onto the wall side of the bed, with Coralie choosing the one who answers.
- Have Maylis watch it from underneath and be unable to say a word against it.
- Make Lesca's not coming back a live possibility for one chapter.

Then "I'll have yours" is a retreat by a woman who has seen herself beaten, not a swap.

### 5. Chapter 1 spends the opening's credit (1 and 2)

> "The loto was on Saturday week, and Marie-Jo Labat wanted the tables up for it now, to see what she was short of." (1)

After the wardrobe and the stone, the reader has one question (what was that thing?) and is given a working Tuesday. The fact that makes the Tuesday frightening is held back to chapter 2: "He had been dead for twelve years less eighteen days."

**What I would do:** reorder. Put the first section of chapter 2, down to "she had gone down to the gate and turned the handle", directly after the first section of chapter 1. The reader then watches Jeannot laugh at his van window already knowing he is drowned next door, and the postman, the rake and the eggs carry dread at no cost in words.

### 6. The rules are hearsay and their threats are cancelled (3, 7, 18)

> "'Nobody's said. Nobody they believed has done it and come back to type.'" (7)

Thirty lines later: "'Good,' the mayor said. 'You can get up. That saves a week.'"

- The fourth don't is broken for nothing.
- The fifth, "don't leave it the chair", is not explained until it has been survived.
- The hall with no morning in it is refused with a quip: "The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies."
- Inguma's counting could be a countdown, and is only ever an exit line: "Somewhere the last kernel went onto the last card."
- The flood itself has no law. Lesca's "I let it across" is refuted in ten lines in 7, nothing replaces it, and the question the middle should run on (can it be stopped?) cannot be asked.

A reader fears only what he can work out.

**What I would do:**
- Have the two women state the governing rule once, plainly, in 7.
- Make standing up cost something that is seen.
- Let the visitor feel the count running down inside the scene.
- Give the hall one real pull before she walks.

### 7. The secret is guessed in chapter 2 and kept until 16 (2, 7, 11, 15)

> "'Did you know he was out?' she said. 'No one came to tell him,' Lesca said. 'That isn't what I asked,' Maïté said." (2)

From that page the reader knows she knew. For thirteen more chapters the book is in her head and steps round it: "The dyke road was there, in the rain, and what stood at the end of the track." (15) That is the author withholding, not the character.

The real surprises are strong enough without it: the one ring at 02:51, Coralie having known for twelve years, Coralie in the building, and Larrère in the kitchen.

**What I would do:** give the reader the headlamps on the van early, by 7. Hold back only the telephone, Coralie and the kitchen. Then 15 and 16 answer "for whom?", which is the better question.

### 8. Cliffhangers are answered offstage (8 to 9, 10 to 11, 12 to 13)

> "'Is it you? And where have you been, Maylis?'" (10)

Coralie has sat thirteen hours by a body with its eyes open. What Maylis says to her is never shown. The same goes for "Are you coming up?" (8) and "What have you been turning, Maylis?" (12). Chapter 13 ends on a line Coralie already spoke in the middle of it: "Which of you is that?"

After the third one the reader stops trusting the hook.

**What I would do:** answer each at the top of that world's next chapter, even with an evasion that costs her. Otherwise end those chapters on something the alternation can pay.

### 9. The kitchen twist does no work at the climax (19)

> "Under the time it said Castagnet, house." (19)

At 02:53 she learns the one thing that would bring a stubborn man up a ladder without a hand laid on him. She carries it down the track and does not use it. The twist becomes paperwork for Sarthou on Sunday.

**What I would do:** at the hatch, with no minutes left, have her say where Larrère is ringing from. Jeannot comes up for that, and it costs him his marriage in one line. The bus, "I saw the wardrobe" and letter 42 then pay twice. It also repairs fault 3's question of why the hatch opens at 03:03 and not at dusk.

### 10. The two women never close (18 to 22)

> "Then she had no hand, and no rain." (18)

Their last contact is a wrestle over an ignition key.

- Maylis never learns that Lesca said it into a microphone.
- Lesca's "I sent it to her" is said to Coralie, not to the woman it was done to.
- The trade the book set up in 11, Friday for Saturday and the chair for the sash, dissolves without either of them saying so to the other.

**What I would do:** one last steel room on the Saturday night, short, both standing and the chair empty, one with forty-one and the other with *drowned him*. The least that would serve: let Lesca's sentence cross to Maylis in 22, as the hall crossed to Lesca in 20.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The last two lines of 4.** "That village had not been spared December 2014. It had not had it yet." This is the book's best turn.
2. **The motion at the end of 6**, in full, with the comma made from the full stop.
3. **Coralie's call log in 15.** "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." And "Nine minutes to three. I've had that for twelve years."
4. **All of 16.** The reflector "screwed on crooked, burning red", the water that "put it out", the nine rings and Jeannot's six numbered points.
5. **Chapter 19 from the breach to "Forty-one".** The clock times, the street lamps going out "one after another", Kévin's five fingers in the headlamps, Monsieur Daguerre's "Here", and the line through the last name in room twelve.
