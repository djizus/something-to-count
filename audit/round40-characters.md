# Critic report: Characters

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** A marriage, a man in a cellar, forty-two beds and the use of her own body, all personal and all clear: "It's a whole life with one hand. I won't pay that."
- **Tension: 7.** Chapters 12 to 14 are an 8 or better; chapter 7 is a 5. "She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it broke."
- **Progression: 6.** The middle goes in and out of the same chair and takes back its own gains: "Maylis had kept out of the chair for two nights. She meant to have a third before she asked Coralie back."
- **Comedy: 7.5.** The village is at the level of the comparison; the two leads are not. "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station."
- **Characters: 7.5.** Coralie and Jeannot are 9. Maylis is 7. Lesca is 6.5. "I'd have this one again."
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** The same gag carries both without a join: "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'"
- **Prose: 7.5.** Plain and exact, and it thinks in the character's trade: "It wasn't much. She took more than that to the dump in a morning."
- **The ending: 7.** Maylis's ending is an 8, Lesca's is a 6: "Lesca left her door open and the lamp on", which is where chapter 3 left her.
- **Clarity: 6.** Too many rules stand between the reader and the two women: "The owner keeps her right hand."

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. From chapter 10 I could not have stopped.

Would I press it on a friend? Yes, for Coralie and for chapter 14, and I would warn them about chapter 4.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Above Crouch on the wife and on everyone who is not the lead.** Daniela in Dark Matter and Helena in Recursion are what the hero stands to lose; they are seen through his wanting. Coralie wants something that has nothing to do with Maylis ("I wanted to be her before I'd heard of Peyrebarthe"), fancies the wrong one out loud, refuses her on a principle from her own work, and leaves for the job. Nobody in Wayward Pines is as particular as Jeannot counting his grievances or Larrère's window.

**Level with The Good Place on people who are each wrong in their own way.** Larrère will not sign, Jeannot will not be told, Sarthou will not leave a form, Josiane has the right house and the wrong name, Maylis will not choose, Lesca will not ask. The chorus is as good as the show's.

**Below both on the central pair.** Dark Matter keeps the double in the husband's bed for most of the book, so the fear for the marriage never lets up. Here the double is stopped on the fourth stair in chapter 5 and on the landing in chapter 8, and she is not a rival at all by the time the bank goes. The Good Place lets Eleanor and Chidi argue until the viewer knows what each is to the other. Maylis and Lesca meet five times, every meeting is chaired, and they never have it out.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Lesca gets her name back from a Coralie who does not know what she did. (Chapter 16)**
"'Good night, Maylis,' she said, and went into the spare room. Lesca left her door open and the lamp on."
Her Coralie was told she was going over "to get him out". She was never told about the motion ("and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it"), the stairs, the landing, or room twelve. Lesca confesses 2014 to two hundred people, which the book half defends, and confesses 2026, which it does not, to an old woman in a car park in six lines. She also declines her own death in her prefecture voice ("apologies received") and Madame Etcheto takes the chair. The bill for everything Lesca does this December goes to somebody else, and the one person whose verdict she wants gives it on half the facts. That is softening. Have Lesca tell her own Coralie what she crossed for, and say the sentence the book opened on to "the woman it was for", or the half of it Coralie said she would have come for. Then let Coralie say "Good night, Maylis" or not say it.

**2. The fight over Coralie is finished before the climax. (Chapter 13)**
"Rain was loud on the roof of the van. She had crossed for that sound, and for a bed with two pillows."
The most frightening sentence in the book is Lesca's motion in chapter 3. By the van it has become weather and furniture. In the van the two women fight over a steering wheel and a man in a hut; Coralie is at stake only from the water. Lesca never learns that Maylis asked on the doorstep and was told 7. Give her that in the chair, since "what one of us knows, the other gets". Then holding the wheel is holding on to being the one in the house at 7, and "I'm letting go" gives up Coralie by name. The triangle and the flood would then break in the same minute.

**3. The room where nobody can lie is never used on the question that defines Lesca. (Chapters 11 and 17)**
"'I opened it for Coralie.' That sounded no more true, and no less."
Chapter 11 says outright that her bedroom is not the steel room and she can tell herself anything there. Chapter 17 then puts her standing in the steel room and nobody asks. The last meeting is thirty lines, and even: one admission each, an envelope, lights out. Maylis has lost her job, her wife and a resident to this woman and says "Don't come again." Make Maylis ask which it was for. Let Lesca try both sentences standing. Whatever comes out, or sticks, is the answer the book owes, and it gives Maylis something to be angry with.

**4. For three chapters the two women are a jaw and a right hand. (Chapters 5, 7 and 8)**
"Lesca pulled at the jaw and Maylis held it, so all Josiane got was a noise through the nose."
The point of view is Maylis's, so Lesca's mind is shut; Maylis is not acting, so hers narrows to clenching. Through the whole bingo scene the protagonist's plan is to keep her teeth together. "The legs", "the eyes", "the mouth" make a body nobody owns. The one time Maylis takes the mouth for a joke is the best moment in chapter 5: "'Put it in forty-two,' she said, in her own voice", and Jeannot sees who he is talking to. Do that more. Let the village hear two women disagree out of one face, twice a scene, and let Maylis win on wit where she cannot win on strength.

**5. Nobody can say what Larrère did. (Chapters 12, 14 and 16)**
"You knew he was out there, Guy. You stood in my kitchen that night and rang her from my phone, with his van gone down to the fields, and you never told the woman you'd sent to the gate."
This silences him, but Lesca saw the van herself, so his silence changed nothing the reader can point to. His reason for "shut" is three things at once: the corn ("three hundred hectares"), the affair, and Jeannot ("He stays dry as long as you do what you're told"). I half inferred that an order to clear the fields sends the husband home to a kitchen with Larrère in it. If that is the design, it is the best thing about him and nobody says it. One line from Maïté, naming what a cleared field would have cost him, makes his guilt as exact as his window.

**6. Maylis is let down gently. (Chapter 17)**
"'One,' she said, in the voice she called the numbers in. 'Just the one. Sit down on it.'"
She says "I had the key" three times, but the only person who lays it on her without a cushion is Madame Darrigrand, in ten lines. A chair, a tow and a shovel follow within pages. The death falls where it is easiest to carry: a woman of 94, back in her room by her own will, with a share taken by Coralie ("That part is mine") and a share by Lesca ("It was you." "Yes."). The daughter who is burying her never appears. Put the daughter at the cemetery gate in place of the priest, with the words written out in her hand, and let Maylis measure the grave in front of her. Jeannot's shovel will mean more after that.

**7. The first meeting with herself is an agenda. (Chapter 4)**
"She sat much straighter than Maylis did. There was nothing wrong with her ribs."
That is all either woman sees of what the other became. Four numbered points of rules follow. Maylis's envy arrives two chapters later and is excellent ("She liked it. She slowed down to get more of it"). Lesca's never arrives in words: she looks at a raincoat she handed in and a bed with two pillows and the narration does not say what that is like. Six lines before the first item: what each of them looks at first.

**8. Coralie keeps explaining Maylis, and "Either" does Maylis's talking. (Chapters 1, 4, 8, 17)**
"I've waited a long time to be asked for one thing in this house."
The diagnosis is given in chapter 1 ("Sixteen years, and you've told me once what you wanted") and restated in 4, 8 and 17. Maylis's own voice in chapter 1 is quick ("Don't. He'd only ring me to dig the hole"). After chapter 2 she measures and says one word. Her real idiom is the measurement offered where a feeling should be ("It's twenty minutes to Bayonne"), which is funnier and sadder than "Either" and is used twice. Cut the restatement in chapter 4, and give her three or four more deflections of the chapter 1 kind in the middle of the book. She is the lead and the least good company in her own kitchen.

**9. "Stay" is thrown away. (Chapter 17)**
"'Stay.' 'That's what you want,' Coralie said."
Sixteen years of boxes lead to that word, and it goes by in one line. Half a page later Coralie asks "What do you want, Maylis?" as if it had not been said, so the answer about the grave lands as the first answer and the real first answer is lost. Either "Stay" is the answer and Coralie has to stand in front of it for a beat before she refuses, or Maylis does not say it until the question is asked with no box. Not both.

**10. Lesca's village is peopled by job titles, and Maylis has no mother. (Chapters 3, 6, 11, 15; chapter 1)**
"'The timer?' the secretary said. 'You have it, Madame le maire. It's in the inventory.'"
The secretary is in three chapters with some of the best lines and has no name. Nor has the deputy mayor, the priest or "the young man from Pau". Maylis's village is all names, so it is the one the reader would miss. Name the secretary and the deputy. And Arlette, who sits by Lesca's bed with her coat on, does not exist in Maylis's village, alive or dead. One line in chapter 1 settles it; a living Arlette with ponies still on the flood fields would do more.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. Coralie on the landing: "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them."
2. The oven door, all three times, and above all the third, where she cannot finish it.
3. Kévin's hand in the headlights and what he says about it afterwards: "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much."
4. "Twelve. One, two." and the chair pulled in, with "Forty-one" at the end of chapter 14.
5. "She wanted it not to be her", and the letter that answers it: "It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you."
