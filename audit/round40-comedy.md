# Critic report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else opened but the brief.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** "'I'd have had you. You answer. But what I'd have had is a river over forty-two beds.'" (9) The price of the love story and the price of the flood are the same price, said in one breath.
- **Tension: 7.** "She wrote 47 under 48." (6) The pulse chart under the mayor's day is the tightest thing in the book. Chapters 9 to 12 do not hold that grip: the date is fixed, and the book waits for it.
- **Progression: 6.5.** "She crossed out the line about the trapdoor and wrote it again at the top, above the key, with *before dark* after it." (10) The plan is written, questioned and rewritten on the same pad across three chapters, and Maylis asks for the gate and is told "shut" eight times before Friday.
- **Comedy: 7.5.** "'Opened what? Castagnet, Opened. It reads that he was opened.'" (3) I smiled from the postman in chapter 1 to the council table in chapter 6 without a break, and again through 8. It is one register, played very well.
- **Characters: 8.** "'Because you'd have told me.'" (11) Coralie, in both villages, is the best company in the book and the funniest person in it.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.5.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'" (17) A running joke that fails on purpose, and the failure is the grief.
- **Prose: 7.** "Her legs swung out of the bed and carried her down to the kitchen. She hadn't told them to." (4) Plain and exact, and the deadpan lives in it. The closing gloss ("She hadn't told them to", "Maylis hadn't told it to", "Maylis hadn't said it") is a habit, and so is the paragraph of minutes and metres.
- **The ending: 8.** "'It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you.'" (17) It costs a death, a job and a marriage, and it still lets Jeannot be Jeannot.
- **Clarity: 6.** "Inguma hadn't taken her home. She had held the wheel against it for a whole ring, and this was where that put her." (13) The rules of the crossing arrive one at a time, each at the moment the plot needs it. Farce needs the rules known before the door slams.

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. Would I press it on a friend? Yes, with the instruction to read to the end of chapter 5 before deciding.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Just below The Good Place on comedy. Level with it on making the moral problem funny. Above it on the switch.** Crouch is not funny at all, so on this lens the book is simply ahead of him: his people are never company the way Jeannot or Coralie are.

- **Below, on range.** The Good Place has a fool, a snob, a liar and a man with a stomach ache, and four kinds of joke. This book has three kinds: a catchphrase comes back; a form is applied to something that is not paperwork ("It's soup. It doesn't need a vote."); a room misreads what it hears ("She wants water"). Every one of them is dry. Nobody in Peyrebarthe is allowed to be silly. By chapter 9 I could predict the shape of the next joke, though not its words.
- **Level, on the flaw as engine.** "Either" is Chidi's stomach ache. It is a pleasure for eleven chapters ("I don't cook Either. There's no recipe for it."; "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.") and then it decides the night: "'Either,' Maylis said. / 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said." The verb for the stone is the trolley problem run as a committee, and it is not made small: Darrigrand's "It's four when you're the one counting. When it's your house it's one" is as hard as anything in the show.
- **Level, on reversals.** "You're the water" re-reads every warning Lesca gave. "Castagnet, house" on the phone screen, with the kitchen clock behind it, re-reads "No one came to tell him". Josiane's "I only had the one name wrong" re-reads the whole adultery farce as evidence.
- **Above, on the switch.** The Good Place rarely lets a gag fail. Here the gags are the instruments of the dark: the oven door that cannot be finished, "Twelve. One, two. / Somebody pulled a chair in to a table", the café's new greeting. "The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies" in the hall of the dead is funnier and colder than anything in the show's afterlife.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The steel room is funny once. (4, 9, 13)**
In chapter 4 the meeting of the two women is the book's best comic invention: "'Then it's carried, or it's a tie, and in a tie the chair decides.' / 'There's nobody in the chair.'" After that the room goes plain. In chapter 9, where Lesca is at her most wrong, she delivers a rule as a speech: "'A week ago you asked me why it comes, and I told you I didn't know. Now I know. It comes with me. Keeping me out for a night or two changes nothing, because the river catches up the day I'm back. One night matters.'" No agenda, no item, no vote. The room where the two versions of one person meet is the centre of the premise, and it is the place the comedy leaves first. It also costs Lesca: a woman ready to drown a village for a bed with two pillows is bearable while she minutes it, and merely grim when she does not. And it starves the last scene, where "She didn't call anything to order" should pay three meetings and pays one.
*Do:* run both chair scenes in chapter 9 as meetings. Lesca hides in procedure hardest when she has most to hide: the Friday rule goes in as an item, "Both" is moved by Maylis and ruled out of order, "Nil" is entered as the figure it already is. Let the form crack in 13 ("Lesca had answers") and be gone in 17.

**2. There is no fool, and the nearest thing to one is kept off the page. (2 to 12)**
Kévin is the only person whose comedy is not dry: "'Does it hurt? Can I go up in the bucket?'", "'Do I turn it off? Will they mind it, at the care home?'" He speaks in chapter 2 and then not again in his own village until chapter 12. In chapter 9 he is a prop: "Kévin was up a ladder at it, in the rain." The book's best line at the end is his ("'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much.'") and it lands on a man I had met in three scenes.
*Do:* give him one scene of his own between chapters 6 and 10, talking down from that ladder, wanting something daft out loud. It widens the register and it pays the ending.

**3. The middle asks the same question eight times, and Larrère's last window scene is a recap. (9, 12)**
Maylis asks for the gate and is refused by Larrère (5), by the vote (9), by Hontaube (12), by Larrère again (12), by Jeannot on the platform, at the trapdoor and at the trapdoor again. The car-chaired meeting and the Hontaube call are new jokes on the pattern. The second window scene in chapter 12 is not: "'I have a letter in here in your handwriting. It's your resignation, with a gap where the date goes. If that gate moves tonight before I ring, the letter gets tomorrow's date. You know what's under the letter.'" He is telling her what chapters 6 and 9 showed us. He does not even say he will be brief. It is his dullest appearance, placed just before the night.
*Do:* cut the scene to its two new facts, the orange warning and "I saw your car", and end it on "The wipers crossed the windscreen." The hand on the folder says the rest.

**4. "Still in the box?" is planted late and explained on arrival. (5, 17)**
The café's greeting first appears in chapter 5 with its footnote attached: "It was what the café had said to anybody who came in late, ever since the June of the steel room." Its replacement is one of the hardest lines in the book: "'Late,' the dealer said to him, without looking up from the cards. 'Had the key and went for a walk, did you?'" That lands on a joke I had heard once, twelve chapters back, with a gloss.
*Do:* have somebody say it to Maylis in chapter 1, unexplained, and let her take it. Then Lesca's answer in 5 and "Nobody asked whether she was still in the box" in 6 are a second and third use, and 17 is the fourth.

**5. Chapter 16 explains its own callbacks. (16)**
"Marie-Jo looked at the sash. She had handed her own back across this table in a plastic bag." "Lesca had made the rule in her first month, for Larrère." "She said it to Lesca this time, not to the wall." Each payoff arrives with its set-up restated beside it. Chapter 17 trusts the reader with the oven door and says nothing; chapter 16 trusts nobody. A callback that is glossed is half a callback.
*Do:* strike the three glosses. Chapters 3, 6 and 15 have done the work.

**6. The coins are planted and paid fourteen lines apart, and Coralie pays for it. (7)**
"He said his mouth tasted of coins. He coughed, and it came out pink on the sheet." Then, the same page: "Her mouth tasted of coins. She got up by the leg of the table, coughed into the sink and looked. It was pink." The machinery shows. Worse, the speech that carries the plant is the only wholly humourless thing Coralie says in the book, and chapter 7 is the one chapter where I did not smile after its first page.
*Do:* move the man of eighty-one to chapter 1, as one of her work stories told with her fingers on the wrist, where it can have its edge ("to reach his glasses"). Then chapter 7 needs no speech and the symptoms explain themselves.

**7. "Either" is said to Inguma twice, the same way. (2, 9)**
Chapter 2: "'Either,' Maylis said. / At its usual minute Inguma came down off the wardrobe and landed on the floor." Chapter 9: "The clock radio went to 02:52. Inguma was still waiting. / 'Either,' Maylis said." The second is better (a demon kept waiting for its instructions) and the plot needs it. The first spends it early. Chapter 2 also already has its joke for that night: "But she had raked the square that afternoon, right down to the drain covers."
*Do:* end the chapter 2 attempt on the raked square. Keep "Either" out of the bedroom until chapter 9.

**8. The key and the letters hold up a man who has to be carried. (14)**
"There was nobody to carry him." Then Jeannot arrives chest-deep, and for a dozen lines the scene returns a key: "'I put that handle in letter thirty,' he said. / 'I wrote to them too.' / 'I know you did. They took note.'" It is the best dry exchange in the chapter, and it borrows time from a corridor that has been told "not to stop for anything. She said it twice."
*Do:* give him Hourcade first. Let him say "letter thirty" with the old man on his back, crossing the yard. It is funnier there and it costs the flood nothing.

**9. The square's reactions are a census. (15)**
After "and I drowned him", nine paragraphs hand one reaction to each named person: "Kévin was by the loudspeaker with the cable in his fist. Sarthou held the flag and kept his eyes to the front. / The priest looked at his shoes." The strokes that matter are the comic ones: the loudspeaker saying "drowned him" late, the deputy who "turned the sheet over to see whether there was more on the back", the man who claps, the child. The rest is attendance.
*Do:* keep the echo, the deputy, the sentence travelling "at walking pace", the clap, the child, Arlette walking to the stone and Larrère's hat. Cut the others.

**10. The premise is stated without a smile, and then shown. (3)**
"The other Maylis Lesca lived in a Peyrebarthe that had its flood in 2014. Afterwards it made her mayor. Call her Lesca. She had just woken up in Maylis's bed, in Maylis's body, beside Maylis's Coralie. At home, her own Coralie had slept in the spare room across the landing for eleven years." Five sentences of briefing, in the only place the narrator turns to face the reader. The next page then shows all of it better: one pillow against two, the coat on the door, "At home that alarm rang on the other side of the landing."
*Do:* keep "Call her Lesca." Cut the bed, the body and the landing from the paragraph and let the pillows do it.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie at the oven door**, all three times (1, 9, 17): the full impression, the one she watches Maylis laugh at, the one she cannot finish.
2. **The verb for the stone** (3) and what Maïté's verb turns out to be (16): "*Left*."
3. **Bingo night** (8), whole: the coffee over Josiane's shoes, "Tooth", "You're the water", "She's got a bar full of coffee", Madame Etcheto finishing the words over her duck, "How's that spelled?"
4. **"The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies"** (13) and **"Twelve. One, two. / Somebody pulled a chair in to a table."** (15)
5. **Jeannot's six points down the phone** (10), ending "And six. Bugger off.", and **letter No. 42** (17).
