# Critic's report: Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. About 42,300 words. Counts below are mine, taken from those eighteen files.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Then his death is mine as well, and I've stood beside his widow for eleven Decembers." (11) The stakes are exact once Coralie names them. Before chapter 7 the reader knows a date and not yet what Maylis stands to lose.
- **Tension: 7.** "The gate was six hundred metres behind her. The red reflector was four hundred metres in front. The bank had six minutes left." (14) Chapters 10 to 15 hold. In 4 to 9 the tension is a question of which rule applies tonight.
- **Progression: 6.** "Then Lesca told her about the night, in order." (4) The flood night of 2014 is told six times (0, 3, 4, 10, 11, 15) and the discovery that Lesca brings the water is delivered three times (7, 9, 9).
- **Comedy: 8.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station." (6)
- **Characters: 8.** "Because you'd have told me." (11)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Here,' he said. 'I know you are.'" (14), said over a wheelchair in a flooded corridor.
- **Prose: 7.** Best: "She heard the passage, then the front door, then the car door shutting on a pillow." (17) Worst: "Lesca had the jaw back now and nothing to put in it. Maylis took it again." (13)
- **The ending: 7.** "I know what you picked. I was the other one." (17) It costs enough. Too much of the last chapter is people summing themselves up.
- **Clarity: 6.** "Inguma had gone to count, so Lesca was still there. Maylis had kept her teeth shut all evening to stop that." (8) A sentence the reader needs, and should not need.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. From chapter 10 I did not stop. Chapters 5 to 9 I read on the strength of the dialogue.

**Press it on a friend?** On a friend who reads Penelope Fitzgerald or Magnus Mills, today. On the Crouch reader the brief describes, not yet: that reader stalls between chapters 5 and 9, where the sentences are busy saying who holds the jaw.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Prose: level with Crouch overall. Above him by the line, below him in ease.**

- **Above, the ear.** Crouch's people all talk alike and need tags. Here a line can be assigned with the tag covered: Jeannot numbers his grievances ("One. It's Jeannot. Two. Jean's for my gravestone."), Sarthou never contracts ("I am reporting a line"), Maïté cannot finish a sentence ("In the— It's blankets."), Larrère opens every evasion with "I'll be brief", Lesca moves items. The Good Place builds its people the same way, each wrong in a fixed verbal form, and this book matches it there.
- **Above, the noun.** Crouch reaches for the italic thought and the stacked fragment. This book reaches for an object and lets it carry the feeling: a shopping pad with boxes, a pillow, a kitchen timer, a white cross of tape. The narration is almost free of stock phrase across 42,000 words. There is no narrator's dash and no semicolon anywhere.
- **Below, ease.** Crouch states the rules of his box once, in one scene, in a physicist's mouth, and the prose goes transparent for three hundred pages. A reader never re-reads a Crouch sentence to find out who did what. Here the narration re-states the rules after every use and, in four chapters, tracks the custody of a mouth, a jaw, a left hand and a pair of legs clause by clause. The Good Place makes its rule-explaining the joke. This book does that once, superbly, in chapter 4 ("All those in favour." "There are two of us."), and everywhere else explains straight.
- **Below, variety.** The sentence is one shape. Narration averages 10 words a sentence; 35% of sentences are six words or fewer and 2.4% run past 25. One narration sentence in six begins "She". That shape is right for chapter 14. It is the same shape for a woman buying bread.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Chapter 13. Lesca's turn is argued in narration.** This is the moral hinge of one of the two leads, and it arrives as reasoning:

> "She had told Maylis there was no both. She had wanted both herself, the bed and Castagnet. But the motion she had written at her own kitchen table was for a seat and a bed. There was no man in it."

> "Twelve years ago she had let his phone ring once and then done what she had come to do. Holding this wheel was the same thing."

The paragraph before already showed it: a dead engine, a hand locked on the brake, 02:53, "the reflector was red, and no nearer than it had been twelve years ago". That image is the realisation. The two paragraphs after it tell the reader what the image meant, in the abstract nouns of a summing-up, and the turn stops being something she does. Cut both. Keep one sentence the reader cannot supply ("As long as she held the wheel, nobody was going down that track at all.") and go straight to "I'm letting go." Same chapter, same habit: "Inguma hadn't taken her home. She had held the wheel against it for a whole ring, and this was where that put her." The hall says so.

**2. Chapters 5, 8, 9, 13. The shared body is written as a custody log.** "The left hand" appears 28 times, "the mouth" 18, "the jaw" 9. Because "she" is ambiguous, the name carries every clause: "Lesca" 36 times in chapter 5's 2,700 words.

> "Lesca unlocked the box with the key from the belt, fitted the handle and began to turn it with the left hand. The chain came up wet. Lesca counted the clicks of the catch, the steel tooth that stopped the chain running back." (5)

> "Lesca had an answer for her. Lesca pulled at the jaw and Maylis held it, so all Josiane got was a noise through the nose." (8)

In the bingo scene every custody sentence stands between a set-up and its punchline. The scene is funny for what the hall sees and mishears, and the narration keeps going back inside the skull. This needs a decision, not line edits. The book already owns two signals that need no narrating: the right hand is always Maylis's, and the voice is low when it is Lesca's and higher when it is Maylis's. Attribute speech by pitch and drop the jaw and the teeth. Give Lesca's actions in Maylis's chapters as things done to "her" ("Lesca walked her down" is already the best version). Show the struggle only where an onlooker would see it: the coffee running over, the hand over the mouth, the handbrake. The convention also slips: "Maylis clapped her right hand over her own mouth" (9) against "the mouth" everywhere else.

**3. Chapters 7 and 9. The book's central discovery is delivered three times, twice as recap.** Chapter 7 gives it in a narrator's summary before the line that should carry it:

> "On Wednesday and Thursday night Maylis had pushed Inguma off until 4. She had never got as far as the chair, and the chair was Lesca's only way across. The two days that followed, Thursday and Friday, had been the only dry ones."

Then "The rain came when Lesca came." Then, at the chapter's end, a third pass: "But he would only need saving if Lesca was here on Friday night." Chapter 9 has Maylis lay it out again for Coralie under three column headings, and forty lines later Lesca lays it out again in the steel room ("It comes with me. Keeping me out for a night or two changes nothing, because the river catches up the day I'm back. One night matters."). Bordenave's cross on the pad and the jam jars in his cellar already prove it in the scene. Let Maylis see the jars, say nothing, and let the three-column chart in chapter 9 be the one place it is spelled out, since there it costs Lesca Coralie. Cut the chapter 7 recap paragraph and the closing paragraph of 7. Cut Lesca's version in 9 to "It comes with me."

**4. Chapter 3. The narrator changes voice, then writes a file.** The chapter opens with a synopsis and an address to the reader that occurs nowhere else in the book:

> "The other Maylis Lesca lived in a Peyrebarthe that had its flood in 2014. Afterwards it made her mayor. Call her Lesca."

Chapter 2 ended on "her mouth said. Maylis hadn't said it." The reader is leaning forward, and is handed the answer in three flat sentences. Open inside the body, nameless, as the prologue does. Let one pillow, a dry roof and "Give me that wrist, Madame le maire" do the telling, and let the narration take up "Lesca" from there. The chapter is also the book's heaviest in backfill: "had" runs at 27 per thousand words against 6 in chapter 12. "She had been the keyholder that winter. Every morning she had gone down to the old gate, where her father had painted a red line on the pillar…" The entries from the flood file, set out in italics, do this work without the paragraph that introduces them.

**5. Chapters 12 and 14. Paragraphs written to answer an objection.**

> "She had tried the café first, but the nine who had signed on Tuesday had watched Jeannot's hand go up. A vote was a vote." (12)

> "Kévin could turn the handle while she went back down for Jeannot. She wouldn't ask him to. If that gate moved, Larrère would send the sheet with Kévin's name on it. Kévin had said he would do the six months. She wasn't going to have him turn the handle that sent him there." (12)

> "Jeannot had told her not to bring the van down, and at dusk she had waded through the dip herself. She took the van anyway. In the van she could still have both, Jeannot and the gate." (14)

Each one is the narrator stepping in front of a reader who has asked "why doesn't she just". The Hontaube phone call on the same page is how to do it: the refusal is a scene, the refusal is funny, and nobody has to explain it. Give the café its refusal in two lines of dialogue. For Kévin, he has just said "Don't keep a gate shut for me"; one line from her ("He's mine") is already there and is enough. Cut the five sentences.

**6. Chapters 17 and 11. People who could not say "duck" now deliver verdicts on themselves.** Maylis explains the same act twice, with two different motives, to two people:

> "I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me." (17, to Kévin)

> "At the gate I thought I could do better than you. I drove past the handle to be the one who did." (17, to Lesca)

Chapter 14 said it once, at the moment it was true: "She wanted it not to be her." Add Lesca's "It was my own night I was putting right", Maylis's "The arm is the excuse you've written for me. It isn't mine", Coralie's "That part is mine. … I'm not telling you this so that you'll carry less of yours", and "You had the key" four times, and the last chapter reads as a hearing. The steel-room scene needs "Forty-one", "She took the pillow", "Don't come again", "I won't", and "It came out whole." Everything else in it is commentary. In 11, Coralie's speech earns its length up to "I've never wanted to hear which one it was." The next sentence, "I wanted you in the house, and I thought I could have you without it, if I kept a landing between us", states what the landing has shown for ten chapters.

**7. Throughout. One sentence shape, one entrance, one beat.** "Then" appears 120 times, 40 of them as "Then she". "It was" 78. "She had" opens 67 sentences. A character enters as a tableau of garment plus object: "in her uniform / dressing gown / coat / rubber boots" 30 times, "in his/her hand" 18.

> "Coralie stood in her work uniform with the coffee pot in her hand." (4)
> "Coralie was standing in the kitchen with the shopping pad in her hand." (1)
> "Coralie stopped at the bedroom door in her uniform, with her keys in her hand." (9)
> "Coralie was standing in the bedroom doorway with the speech in her hand" (11)

About 300 trailing "with his / her / the / a …" phrases, seven per thousand words. The default pause is a look: "looked" 94 times, "looked at" 53, "stopped" 69, "turned" 98. Blocks are also reused word for word where no echo is intended: the hall is inventoried three times (waxed floor, strip lights, three rows, paper tablecloths, dish of corn; "each one whole and then in parts" five times), Inguma "pressed down just as hard" three times, "Something heavy landed on the roof of the van. The metal bent in" twice within two pages of reading. Keep the plainness. Halve the "Then"s, vary how Coralie comes into a room, and let the sentence run when a body is moving through space, as it does in "Sarthou himself was in the street in his cape, up to his waist in water, coming up from the lower houses." The echoes that are meant (prologue against 14, the reflector, three breaths) will read as echoes once the accidental ones are gone.

**8. Throughout, worst in 0 to 3. Credentials and durations do the work of feeling.** The narrator licenses each perception with a qualification: ", so" 46 times.

> "She had repaired that bank every spring for six years, so she knew what it could take." (0)
> "Maylis dug the graves in Peyrebarthe, so she knew what a name and a date on a stone meant." (1)
> "I've held hands for a living since I was nineteen, so I know when a hand is holding on" (4)

Nobody needs a trade to know what a name and a date on a stone mean. And weight is supplied by a number of years: "years" 41 times, "twelve years" 15, "eleven years" 10, "never" 40 ("Nobody in Peyrebarthe had ever heard Maylis's mouth forbid anything", "Maylis had never smiled at Jeannot Castagnet in her life"). The count works when a character uses it as a weapon ("I've had sixteen and a shopping pad"). In narration it has stopped registering by chapter 6. Cut the "so she knew" clauses outright and keep one duration per scene.

**9. Chapters 1, 2, 3, 5. Definitions by apposition, including in the mouths of people who know.**

> "Boxes like that were for glass eels, the baby eels that came up the river on the winter tides." (2)
> "the catch, the steel tooth that stopped the chain running back" (5)
> "a motion, the kind the council votes on" (3)
> "The prefecture at Pau, the State's office over all these villages, sends you a yellow warning." (5, Lesca to Larrère, the mayor it is sent to)

"The kind" does this five times. The reader does need these things explained. A gloss bolted on after a comma is the narrator turning to the audience; in dialogue it is one character telling another what both know. Explain by use: the price of a kilo and the prison sentence already define a glass eel; the catch defines itself when it slips.

**10. Usage and consistency.**

- **Mixed English.** British throughout ("windscreen", "pavement", "handbrake", "rubbish bin", "mum", "dressing gown") except "truck" (11, 14), "baby carriage" (17, twice) and "dish towel" (six times).
- **Time.** "a.m." appears twice in the book (the heading of 1, and "It was 4 a.m." in 14) and nowhere else. In chapter 12 a bare "5" means five in the afternoon in the narration and five in the morning in Jeannot's next line ("You rang me at 5").
- **Dates in mouths.** "'I saw your car on 1 December,' Maylis said." (12) Nobody says that; it is Lesca's file voice in Maylis.
- **One word, several things.** "Handle" (68 uses) is the gate's until chapter 14, where it is also a wheelchair's, and 17, a baby carriage's. "The chair" (43) is the steel chair, the presiding chair and the wheelchairs. "The track" is the road and "wheel tracks" are its ruts, in the same sentences ("He knew every wheel track").
- **Several words, one thing.** The river is read against "her father's red line" on "the pillar of the old gate" and then against "the marker post" (7, 9, 12, 14). The steel room is also "the box". "The terrace road" (17) is new for the road along the high ground. "Cameras on the gates" (6) when the book has one floodgate.
- **"Lesca had had forty houses to open the gate for. She had also had Coralie's car in that yard."** (10)
- **Two charts in one scene** (9): the pulse chart on top of the pad and the three columns, then "Is that chart right?" and "tore the chart off the pad".
- **Order.** Chapter 16 ends on the Tuesday after the ceremony and 17 opens on the Sunday before it, unsignalled; its steel-room scene falls two days before the vote the reader has just watched.
- **A query against the book's own rule.** On Wednesday 2 December both women are in Lesca's village, yet "At home it was raining" (6). By the rule chapter 7 states, that day should be dry.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **How each person talks.** Jeannot's numbered points, Sarthou's uncontracted statements, Maïté's broken sentences, Larrère's "I'll be brief" and its payoff at the garden gate in 16: "'I'll be brief,' he said. … He said nothing."
2. **Jeannot on the phone at 5 in the morning (10)**, all six points, and the line after: "Jeannot had just sworn at her in six points, and he was alive."
3. **Chapter 14 from the yard to "Forty-one".** The corridor ("They were quiet. They were looking at the headlights."), the man and his bins, Kévin counting five fingers at 4, room twelve ("There was a hatbox on it. Nothing else.").
4. **Chapter 15 after she looks up from the sheet.** The deputy turning the page over "to see whether there was more on the back", the sentence going down the road "row by row, at walking pace", the medal handed back across the tape, "Somebody has to stack them."
5. **The pad and the pillow.** *Either* written across the boxes, "Eggs is what Either gets in this house", "I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it", and the car door shutting on a pillow. The marriage is told in those two objects and nothing should be added to them.
