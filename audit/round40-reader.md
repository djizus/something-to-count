# Round 40: Reader

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else opened but the brief.

Overall against the bar: **7.5**. Level with the comparisons on people, comedy and the last third. Below them on how clearly the machine works and on how hard the middle pulls.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** 'I'd have had you. You answer. But what I'd have had is a river over forty-two beds.' (9) By then I knew what could be lost, who by, and on which night.
- **Tension: 7.** 'The last line on the chart said 36.' (6) That is the one place before chapter 12 where I was afraid in the moment. Elsewhere the fear is by appointment, for the 11th.
- **Progression: 6.5.** 'The rain came when Lesca came.' (7) The book's best turn, and it changes everything after it. But chapters 9 and 12 each carry six or seven scenes, and the two women change places often enough that at the third steel-room meeting in chapter 9 I thought: again.
- **Comedy: 8.** 'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.' (6) I smiled from the registered letter to the oven door and did not stop until the bank broke.
- **Characters: 8.5.** 'I know what you picked. I was the other one.' (17)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** '"Here," he said. "I know you are."' (14) The running joke goes into the flood and comes out as grief without a word changed.
- **Prose: 7.** 'She heard the passage, then the front door, then the car door shutting on a pillow.' (17) Easy to read everywhere. Marked down for the gate drill, which I read four times: 'She leaned on it until the chain was tight and the catch dropped into its first tooth' is in chapter 12 twice.
- **The ending: 8.** 'He went and got it. Then he stepped over the string in his rubber boots and began to dig.' (17)
- **Clarity: 6.** 'One night matters. If I'm in your village at 2:51 on Friday night, your bank goes, as mine was going. If I'm not, it holds.' (9) Nobody in the book could know this. I was told it, and the climax stands on it.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. From chapter 10 I did not put it down.

**Would I press it on a friend?** Yes, with one sentence of warning: get to the end of chapter 2.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch on pull through the middle, level with him from chapter 12, above him on people.**

- Crouch has his premise in play inside twenty pages. So does this: the last line of chapter 2, 'Come back across. I've left you on that landing eleven years', arrives at about 4,400 words. That is as fast as Dark Matter and I had no complaint.
- A Crouch chapter ends by taking something away and the next opens with the hero already moving. The ends here are as good (2, 3, 5, 6, 9, 12, 14). The openings are not. Chapter 6 opens on a resignation letter, 11 on a mother who won't sit down, 12 on a phone call about a form. Three times the book let me breathe when I did not want to.
- Crouch gives his machine one explanation and never changes it. This machine comes in instalments: three points and a fourth 'I didn't have on my list' in chapter 4, the rain in 7, the one night in 9, the hall in 13. By chapter 9 I could not have recited the rules.
- I remember nobody from Recursion by name. I will remember Jeannot Castagnet, Coralie Mounet and Guy Larrère.

**Level with The Good Place on people wrong in their own way and on the reversal. Below it on making the rules a pleasure.**

- Each of them is wrong in a way nobody else is: Larrère hides behind a car window and a procedure, Jeannot numbers his grievances, Josiane always gets there first with the wrong name, Lesca can only want something as a motion, Maylis says Either, Coralie never asks. That is the show's method and it is done as well here.
- 'You're the water' re-reads every chapter from 3 to 7: the woman who came to stop the flood is the flood. That is a turn of the show's kind. It is weaker than it should be because the proof is a paragraph of dates (see fault 2).
- The show makes each new rule a joke told by Michael. Lesca chairing a meeting in a room with one chair is the same idea and it works in chapter 4. After that the rules arrive as statements.
- The trolley problem at the dip is played straight and it is better for it. 'She wanted it not to be her' is the most honest sentence in the book.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The plan for Friday night does not make sense, and the rule it answers is given by decree. (9, 12)**

'I can't push, and I can't give it its work. So on Friday I won't lie down. I'll sit up in the van, awake, until 4, when it gives up.' And to Kévin: 'The mayor can't get in if I don't sleep.'

The same chapter has just shown the opposite: 'She lay on her back and stayed awake. At 2:51 it came down off the wardrobe.' It has sat on her standing at the stair post, sitting at the council table and sitting at her own kitchen table. So I knew the plan could not work before she made it, and nobody in the book says so. When it lands on the van roof she is not surprised either: 'It was an hour and nine minutes short of 4.' So the real plan was to push for sixty-nine minutes on a rib she has been told will go through her lung, having said she can't push. I spent the vigil unsure what she was trying to do.

The rule itself, 'One night matters', is said by Lesca standing up, so the book vouches for it, but nothing shows it. Then the night blurs it: Lesca is in for one minute, is put out at 2:53, and the bank goes anyway. 'It had come too late' is all I am given.

What I would do: say the plan as it is. It comes at 2:51 whether she sleeps or not. Sitting up, awake, she means to push until 4, and she knows what the last night of pushing did in the sink. That makes the vigil a countdown to something I can dread, and it removes a sentence that is false by the book's own rules. For the one-night rule, give Lesca a reason I can check: her own bank began to go at 2:51 and her file says so, or the river on Maylis's side has tracked her file only on the mornings after she crossed.

**2. The turn of the whole book rests on weather I was not shown. (7)**

'The two days that followed, Thursday and Friday, had been the only dry ones. Last night she had lain still and Lesca had come across. By noon it was raining again.'

This is the moment the rescuer becomes the flood. I had to stop and rebuild the week to follow it. The only dry day on the page is Bordenave's 'the road's dry', said as a point against her. Wednesday and Thursday night are two paragraphs of summary. So the discovery is a deduction from a log I had not been keeping.

What I would do: one short scene in dry daylight on Thursday. A dry coat on the hook. Maylis working in the square without a hood, noticing nothing. Then when the rain comes back at noon on Saturday I get there a line before she does.

**3. I cannot tell what killed Madame Etcheto, or what let Lesca out of the hall. (13, 15, 16)**

'The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies. She has a ceremony at 11, and she will not be taking this seat.' Then: 'Twelve. One, two. Somebody pulled a chair in to a table.' Then: '"Then it wasn't Inguma that came for her. It was you." "Yes."'

The rule since chapter 2 is that the one it cannot take home dies in her own bed. Lesca refuses to be taken, reaches the hall, declines her seat in the voice she uses for the prefecture, and is taken home alive. I laughed, and then I did not know why she had been spared. Two readings are open. Either she lived because she had already let go of the wheel, or the seat she refused had to be filled and room twelve filled it. The second is terrible and much the better book. The text will not say which.

What I would do: choose, and let Lesca say it to the old woman in chapter 16 in one plain sentence. If the seat was filled for her, 'I took my river over there with me' is the lesser half of what she did.

**4. Chapter 12 shuts every door in turn, and one of them does not hold. (12, 14)**

'Kévin could turn the handle while she went back down for Jeannot. She wouldn't ask him to.'

In one chapter the café won't come, Hontaube can't come, the care home can't move on orange, Jeannot's phone stays in his van, the track won't take a van, and Kévin isn't asked. By the fourth I could see them being shut. Five hold. Kévin's does not: that same afternoon he said 'I'd do them, Maylis. I want you to know I would. Don't keep a gate shut for me.' With forty-two beds under the bank, sparing him six months reads as the story needing her alone. At 2:55, when she rings Coralie, Kévin is two hundred metres from the handle and she does not think of him.

What I would do: make it her failure and not a kindness. She does not ask because asking is the thing she cannot do, and chapter 17 charges her for it. Kévin on the van step is the place: 'You could have asked me.' And spread the shut doors. The Hontaube call is funny enough to sit in chapter 9.

**5. I could not draw the place. (00, 1, 12, 14)**

'From up there the river was on one side of her and the fields were on the other, far below, dark and empty.' And: 'the forty houses that stood on the low ground behind the river bank'.

The houses and the fields are both on the land side of one bank. The gate puts the river on the fields. I never learned why that water does not reach the houses, or which way along the bank each lies. The choice at the dip is a choice between two directions, and I felt it as two numbers.

What I would do: one sentence in the opening, while she stands on the bank and looks both ways. Houses to one hand, fields to the other, and what lies between them.

**6. The first half of chapter 11 stalls the book at its tightest point. (11)**

'A point of order. You have the flowers as one item and the kiss as the next. They're one item.'

Chapter 10 ends with the plan changed and Friday begun. Chapter 11 opens in the other village with a visitors' book, a roll of tape and a young man from Pau. It is 1,100 words before Coralie picks up the speech. Darrigrand's swept step and the eight strips are worth having. The rest I read with my eye on the next section break.

What I would do: cut Arlette's scene to her one instruction, cut the kiss and the medal from the rehearsal (both come back in 15 and are clear there), and reach the bedroom doorway in half the length.

**7. Chapter 9 is three chapters, and it repeats a scene from chapter 2. (9)**

'"Either," Maylis said.'

The kitchen chart, a steel-room meeting, the yellow warning, the petition, the vote from the car, the oven, the pad, the words to Inguma, a second steel-room meeting and the hall of the dead. Any two would make a chapter. And the scene where she is asked what Inguma should count and says Either is the same scene, staged the same way, as the end of the third section of chapter 2. The second time it matters. The first time spent it.

What I would do: cut Either from chapter 2 so that she fails Inguma with that word once, when it costs her. Break chapter 9 after the vote.

**8. Forty-one and forty-two each mean two things. (1, 5, 10, 14, 17)**

'I've paid for forty-one registered letters.' 'Forty-two people in their beds.' 'Put it in forty-two.' '"Forty-one," Coralie said.'

The last word of chapter 14 is the hardest hit in the book. When Maylis says it again in the steel room it should mean one empty chair and nothing else. Both times I also heard Jeannot's letters. Nineteen has the same trouble: turns of the handle, days of rain, sandbags, Coralie's age on the Bayonne floor.

What I would do: move the letters away. Thirty-one and thirty-two cost nothing. Give the sandbags another number.

**9. The first hard paragraph comes at 1,400 words, before I have a picture. (1)**

'Two. That gate lets water through both ways. On Monday the river was lower than my fields. You put the gate up, and my water ran out through it, down into the river. Last night I sat over mud.'

This is where I first had to read twice. I did not yet know that numbered points are how this man talks, or where his fields are, or why a duck hunter wants water. A reader trying the first chapter in a shop stops here.

What I would do: fix fault 5 first, so the fields are already placed. Then let him have one point about the water and put the weight on 'Three', which is the joke.

**10. Small things a cold reader trips on. (2, 5, 17)**

'You won't get that down two metres with one arm, Lesca.'

For fifteen chapters Lesca has been the other woman. In the last spoken line of the book Jeannot drops 'little' and I thought for half a second he was speaking to the mayor. If the echo is meant, Maylis must hear it. If it is not, he says Maylis.

Also: Coralie's 'When has it ever sat on me?' closes a scene in chapter 2 and is never answered. And 'there's a lake where the village field was' in chapter 5 means nothing until chapter 11.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 14 from the dip to 'Forty-one'.** The chairs waiting in the dark while the van is away, the man who asks whether it is Thursday, Coralie counting the chairs before she speaks, and Kévin's phone buzzing at 4.
2. **Jeannot.** The six points down the phone ending 'Bugger off', 'Jean's for my gravestone', letter forty-two that 'doesn't thank you', and the second shovel.
3. **The shopping pad and the oven door.** Two boxes ticked, 'I don't cook Either', and the third time at the oven, when she gets as far as 'The—' and shuts it.
4. **Bingo night.** 'You're the water', 'She wants water', 'How's that spelled?', and the old woman finishing the words out of kindness and undoing the whole evening.
5. **The secret leaving Lesca, both times.** 'Because you'd have told me' in the bedroom doorway, and the sentence walking back through the square to the man who claps.
