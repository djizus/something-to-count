# Critic's report: Thriller

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

Verdict against the bar: 7. A Crouch reader finishes it and does not find it as good as Crouch at what Crouch does. It is at the bar on fear for the marriage and on the design of its two reversals. It is under on rule economy, on speed in chapter 1 and chapters 9 to 11, and on collecting the stakes it announces.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** Dated, personal and counted, but three of them are announced and never collected. Coralie spends a paragraph on the punctured lung ("He was dead before the doctor had his coat off"), Maylis ticks off coins, pink and half a breath, hears her side "go" at the trapdoor, and on Monday she is cutting turf for a grave.
- **Tension: 7.** Four sequences are at Crouch's level: "Maylis pulled. The wrist was all she had left to pull with." Between them, chapters 9 and 11 let the clock stand while a vote is taken from a car and a stage is taped.
- **Progression: 7.** Every attempt to stop the flood locks it in, which is the right engine: "If she can be wrong about Friday the 4th, she can be wrong about Friday the 11th." But Maylis's thirteen hours in the other village (chapter 6) move the flood plot nowhere.
- **Comedy: 8.** "'Is this a request, or a notification?'" The joke is also the obstacle, with the river at the boards.
- **Characters: 8.** "'I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it.'"
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much.'" One bad switch: Lesca leaves the hall of the dead on a joke ("apologies received").
- **Prose: 7.** Plain and exact, and it then explains the turn it has just shown: "Lesca had walked her down the track with a watch because she wanted to save Jeannot. But he would only need saving if Lesca was here on Friday night."
- **The ending: 8.** It does not console. "It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you." The car door "shutting on a pillow" is the price, and it is paid.
- **Clarity: 6.** Nine working rules by chapter 9, and the one the climax hangs on is a sentence the antagonist says: "If I'm in your village at 2:51 on Friday night, your bank goes, as mine was going. If I'm not, it holds."

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. From chapter 12 I could not have stopped.

Would I press it on a friend? On the friend who loves The Good Place, yes. On the friend who reads Crouch for speed, not until the first chapter and chapters 9 to 11 are shorter.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch as a thriller.** Three things put it there.

1. **Rules.** Crouch gives one rule, has a scientist explain it in a page, and spends the book on consequences. Here the rules arrive whenever the plot needs one: the count, staying seated, the owner's right hand, shared memory in the chair, the truth rule, the host weakening, the body left cold, the rain, the minute of 2:51, the seat among the dead. Each is clear when stated. Together they leave the reader checking the rulebook during the climax when he should be afraid.
2. **Speed.** In Dark Matter the other self is on the page inside the first chapter. Here the second woman speaks at the last line of chapter 2. Chapter 1 spends its middle on a registered letter.
3. **Paid stakes.** Crouch collects. Here the lung, the seat among the dead and Kévin's prison sentence are each waved away in a line.

**Level with Crouch on the marriage, and past him at one point.** In Dark Matter the wife never knowingly prefers the impostor to her husband's face. Coralie does ("I'd have this one again"), with the husband listening in the right wrist, and then refuses on a principle from her own trade: "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." That is the most frightening scene in the book and there is no water in it.

**Level with The Good Place on design, below on staging.** The book runs the trolley problem down both tracks: one woman pulled the lever and hid it, the other would not pull and lost a life anyway. Its two reversals re-read what came before: the rescuer is the flood, and her act of repair ("Go to the hut first") is what sends Maylis past the handle. The Good Place stages its reversal as a scene with a face in it. This book works its first one out from a rain calendar.

**The lens questions, answered.**

- *Premise in play fast enough?* No. End of chapter 2. It needs to be end of chapter 1.
- *Chapter ends?* Fourteen of seventeen pull. Chapters 10 and 16 end soft. Chapter 5's hook ("Are you coming up?") is let down in the first three lines of chapter 6, on purpose, and it works.
- *Where is the reader afraid, and of what?* Of losing Coralie to the better version (5, 8). Of the wrist chart falling from 64 to 34 (6, 7). Of the bank (12). For Coralie behind the fire door (14). Never of Larrère.
- *Where would a Crouch reader put it down?* At the postman in chapter 1. At the association vote in chapter 9. At the roll of tape in chapter 11.
- *Does the central choice land?* The one at the dip lands: "She wanted it not to be her." The one that decides the night, at 5 p.m. on the platform, goes by in ten lines.
- *Does the ending cost enough?* For Maylis, yes: the job, the key, the village's face, Coralie. For the book, one notch short: the one death is a woman of ninety-four who walked back to the room herself, and Coralie claims half of it.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Friday has two exits standing open, and the book walks past both. (12, set up in 9)**
"'Either,' Maylis said. / 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. 'That's how the vote left it.' / He was off the platform and down the steps before she could take it back."
At 5 p.m. it is daylight, Jeannot is above ground an arm's length away, the handle is fitted, and Kévin has just told her not to keep the gate shut for him. This is where the night is decided, and Madame Darrigrand says so in chapter 17. It gets ten lines and no reason for lifting the handle off once he has gone. The second exit is worse. In chapter 6 a count of roadsides kept Inguma away for thirteen hours, and Lesca gives the lake twice, so a count can be reused. On Friday Maylis never says the words. Her whole case is one failed try on Tuesday and "I can't give it its work." Her plan instead is "I'll sit up in the van, awake, until 4", when the reader has watched it sit on her standing ("onto Maylis's chest, where she stood"), seated at a table, and awake in chapter 1's first line. So the four hours in the van are suspense over a plan the reader knows is void, beside a remedy nobody tries.
*Do:* close both on the page. At 5 p.m. let her turn it, count the clicks, and stop with the gate a hand up, so that letting it down is an act the reader watches. At 11 p.m. let her say the words in the van with the handle fitted, and fail there, in front of us, on the thing she will not name. Then staying awake is what is left, not what she chose first.

**2. The reversal is deduced, and the rule that follows is asserted. (7, 9)**
"The rain came when Lesca came."
The book's turn reaches the reader as a paragraph of day-reckoning ("The two days that followed, Thursday and Friday, had been the only dry ones") resting on two dry days in December. Two chapters later the mechanism of the climax is handed over by Lesca as a thing she now knows, with no source.
*Do:* stage it. Put Maylis on the bank when Lesca goes out of her and let her see the river do something a river does not do. Let the 2:51 rule be found by both of them in the truth room, by one of them failing to say its opposite. The room exists to do exactly that work.

**3. Three stakes are announced at length and never collected. (7, 13, 17)**
"I've worked out what I'd be giving up, and I've written it down. Nil."
Lesca then holds the wheel, lands in the hall, sits, and leaves by saying "The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies." The rule set up in chapters 2, 6 and 9 is waived because she declines. The lung is waived by a bandage ("Then you'll dig it. With that side. I'll strap it first"). Kévin's prison is waived in six words: "There is no other sheet."
*Do:* collect one of them properly. The cheapest is the lung: Maylis does not dig; she is in the bed Coralie drove her to, and Jeannot digs alone or with Kévin. The strongest is the seat: make it plain that when Lesca refused it, somebody else was called to it, and let her know that before she stands at the microphone. Chapter 16 says this to Madame Etcheto after the fact, and too softly.

**4. Maylis spends a day in the village that had the flood and learns nothing about the flood. (6)**
"She wanted one day as the woman who signed things."
That motive is thin against a known risk of death, and the day pays only in comedy and in Coralie's chart. The price of an opened gate (the lake, the four houses, the swept step, eight left of forty-two) is shown in chapter 11, to Lesca, who knows it already. Maylis chooses at the dip without ever having seen what the other choice bought.
*Do:* send her down to the Port on that side. Let her meet Darrigrand's broom and read the file's last typed line herself. Then "both" is said by a woman who has seen each arm.

**5. The flood night is given as a download the day before the climax. (10)**
"Lesca wasn't in it yet, but the night of the flood came to Maylis anyway."
The reader has had its outline since the opening pages and chapter 3. What is new is the kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice, Coralie's car in the yard, and the reflector that shines "for four more minutes". Those three are worth the chapter. The turning of the van and the handle are a second telling.
*Do:* start at the headlights on the reflector. Cut what the opening already did.

**6. The clock stands still from chapter 9 to chapter 11.**
"He read it to her off his sheet, from the anthem to the photograph."
Chapter 9 has seven scenes and two visits to the chair. Chapter 11 opens the eve of the flood with a point of order about a kiss. The Coralie scene that follows is one of the best in the book and the reader reaches it tired.
*Do:* end chapter 9 at "Four to three. It stays shut." Move the taping of the square to chapter 3's committee morning, where the stone is already the subject. Chapter 11 then runs Arlette, short, and the phone.

**7. Larrère never raises the temperature, and Maylis's lever on him moves nothing. (6, 9, 12)**
"The wipers crossed the windscreen."
That is the whole effect of her saying she saw his car behind the Castagnet house. At the vote she is silenced by a name on a sheet while she holds a fact that would turn Jeannot round on his stool, and the page does not show her weighing it.
*Do:* one beat at the vote. She has it in her mouth, looks at Jeannot's back, thinks of Maïté's "Not there", and swallows it. Her silence becomes a choice with a cost, and Larrère's win becomes something she gave him.

**8. Chapter 1 takes too long to reach the second woman. (1)**
"'I've got a registered letter for the mayor,' the postman said into the gap."
The scene plants the window, the letters and "Either", and it has to stay. But the chapter also carries two flashbacks (June 2014, August 2010) and Jeannot's three points on which way water runs through a gate.
*Do:* cut a third of the square. Move the festival flashback to chapter 4, where "you've told me once what you wanted" is being tested.

**9. The narrator explains the turn after the scene has made it. (7, 13, 14)**
"Twelve years ago she had let his phone ring once and then done what she had come to do. Holding this wheel was the same thing."
Also: "She had put Lesca out, and it had come too late: the river was through the bank already." In a book this plain, each such sentence lets the pressure out at the moment it should hold.
*Do:* cut them. "I'm letting go" after the dashboard clock and the red reflector "no nearer than it had been twelve years ago" needs no gloss.

**10. The countdown is planted and dropped. (1, 3)**
"She opened her pocket knife and cut eighteen marks down the rake, starting level with her shoulder."
The rake is never seen again. The day count stops at "thirteen days away" in chapter 3. Only chapter 1 carries a date line, and from chapter 4 the reader works out the day from the soup.
*Do:* head every chapter with day, date and, from chapter 12, the hour. Bring the rake back once, with its last notch, on Friday.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 14 from the glass doors to "Forty-one."** Maylis made to push other people's chairs while Coralie is behind the fire door; "Here." / "I know you are."; Kévin counting five; the empty chair.
2. **Two fingers on the wrist**, and above all the landing in chapter 8: "That's her. In this hand."
3. **The cross beside Friday the 4th.** Her resistance makes the prophecy wrong, and the wrong prophecy costs her Jeannot's vote. The plot does not turn a better screw anywhere.
4. **Coralie on the edge of the bed in chapter 11:** "2:51. I've known that for twelve years." And "Don't tell me now."
5. **The confession travelling back through the square "row by row, at walking pace"**, the child who asks what the lady did, and the young man from Pau asking for the medal.
