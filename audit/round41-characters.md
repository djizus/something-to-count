# Critic's report: Characters

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** A marriage, one named man, one named old woman and a corridor of people in blankets, all of whom I could tell apart. "'Thirty-nine,' Coralie said. 'Forty.'" I was counting with her.
- **Tension: 7.** Chapters 12 to 14 are at Crouch's level. Before them the fear is for a kitchen table, which is real but slow. "The phone went on ringing in a hand she couldn't close."
- **Progression: 6.** The book moves by returning to the same scene with the water a little higher. "'I asked where you'd be,' Coralie said. She went up to the spare room." That is the seventh time she has asked and left the room.
- **Comedy: 7.** I smiled most of the way and the people are funny in themselves. "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'"
- **Characters: 7.** Coralie, Jeannot, Madame Etcheto and Kévin are better than anything in Crouch. Of the two leads, one is kept at arm's length for the whole of her time in the other's life. "Lesca didn't know there was another half."
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** The best switch in the book is a joke that fails: "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'"
- **Prose: 7.** Plain, exact, built from work. "It hit the floor like a full sack dropped off a trailer." It loses a point for "the mouth", "the legs", "the left hand" across chapters 5, 7 and 8, which turn two people into luggage for pages at a time.
- **The ending: 8.** It costs her the wife, the job, the village's good opinion and a woman of ninety-four, and the last line takes one word off her name: "'You won't get that down two metres with one arm, Lesca.'"
- **Clarity: 6.** Who is driving, what the count buys and who owns which hand have to be held in the head together. "Inguma had gone to count, so Lesca was still there. Maylis had kept her teeth shut all evening to stop that." I had to stop and work that out.

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. Would I press it on a friend? Yes, on the friend who loved The Good Place. Not yet on the one who reads Crouch for the other self.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Above Crouch.** His people are a profession and a want. Daniela in Dark Matter is someone to get back to, and I cannot quote a thing she says. Coralie has a shopping pad, an impression of the mayor done through an oven door, and a reason of her own to leave. Seven villagers are recognisable from one line of speech each: Jeannot numbers his grievances, Sarthou talks like a report, Kévin ends on a question, Maïté cannot finish a sentence, Larrère cannot start one without "I'll be brief". Crouch has no bench like that.

**Below Crouch on one thing, and it is his thing.** In Dark Matter the reader is inside the house with the wrong Jason, on the wife's side of the bed, and that is where the fear for the marriage comes from. Here the wrong Maylis sits down to soup in the right kitchen and the reader watches from inside the passenger. The other self is good company. She is never wanted and never feared.

**Just below The Good Place.** The machinery is the same and it is used well. Everyone is wrong in their own way: Maylis will not choose, Lesca makes the sum and then repeats it for eleven years, Coralie did not ask because she would have been told, Jeannot would sooner be right than dry, Josiane files everything under adultery and is one name out. The centre is the trolley problem with a real lever on a real bank, and there is a room where a lie will not come out of the mouth. What the series does and the book does not: it plays every pairing, and it makes you love the wrong person from inside her wrongness. The book skips one pairing outright (fault 2) and keeps Lesca outside her own wanting (fault 1).

**Net: 7 against a bar of 8.** Both gaps are closable with scenes the book already owns the material for.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Lesca is never inside her own wanting while she has it. Chapters 5, 7, 8, 9. Structural.**

> "Maylis got that laugh three or four times a year. She had always laughed with Coralie. Lesca didn't know there was another half." (5)

> "'I've had one bowl of soup at your table.'" (9)

Lesca is the one who acts: she crosses, takes the body, climbs the stairs, brings the river. The reader is inside her only at home (3, 11, 15, 16), where she wants nothing she can have. Every hour she spends in the house with two pillows is told from Maylis's seat, so she is a left hand, a low voice and a pair of eyes that go up the stairs. I was told about the soup. I never tasted it with her. The result is a triangle with two sides: I never once wanted her to get the bed, so the stairs in chapter 5 read as an assault and nothing else, and "I'd have this one again" frightened me for Maylis but tempted me with nothing.

The book proves it can do this. Chapter 13 puts me inside Lesca in the van for the first and only time over there: "Rain was loud on the roof of the van. She had crossed for that sound, and for a bed with two pillows." That sentence arrives one page before she lets go. It is needed eight chapters sooner.

What I would do: tell the last two sections of chapter 5, from the soup to the fourth stair, from inside Lesca. The book already changes seats after a section break (Coralie in 6 and 10, Arlette in 8). She eats soup made by a Coralie who looks at her. She is asked questions and likes answering. She makes Coralie laugh, sees her look across the table for something and does not know what. Under the dish towel a hand closes that is not hers. Then her foot is on the first stair, the reader wants it with her, and the right hand locks on the post. Nothing in the plot changes.

**2. Maylis spends a day in the mayor's house and never meets the mayor's Coralie. Chapter 6. One missing scene.**

> "Maylis woke up in the mayor's bed, under a dry roof. She was the one driving." Then: "The bed had one pillow." Then she is in the square.

There are two Maylises and two Coralies. Three of the four pairings are played. The fourth, Maylis facing the woman who has slept across a landing for eleven years, is skipped on the one morning it is free. It is the scene that would show Maylis what her own silence looks like when it has set, and she needs it: she makes her own landing in the very next chapter. It would also give the mayor's Coralie, who appears only with Lesca, a wrist that says nothing and a woman who cannot pick between two things.

What I would do: a page at the top of the Wednesday. This Coralie comes round the bed, takes the wrist, calls her Madame le maire and asks something with two answers. Maylis says "Either" in the mayor's low voice. Coralie has not heard that word in this house since 2014. Let her put down what she is holding. Then the pillow crossing the landing in chapter 7 is a thing Maylis has already seen the end of.

**3. The "Either" that floods the Port is the only one with nobody inside it. Chapter 12.**

> "'Either,' Maylis said. / 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. 'That's how the vote left it.' / He was off the platform and down the steps before she could take it back."

She says the word aloud five times (2, 5, 6, 9, 12). The first four each have a reason on the page: in 5 she uses it on purpose to stop Lesca's mouth, in 6 it is literally true, in 2 and 9 she really has no preference. The fifth is the hinge of the character and of chapter 17, where Madame Darrigrand says "At 5 on Friday it was light... You had the key on your belt then." At that moment Maylis has the handle fitted, Kévin has released her an hour earlier, the man she wants to save is standing on the bank, and she wants it open. Then the word comes out and she lifts the handle off. I am given "The river went by under the boards" and nothing of her. The word is treated as binding, as if it were a rule of the world like the ones in the steel room. It is not. It is a habit, and I need the half second in which the habit wins.

What I would do: put me in her for that half second. Let her know what the word has always done for her, which is to hand the choice to whoever asked, and what it has cost until now, which is a duck dinner. Then let her stand with her hand still on the handle after Jeannot has gone down the steps, with Larrère's car a hundred metres off and the folder in it, so that taking the handle off is hers and not the word's.

**4. Coralie's preference for the other one is ended by the weather, and never comes back. Chapters 5, 8, 9, 17.**

> "'I'd have had you. You answer. But what I'd have had is a river over forty-two beds.'" (9)

The most dangerous thing in the book is that the wife, honestly and out loud, prefers the version who answers. It is Dark Matter's question, asked with soup. Chapter 9 closes it with a fact about rainfall. Coralie never has to turn Lesca down for what Lesca is. The better reason is in the same speech and is passed over: the one who answers has just answered with a lie ("you give me the tide"). And in chapter 17, when Coralie says "I know what you picked. I was the other one", the woman who would have said "the gate" down that phone is not in the room.

What I would do: end the chapter 9 speech on the lie, not the river. And in the kitchen in 17, give Coralie one sentence that owns it: she wanted the one who would have answered, and that one would have drowned Jeannot. Then her leaving is partly her own business and not only Maylis's failure.

**5. One scene, nine times. Chapters 1, 4, 5, 7, 9 (twice), 10, 14, 17.**

> "Coralie picked up the bowl of coffee and poured it down the sink." (9)
> "She left the pencil lying across the pad." (9)
> "'I asked where you'd be,' Coralie said. She went up to the spare room." (10)

Coralie asks. Maylis gives a measurement or the word. Coralie leaves the room with an object. By chapter 9 I was answering before Maylis did, and the best person in the book had become an examiner. What sixteen years were for rests on the eggs in chapter 1 and "Duck" in chapter 10. Both are excellent. They are not enough to carry the middle.

What I would do: chapters 9 and 10 hold three of these inside one sitting. Keep the two lines that cut ("It's twenty minutes to Bayonne", "And then there's me, as a distance") and cut the exit in chapter 10. End that scene on Coralie's hand flat on the rib while Maylis laughs. Then the doorstep in 12, the one time Maylis asks, has a working marriage directly behind it.

**6. Madame Etcheto's death has four owners, and one of them is a chair. Chapters 15, 16, 17.**

> "'Twelve. One, two.' / Somebody pulled a chair in to a table." (15)
> "'Then it wasn't Inguma that came for her,' Madame Etcheto said. 'It was you.'" (16)

Maylis says "I had the key." Coralie says "That part is mine." Lesca says yes, it was her. Those three I believe, and their overlap is the point. The fourth is the hall of the dead, which called a name, was refused, called twelve and got a sitter. That reads as a trade: the place had to be filled, so it was. If that is true, nothing Maylis did at the dip and nothing Coralie did at 10 o'clock mattered, and the cost I care about most is paid by a rule.

What I would do: cut "Somebody pulled a chair in to a table" and "when they called twelve, somebody sat down". Keep Lesca's guilt as the river she carried over, which is enough and is true. Keep "Nobody emptied hers."

**7. Lesca's eleven years in office arrive as a footnote. Chapter 6.**

> "Maylis knew why, from the chair. Lesca had put cameras on the gates and sent the film to the gendarmes, and Kévin had done six months in prison, for eels. After that she had given him the job and the coat. His mother, Marie-Jo, had been Lesca's deputy mayor until a night in 2018, when she handed her sash back across the council table in a plastic bag."

This is the most interesting thing about her: the woman who made one sum at a gate went on making it, over four houses, her mother's field and a neighbour's son. It is delivered in three sentences of what Maylis happens to know. I see it properly once, in Darrigrand and his swept doorstep, and that scene shows what the others could be. The plastic bag is named three times and never played. I had to infer that the bag and the six months are the same event.

What I would do: play the bag in chapter 3, in five lines, when Marie-Jo first speaks to the wall. In chapter 6 leave only what Maylis can see, which is a man holding his cap.

**8. Maylis has no mother. Whole book.**

> "'Nobody gives it that much just to sleep, Maylis.' / Her mother had never called her anything else." (3)

Arlette has four scenes in Lesca's village and is one of the people I would miss. In Maylis's village, across what must be the same yard, she is not mentioned once, alive or dead. The father is accounted for in both. A reader who has met Arlette asks where she is when the other daughter cracks a rib, loses her job and watches her wife pack.

What I would do: one paragraph. If she is alive, her ponies are still in the field that is a lake over there, and Maylis passes them on the walk down the track in chapter 7. That gives the lake a face at no cost. If she is dead, she is at a table in chapter 9.

**9. The two women are told apart by a habit that belongs to somebody else. Chapter 4.**

> "'There are three points, and I'll say them once.'" (Lesca)
> "'One,' Jeannot said. 'Those are my boots.'" (a hundred lines later)

Lesca's voice is the book's best device for separating the two leads: items, motions, carried, apologies received. Nobody else has it. Numbered points are Jeannot's, and he has them more memorably. In the chapter where she is introduced as a voice she borrows his.

What I would do: take "points" away from her. She has "items". Let her move them and second them.

**10. The reader is not told she is forty-three, so "girl" passes as description. Chapters 1 to 14.**

> "'The village's girl,' Madame Etcheto said." (2)

"Little Lesca", "the village's girl", "a girl to my kitchen". The village sees her from outside as a girl, and the book means that as the village's fault. The last line of the novel pays it off by dropping "little". But the only clue to her age is Lesca's "when I was thirty-one" in chapter 4, with arithmetic to do. Until then nothing stops a reader picturing twenty-five, and then "girl" is not an insult.

What I would do: her age, once, in chapter 1, near the postman who asks for "the man who works for the village".

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The landing, chapter 8.** Coralie stops two steps from the bed, takes the wrist and finds her wife in it. "'That's her,' she said. 'In this hand. I've had sixteen years of her saying nothing, and I know it when I've got hold of it.'" And the refusal: "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." This is the scene that makes Coralie a person and not a prize.
2. **The phone screen, chapter 11.** "'Because you'd have told me.'" Twelve years of a landing explained in five words, and then Lesca trying both answers aloud and finding "all she could remember was turning the handle." Do not let her remember.
3. **Kévin's hand.** The count in the headlights in 14 and its payment in 17: "'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much.'"
4. **The oven door that will not open the third time, chapter 17**, and what follows it: "I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it."
5. **Jeannot's letter 42 and what Maylis does with it.** "It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you." Her answer: "The arm is the excuse you've written for me. It isn't mine." Then the second shovel. The book hands her an alibi and she declines it. Nothing here is softened, and nothing should be.

## 5. Chapter by chapter

- **00.** A woman known by one act before she has a name: she looks down the track, then rings once. 8.
- **01.** Coralie arrives complete in the eggs scene. Maylis arrives as a word on a pad. 7.
- **02.** Kévin and Madame Etcheto each get a voice inside a page. The mouth speaks without her. 9.
- **03.** Lesca's entrance is the best in the book, and the amended motion tells me what she is before she does. 9.
- **04.** The meeting with nobody in the chair is funny. The chrysanthemums are funnier and sadder, because her face tells the truth her mouth will not. 8.
- **05.** "I'd have this one again." I was afraid for the marriage, from the wrong seat. 9.
- **06.** Maylis as mayor, slowing down to be greeted, is the first time I saw what she wants. Long, and the house is empty of the one person who should be in it. 7.
- **07.** Pink in the sink, and two women standing on a trapdoor in one pair of boots. Thin on people. 7.
- **08.** The whole village in one room, each of them wrong about what they are watching. Then the landing. 9.
- **09.** Five scenes and two of Coralie's exits. The card with her name on it earns the next chapter. 7.
- **10.** Coralie alone in a wet corridor, existing without Maylis. "Duck." Jeannot alive in six points. 8.
- **11.** The best chapter for people: Arlette in her coat, Darrigrand and his step, Coralie with the speech. 9.
- **12.** She asks, on a doorstep, and gets 7 o'clock. Then the word on the platform, which I did not believe as written. 9.
- **13.** Two hands fighting over a steering wheel, and the mayor sending apologies to the dead. 9.
- **14.** "Here." "I know you are." I could not have stopped. 10.
- **15.** She says it. The square's silence is drawn person by person, and Marie-Jo finds her a job. 8.
- **16.** Three payments in three short scenes. Larrère is finally brief. Reads as an appendix until "Surname first." 7.
- **17.** No next chapter. Coralie's bag, the two cards, the second shovel. I closed it at 9.
