# Critic's report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** Clear from chapter 3 and never inflated. "I'd have had you. You answer. But what I'd have had is a river over forty-two beds."
- **Tension: 7.** Chapters 12 to 14 are tight; chapters 7 to 9 are not. "Then the screen said 01:12. She hadn't seen it change."
- **Progression: 6.** The middle runs the same two situations in turn. "On Thursday night she took a blanket down to the kitchen and lay on the floor between the table and the stove." She pushes until 4, again.
- **Comedy: 7.** It reaches 8 or 9 in about a dozen scenes and drops to nothing for whole chapters that are not the flood. "Eleven years I've been timed like an egg. I have sentences in that register with no ends on them."
- **Characters: 8**, carried by the village more than by the two leads. "It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you."
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8**, and 9 in chapters 10 and 17. "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—'"
- **Prose: 7.** The deadpan is exact and is the right instrument for this comedy; it is also one speed, with measuring tics. "Rain fell on the card and the ink of the L ran. The L cut in the stone wouldn't run."
- **The ending: 8.** It costs a death, a job and a marriage, and every running joke is paid. "'One,' she said, in the voice she called the numbers in. 'Just the one. Sit down on it.'"
- **Clarity: 6.** The reader keeps an inventory of who holds which limb, and has to work out a weather table. "Lesca had the rest: the legs, the left hand and the mouth."

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. Would I press it on a friend? Yes, on chapters 1, 5, 8 and 10, and I would tell them chapter 7 is short.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Against The Good Place: below, by one point. Level at its best, below across the whole.**

Level:

- **The moral problem is funny and stays large.** The one-against-many sum is never argued; it is handed to the village as procedure. A stone that cannot be given a verb ("Castagnet, Opened. It reads as if he was opened."). A man with a broom who answers the mayor's arithmetic: "It's four when you're the one counting. When it's your house it's one, and they knock it down." And the café's greeting changing from "Still in the box, were you?" to "Had the key and went for a walk, did you?", so that the village's joke is the verdict. The series did the trolley problem as slapstick; this does it as minutes of a meeting, and it is as good.
- **Each person is wrong in their own way, and the way is their comic tic.** Larrère's gap in the window, Jeannot's numbered points, Lesca's motions, Maylis's Either, Coralie's boxes. The tic becomes the fault, which is how the series built Chidi. "Either" on the platform at 5 p.m. is that move done at full weight.
- **The steel room's rule** (standing up, a woman cannot say what she knows is untrue) is a device of the series' kind, and it builds: "It's the ti—", "I w—", then "'I won't.' It came out whole."

Below:

- **The reversal.** The series' reversals arrive as a laugh and re-read everything. This book's (Lesca is the water) arrives in chapter 7 as two dry days on a calendar, in a chapter with nothing to smile at. The other reveal, whose kitchen Larrère rings from, is planted well, but the reader has had it since the folder on Maïté's cupboard in chapter 4.
- **Rate of invention in the middle.** The series changes the situation every episode. Chapters 7 to 10 alternate two: Maylis keeps Lesca out or lets her in, and Coralie asks a question that Maylis fails.

Above:

- **The joke that is allowed to die.** Coralie's oven-door impression, done in joy in chapter 1, done in complicity in chapter 9, and broken off after one word in chapter 17. The series never let a running joke fail in the mouth like that.

**Against Crouch**, on this lens there is one thing to say. He makes a reader afraid for a marriage by showing it happy for longer than one chapter. See fault 1.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The marriage is funny for one chapter, then becomes an exam sat nine times. (Structural; chapters 4 to 17.)**
"What do you want, Maylis?" (1, twice, and 17). "Tick one." (4). "What do you say to that, Maylis?" (9). "I'm asking where I am in this." (9). "'I asked where you'd be,' Coralie said. She went up to the spare room." (10). "Say which, Maylis. For once." (14).
Chapter 1 is the best company in the book because both of them are funny at once: "I don't cook Either. There's no recipe for it." against "He'd only ring me to dig the hole." From chapter 4 Coralie asks, Maylis fails, Coralie leaves the room with a line. The reader has the result by chapter 5; the next five sittings only confirm it, and three of them fall inside two chapters. Coralie, the funniest person in the book, is rationed to an examiner, and the thing the reader is supposed to fear losing is not shown again.
Do: cut two sittings. The morning question in chapter 9 ("It's twenty minutes to Bayonne") and the end of the bandage scene in chapter 10 are the same beat as "And then there's me, as a distance", which is the best of the three; keep that one. End the bandage scene on the hand holding the rib while she laughs. And in the sittings that stay, let Maylis fail the way she fails in chapter 1, evasive and funny ("Whichever's less trouble"), not mute.

**2. The woman with the dry tongue loses it at chapter 7. (Structural; chapters 7 to 17.)**
First half: "They've had the practice. This is my first night." "Put it in forty-two." "I go in at the front, Guy." Second half: "It's so I know where you are." "The van will be at the gate from dusk." "Both."
Two causes. When Lesca drives, the rule gives the mouth to the woman who speaks in motions, so the book's wit rides as a right hand (three words at the bingo, three lines in the café). And when Maylis has her mouth back, she is given instructions and measurements to say. Her pricing of the world is the narrator's only comic voice ("By her count he had half a wheelbarrow there"; "Maylis had never kept anybody waiting in her life, so she took her time over it"), and there are four such sentences in chapter 5 and none in chapters 7, 9 or 12.
Do: while Lesca has the mouth, keep Maylis's count running in the narration, one line a scene. When she has it herself, give her one dry answer a chapter up to the dip. "Then it gets dug for nothing" in chapter 17 shows the voice survived; the reader should not have had to wait ten chapters for it.

**3. The book's reversal is delivered as arithmetic, in its one dead chapter. (Chapter 7.)**
"The two days that followed, Thursday and Friday, had been the only dry ones. Last night she had lain still and Lesca had come across. By noon it was raining again. The rain came when Lesca came. Maylis had nobody to say it to and no mouth to say it with."
This is the turn that makes the rescuer the flood. It is a paragraph of dates the reader has to check, inside a chapter of lung symptoms and a timed walk. I stopped smiling at the top of chapter 7 and started again at the coffee pot in chapter 8.
Do: the instrument is already on the table. Bordenave's pad has the cross against "her mayor" for the dry Friday, and Jeannot calls it "the best thing on that pad". Let the café read the pad aloud and draw the wrong conclusion (she can be wrong) while Maylis, standing there, draws the right one from the same two lines (she was wrong on the days she was kept out). The reversal then arrives through the village's joke, and "You're the water" at the bingo lands on a reader who got there one scene earlier, not on one who was told.

**4. In the last chapter Maylis explains her fault four times, in four different versions. (Chapter 17.)**
To Kévin: "I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me." To Coralie: "I picked." To Lesca: "At the gate I thought I could do better than you. I drove past the handle to be the one who did." To Jeannot: "The arm is the excuse you've written for me. It isn't mine."
The book's method is that other people say what you did, and say it slightly wrong: "fetching a man out of a dry hole", "I was the other one", the dealer's new greeting. Those are funnier and harder than any of the four confessions, and the confessions do not agree with each other about whether the fault was clean hands, vanity or choosing him over her.
Do: cut the line to Kévin (chapter 14 already has "She wanted it not to be her"). Cut "to be the one who did". Keep "At 3 I never tried the handle", because it refuses Jeannot's kindness and he then digs anyway.

**5. Chapter 16 is four scenes of people speaking verdicts, and wastes Madame Etcheto. (Chapter 16.)**
"'Then it wasn't Inguma that came for her,' Madame Etcheto said. 'It was you.' 'Yes.'"
The scene exists to make sure the reader understood "Twelve. One, two" and the chair pulled in. A woman of ninety-four who sets a demon to count sand is told that she died last night in another village and sat down to bingo, and she responds as a judge. In chapter 2 this woman reported a care assistant for making it lose its count.
Do: let her be herself first. She would want to know the things she would want to know: whether the other one said her words, what she had on her knees, what was called before twelve. Then "Nobody emptied hers", which is the line worth the scene, lands on a reader who was smiling.

**6. The catchphrase that carries the ending is heard once before it is replaced. (Chapters 5 and 17.)**
"'Still in the box, were you?' the dealer said. He didn't look up from his cards. It was what the café had said to anybody who came in late, ever since the June of the steel room."
"Had the key and went for a walk, did you?" is the best single stroke of comic sentencing in the book, and it works at half strength because the line it replaces has been said aloud once, with a gloss attached, twelve chapters earlier.
Do: have it said in the café in chapter 1 or on the Sunday in chapter 4, to somebody else, unexplained. By chapter 5 the reader knows it and the gloss can go.

**7. The best comic premise in the book gets twenty-five lines. (Chapter 9.)**
"'I'm heating the whole square,' Josiane said from behind the counter. 'We can't shut the door,' the oldest member said. 'The president's on the other side of it.'"
An association chaired from the driver's seat, through a gap, with the engine running. Two lines of it, then straight to the vote. Coralie's replay at the oven ("The soup will be consulted") is funnier than the meeting it replays.
Do: let it build for a page before the petition is put. The scene needs its procedure to go wrong three ways through that window before Larrère holds a sheet against the glass, so that the sheet silences a room that was enjoying itself.

**8. Chapter 3 spends chapter 2's last line in three sentences of summary. (Chapter 3.)**
"The other Maylis Lesca lived in a Peyrebarthe that had its flood in 2014. Afterwards it made her mayor. Call her Lesca."
Chapter 2 ends on a mouth saying something its owner did not. Chapter 3 opens with a narrator who has not spoken before, answering the question and assigning a name. It is the only place the voice steps forward, and it does so to remove a mystery.
Do: open in the bed, on the second pillow and the rib that is not hers, and let "Madame le maire" and the stone do the telling. The surname can be taken up without an instruction to the reader.

**9. The same joke, with the same set-up, twice. (Chapters 2 and 9.)**
Chapter 2: "'Either,' Maylis said. At its usual minute Inguma came down off the wardrobe and landed on the floor." Chapter 9: "'Either,' Maylis said. It crossed the room, jumped onto the bed and sat down on her chest."
Chapter 9's is the better (the demon waiting for its work while the clock goes to 02:52) and the plot needs it. Chapter 2's steps on a funnier joke two lines above it: "she had raked the square that afternoon, right down to the drain covers." She has given it nothing to count because she is good at her job.
Do: end the chapter 2 beat on the raked square and keep the word for chapter 9.

**10. Small things that slow the timing.**
- A gloss inside dialogue, said by a mayor to a mayor: "The prefecture at Pau, the State's office over all these villages, sends you a yellow warning." (5). Nobody says the middle clause.
- The unit of measure has become a tic: a hand of water, a hand's width, the width of a hand, for a gate, a door, a trapdoor, a field and a chin; "the width of a door" four times.
- Coralie's refusal in one mould three times: "Don't give me the minutes." "Don't tell me what he was." "Don't give me the measurement." Keep the last.
- Monsieur Daguerre's "Here" is used five times. In chapter 12 ("He thinks it's bingo") it is only keeping the joke warm; the four others each do something.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The oven door, three times.** Chapter 1 with the handle, chapter 9 with the vote, chapter 17 breaking off. Change nothing in any of the three.
2. **"Either" on the platform at 5 p.m., and its echo.** "'Either,' Maylis said. 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said." Then "Both" down the phone, and "I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it." The comic tic is the fatal act, with no cushioning sentence between the question and the word.
3. **Jeannot's points, from the boots to letter forty-two.** Above all the six points at 5 in the morning, placed straight after the sole of a rubber boot under black water. "And six. Bugger off." is the cleanest switch in the book.
4. **Both bingos.** The village hall, where "You're the water" is heard as thirst and Bordenave asks how it is spelled. And the hall of the dead, where a man has his arm around his card so that his neighbour can't copy it, and the mayor "sends her apologies".
5. **The square after the confession.** The deputy turning the sheet over to see whether there is more on the back; the man beyond the last loudspeaker who claps; the medal asked back "until things are clearer"; "Somebody has to stack the chairs."
