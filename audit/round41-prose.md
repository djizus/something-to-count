# Critic's report: Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else. Counts below are my own, taken over the eighteen files (about 42,600 words).

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the care home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the fields there was one man, at his duck hut, and no one came to tell him." (3) The sum is countable from chapter 3 and never moves, which is right, but it is small beside a Crouch premise and the book has to earn the difference in people. It does so only from chapter 10.
- **Tension: 7.** "Five. Her mouth tasted of coins." (12) Chapters 12 to 14 are as tight as anything in the comparisons. Chapters 4, 7 and 9 are spent keeping the device's books.
- **Progression: 6.** "So that's a fourth point, and I didn't have it on my list." (4) The device is still being issued new rules in chapter 9, more than halfway through, and one of them contradicts the evidence the reader has just been shown.
- **Comedy: 8.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station." (6) The moral fault is a running joke with a price on it, which is what The Good Place does.
- **Characters: 8.** "I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it." (17) Coralie is the best thing in the book and has her own grammar from her first line.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 9.** "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'The—' / She stayed bent over for a moment with her hand on the door." (17) A joke the reader has laughed at twice fails in the mouth of the woman who made it. Neither comparison does this better.
- **Prose: 7.** "Maylis got that laugh three or four times a year. She had always laughed with Coralie. Lesca didn't know there was another half." (5) Exact, plain, well made, and then it explains the gesture it has just shown. That is the whole case in three sentences.
- **The ending: 8.** "She heard the passage, then the front door, then the car door shutting on a pillow." (17) It costs a death, a marriage, a job and an office. It is weakened only by saying its moral four times.
- **Clarity: 6.** "Keeping me out for a night or two changes nothing, because the river catches up the day I'm back. One night matters." (9) Two pages after a chart has proved that keeping her out dries the road.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. From chapter 10 I did not stop.
**Would I press it on a friend?** Yes, and I would tell them the book starts paying in chapter 5 and does not stop paying after chapter 10.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Level, made of one win and one loss.**

- **Above Crouch in the sentence.** Crouch writes fast and thin: stock fear, one-line paragraphs, the same heartbeat on every page. This book has no stock emotion word in it. Fear is "her neck didn't know that", grief is "There was a hatbox on it. Nothing else." A Crouch flood is adjectives. This one is "The river went in through the door and came out through the kitchen window, carrying a chair."
- **Below Crouch in ease.** Crouch never asks the reader to hold a table. This book asks for the date, the river's height against a red line, which hand belongs to whom, how many rings, how many letters, how many turns of the handle, and eleven rules of the device. The reader can do it. Having done it, the reader is then told the answer by the narrator, in the past perfect. Crouch states a rule once, in a scene, and moves. The Good Place gives its reversal in one spoken line. Here the reversal is worked out as arithmetic in chapter 7, spoken in chapter 8 and charted in chapter 9.
- **Level with The Good Place in making each person wrong in their own grammar.** Larrère through a gap ("I'll be brief"), Jeannot in numbered points, Sarthou without contractions, Maïté unable to finish a sentence, Coralie in boxes to tick, Lesca in items and motions. The voices are the comedy and the comedy is the characterisation. That is the show's method and the book has it.

The gap to the bar on this lens is ease, and it is closed by cutting, not by rewriting.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. The reversal is spent as bookkeeping before it is spoken (7, 8, 9). Structural.

> "On Wednesday and Thursday nights Maylis had pushed Inguma off until 4. She had never got as far as the chair, and the chair was Lesca's only way across. The two days that followed, Thursday and Friday, had been the only dry ones. Last night she had lain still and Lesca had come across. By noon it was raining again.
>
> The rain came when Lesca came."

This is the book's turn: the rescuer is the flood. It re-reads every chapter before it. It arrives as five past-perfect sentences of weather accounts, on a platform, thought by a woman the text admits has "nobody to say it to and no mouth to say it with". Then it is given again at the bingo bar, where it is funny and terrible at once ("You're the water." / "She wants water."), and a third time as Coralie's three-column chart, which is the proof. The first version is the weakest and it takes the surprise out of the other two.

**Do:** in chapter 7 keep the evidence and cut the conclusion. Keep Bordenave's cross, the dry road, the jam jars in his flooded cellar and "'Fifty-five under,' Lesca said out loud, to the right hand. 'My file has the same.'" Cut the paragraph above, the line after it, and the chapter's last paragraph ("But he would only need saving if Lesca was here on Friday night"). End on the trapdoor: "The gate was a post on top of that line, no bigger than a nail." The reader then watches Maylis clench her teeth through bingo without being told why, and gets the reason in the same three words the hall mishears. The chart in chapter 9 stays as it is.

### 2. The device's rules arrive as rulings, and keep arriving (4, 9). Structural.

> "The second item is how this works. There are three points, and I'll say them once." (4)
>
> "A week ago you asked me why it comes, and I told you I didn't know. Now I know. It comes with me. Keeping me out for a night or two changes nothing, because the river catches up the day I'm back. One night matters. If I'm in your village at 2:51 on Friday night, your bank goes, as mine was going. If I'm not, it holds." (9)

Chapter 4 gets away with a briefing because the chairing is funny. Chapter 9 has no such cover, and its rule undoes what the reader was just taught: two nights out, two dry days, a cross on Bordenave's pad. Nothing shows how Lesca knows it. The room where nobody can say an untrue thing is being used as the author's guarantee. By my count the reader is asked to hold eleven rules: knowledge crosses in the chair; staying seated is crossing; the owner keeps the right hand; Inguma takes the visitor home when its count is done; anyone with the words can set the count; nobody standing in the room can lie; the rain follows Lesca; only one night matters; the one who can't be taken home dies and plays bingo; the owner weakens the longer the visitor stays; the empty body's pulse falls. A Crouch reader gives a book one impossible thing and its consequences.

**Do:** choose the rules the ending needs (she brings the river, Inguma sends her home, the owner keeps one hand, the room won't take a lie) and let every other one happen once without being stated. Cut the chapter 9 speech to "It comes with me." Friday already carries itself: the reader has the file, the date and 2:51, and Maylis's plan to sit up follows from "A third puts this rib through my lung."

### 3. Chapter 3 opens by answering chapter 2's hook in a voice the book never uses again (3).

> "The other Maylis Lesca lived in a Peyrebarthe that had its flood in 2014. Afterwards it made her mayor. Call her Lesca."

Chapter 2 ends on the best line break in the book ("'Come back across. I've left you on that landing eleven years,' her mouth said. Maylis hadn't said it."). The next three sentences are a synopsis, and "Call her Lesca" is the only time in eighteen chapters that the narrator turns to the reader. The page that follows does the same work with objects: one pillow against two, a dry roof against rain, the yellow coat she handed in, the alarm that rings across a landing at home. Chapter 3 also carries twice the book's rate of past perfect (76 uses of "had" in 2,600 words): it reads as the back-story chapter because it is written as one.

**Do:** cut the three sentences. Begin "Lesca lay on her back and listened to the rain on the roof. The room could have been her own." The name needs no introduction; "Give me that wrist, Madame le maire" a page later tells the reader who she is. In the file-reading scene, cut "Today was the 28th." and the paragraph beginning "Ever since she stood at the stone that morning"; the five identical lines of the file and "That village hadn't had its flood yet." are enough.

### 4. The ending states its moral four times (17).

> "I waited for the river to decide, so that it wouldn't be me." (to Kévin)
>
> "'At the gate I thought I could do better than you,' she said. 'I drove past the handle to be the one who did.'" (to Lesca)
>
> "It was my own night I was putting right." (Lesca)
>
> "The arm is the excuse you've written for me. It isn't mine." (to Jeannot)

Chapter 14 already has the line, at the moment of the choice: "She wanted it not to be her." Coralie then gives the only version that is news to the reader and could only be hers: "That was Either, with the river in it." The other four are the book marking its own paper. The chair scene is the worst case: two women tell each other what the reader watched each of them do.

**Do:** cut Maylis's line to Kévin, so that he goes from "I tipped them back in the river" straight to "I flew". In the chair, cut from "I sent you down that track" to "I wouldn't have stopped." What is left is "Forty-one." / "Is yours in the house?" / "Across the landing. Four steps. Yours?" / "She took the pillow." / "Don't come again." / "I won't." and the line that it came out whole. That is the scene. Keep the exchange with Jeannot: she is correcting a public record, which is an act.

### 5. The narrator explains the gesture after showing it (throughout).

The class, not the instance: a sentence in the past perfect or with ", so" that follows a clear action and names its motive. There are 47 of ", so" in the book. Chapter 1 uses one three-part sentence three times:

> "What she wanted was for Coralie to stay awake and keep her fingers on that wrist. But Coralie had to be up before 6, so Maylis didn't ask."
>
> "Maylis wanted the duck. But the duck meant an hour at the stove for Coralie after a day's work, so she wrote *Either* across the bottom"
>
> "She had written to him three times about that handle. But he paid her wages, so she didn't knock on the glass."

Keep the duck, which defines Either. The other instances to cut:

- 2: "If she looked, she would know. Then she would have to do something about it." (She has already turned her back on the open doors.)
- 5: the three sentences about the laugh, quoted under Scores. "Then she looked across the table for the other half of the laugh" is complete.
- 5: "Maylis knew what Lesca wanted. Maylis had lain still for the village's sake. It was going to cost her Coralie."
- 6: "That was the second time he had asked her about that kitchen, and he still hadn't asked about the river."
- 8: "Inguma had gone to count, so Lesca was still there. Maylis had kept her teeth shut all evening to stop that."
- 12: "She wasn't going to have him turn the handle that sent him to prison."
- 13: "Twelve years ago she had let his phone ring once and then done what she had come to do. Holding this wheel was the same thing."
- 14: "She had got Lesca out of her, but too late: the river was through the bank already."
- 14: "If she had stopped at the handle instead of driving past it, the gate would have been up at 3." (Jeannot has just said "3:20".)

### 6. One sentence length, one sentence shape (throughout; worst in 3, 4, 9).

By a rough count the average sentence is under ten words, nearly one in three is five words or fewer, and about one in seventy passes twenty-five. The standard shape is a plain statement with a trailing "with" clause: ", with" occurs 142 times, once every 300 words. "Then" opens 89 sentences and "It was" 73.

> "Lesca's mother stood in the doorway of the mayor's office in her coat, with her bag on her arm." (11)
>
> "Maïté came up the steps with the flowers in both hands and her bag on her wrist." (15)
>
> "She was waiting below the stone with her bag on her arm." (15)

In chapters 13 and 14 one speed is the right speed. In 3, 4 and 9 it is a metronome, and it is the main reason the book reads as carefully made and not as easy. Crouch's ease is change of speed. The paragraph that ends on a flat short sentence is the same habit at larger scale, and once it turns into the narrator's own joke at the worst moment: "There were five of them, locked on the brake. It was a short count." (13) That is the first time Maylis does not say Either, and the narrator talks over it.

**Do:** once a page, join three short statements that are one motion into one sentence. Let one paragraph in three end without a closing beat. Cut "It was a short count."

### 7. Definitions set into the text like footnotes (1, 2, 3, 5).

> "The prefecture at Pau, the State's office over all these villages, sends you a yellow warning." (5)

A mayor is explaining the prefecture to the man who was mayor before her. In narration the same form recurs: "glass eels, the baby eels that came up the river on the winter tides" (2); "the catch, the steel tooth that stopped the chain running back" (5); "a motion, the kind the council voted on" (3); "Sarthou, the gendarme, lived in it" (5); and, dropped into the middle of Jeannot's first count, "It was a cellar dug into the field, under a trapdoor in the grass." (1)

The reader needs every one of these facts. The form is a textbook's, and it stops the voice each time.

**Do:** cut the gloss from Lesca's speech; "Pau says orange" explains itself by chapter 12. Cut the hut from chapter 1, where it interrupts Jeannot; it is shown properly in chapter 7 ("only a low bump in the tall grass, with a chimney pipe sticking up out of it"). Give the eels to Kévin, who would say what they fetch with pleasure.

### 8. In the shared-body chapters the name becomes a drum (5, 7, 8).

> "Lesca unlocked the box with the key from the belt, fitted the handle and began to turn it with the left hand. The chain came up wet. Lesca counted the clicks of the catch" (5)

"Lesca" appears 36 times in chapter 5 and 21 times in the 1,460 words of chapter 7. The convention itself is right and should stay: borrowed parts take "the" ("the mouth", "the legs"), the owner's hand takes "her". But the name is doing a pronoun's job, and one verb moves the body every time: "Lesca walked her" five times in chapters 5 and 8, three more "walked her" in 7. The convention slips once: "Maylis clapped her right hand over her own mouth" (9), where everywhere else it is "the mouth".

**Do:** make the body parts the subjects. The book already knows how: "The legs took her up onto the hut and stopped on the trapdoor." (7) Use that for movement and keep "Lesca" for speech and decisions.

### 9. Stock gestures and overdrawn counters (throughout).

- People look at things to avoid people: "looked at" or "looking at" 66 times. It is a good joke on Larrère ("who was looking at the church") and it is then everybody's ("looked at the radiator", "looked at the water jug", "looking at the flowers").
- Every older woman is fixed by a held bag: Maïté ("held her handbag in front of her with both hands"), Arlette ("with her bag on her arm", twice), Madame Etcheto ("her handbag on her knees", twice), and a dead woman in the hall ("lifted her handbag off the empty chair"). Twelve mentions in all.
- "kept" 55 times, "still" 70, "at a time" 12.
- The hand as a unit: fine for water, which is Jeannot's measure. It has spread to doors and gaps ("open about the width of a hand", "lifted a hand's width", "a hand's width from her chin").

**Do:** halve "looked at". Leave the handbag to Madame Etcheto, where it ends under her hand in room twelve and earns its place. Keep the hand for water only.

Not on this list: "two fingers on the inside of it" (11 times) and "four steps" (5). Those are the love story and they are counted on purpose.

### 10. One retort belongs to everybody (1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 11, 12).

> "A rib doesn't give you 112." (Coralie)
> "A rib doesn't laugh." (Madame Etcheto)
> "It's soup. It doesn't need a vote." (the other Coralie)
> "A tide comes in twice a day. It doesn't take Thursday off." (Coralie)
> "It has enough seats when nobody's counting." (Coralie)
> "It's four when you're the one counting." (Darrigrand)

The cast is otherwise told apart by grammar better than in most published novels, which is why the shared move shows: the literal-minded correction is the author's wit handed round. It is a nurse's habit and a gendarme's.

**Do:** leave it with Coralie and Sarthou. Keep the paired ribs, since two women seeing through the same excuse is a point. Find Darrigrand a line that is a householder's and not a wit's; "All I ask is that nobody puts a glass down on my step" already is.

### Usage and consistency

- **Mixed English.** The book is British (pavement, windscreen, torch, rubbish bin, handbrake, cool box, care home, -ise spellings) but has "truck" (7), "dish towel" (6) and "baby carriage" (2). Choose: lorry, tea towel, pram, or change the rest.
- **Times.** Narration uses the 12-hour clock with no a.m. or p.m.; screens and files use four digits. This holds everywhere except the dateline of chapter 1, "2:51 a.m.", the only one in the book.
- **The coat.** COMMUNE DE PEYREBARTHE is "across its back" (1), "across its shoulders" (3), "across the back" (6).
- **The duck hut** is described from scratch three times (1, 7, 12).
- **A count that is off.** On Monday 30 November the dealer says "The 8th's a week off"; in chapter 9 Bordenave says "I wrote that eight days ago". In a book built on counts, the reader checks.
- **Present-tense rulings by the narrator**: "A motion is supposed to say what it is for." (3) and the generic "you" in "the way you carry a child" and "the kind you turn by hand" (14). Three instances; make them past and third person or accept them as the voice and use them more.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The corridor, chapter 14, from "Here." / "I know you are." to "Forty-one."** The man who asks whether it is Thursday ("Then they'll want the bins out"), the cap that stays on, Kévin counting five fingers in his own headlights, the hatbox on the wardrobe, Coralie counting to an empty chair. Not a word.
2. **Coralie's shopping pad and her last page.** The boxes, "I don't cook Either", "Tick one", "And then there's me, as a distance", and the eleven stairs down to "the car door shutting on a pillow".
3. **The confession and the square after it, chapter 15.** "Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him." The loudspeaker saying "drowned him" late, the deputy turning the sheet over to see whether there is more, the sentence travelling back "row by row, at walking pace", "A child asked what the lady had done. Somebody told the child."
4. **The village's verbal furniture.** Larrère's window and "I'll be brief", ending in the one time he says it and says nothing. The kitchen timer and "Eleven years I've been timed like an egg." Jeannot's numbered points and letter forty-two. The verb for the stone ("It reads as if he was opened"). Sarthou reporting a line.
5. **The plain register at the two worst moments.** The prologue and its return in chapter 10 ("Then the water rose up the door and covered it, and the red light went out"), Coralie's account of the rib going through the lung in chapter 7, and "She wanted it not to be her." in chapter 14. That last line is the one place where the narrator saying it outright is the point. It is why the four restatements in chapter 17 should go.
