# Critic report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "I'd have had you. You answer. But what I'd have had is a river over forty-two beds." (9)
- **Tension: 7.5.** "It was an hour and nine minutes short of 4." (12) Chapters 12 to 14 hold; chapter 7 lets go.
- **Progression: 6.** "She pushed until 4 again." (7) The middle repeats its moves: three nights of pushing, five fights over one body.
- **Comedy: 7.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station." (6) Steady smile in 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 8. It stops in 7 and 16 and thins in 3 and 9.
- **Characters: 7.5.** "Eggs are what Either gets in this house." (1) Coralie is an 8. The village is each wrong in its own way.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies," said to the dead at their bingo table (13). This is the one place the book is level with the comparison.
- **Prose: 7.** "each one whole and then in parts" comes five times (8, 9, 13 twice, 15), and "it stuck on the same word" three times (4, 9 twice). Plain and quick otherwise.
- **The ending: 7.5.** "It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you." (17)
- **Clarity: 6.** "Then Maylis put up the left hand, and Lesca's right hand let go of the timer and went up beside it." (6) The reader has to work out whose hand is whose, and is never told.

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes. Would I press it on a friend? Not yet. I would lend it and say "get past chapter 7".

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below The Good Place on comedy, by one point.**

- The Good Place springs its reversal on the audience and the characters in the same second. This book has its reversal ("You're the water") and a perfect room to spring it in, a bingo hall that hears it as thirst. But the narrator has done the sum for the reader a page earlier, at the end of chapter 7.
- The Good Place tells you the rules of the place as jokes, early, so the farce can run. Here the rule of the shared body (the visitor has the legs, the left hand and the mouth; the owner keeps the right hand) is the engine of every comic scene from 3 to 13, and it is first said outright in chapter 13.
- Its set pieces go to a fourth beat. Two of this book's best comic premises stop at the second: the association chaired through a car window (9), and the café refusing to lend a hand, which is given one sentence (12).

**Level with it** on making the moral sum funny without making it small. A stone that cannot find its verb ("Castagnet, Opened. It reads as if he was opened"). "It's four when you're the one counting. When it's your house it's one, and they knock it down." Monsieur Daguerre saying "Here" when the date is read. The saved forty-two, of whom thirty-four "have died since then, in those beds, of the things people die of". Maylis's "Either" is Chidi's fault and it kills the way his does. The track, the one man and the handle are the trolley problem, and the book is right never to say so.

**Above Crouch** on comedy, since he has none. The Dark Matter situation, the other self who wants your bed, is here written as a council motion: "That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it." A wife who tells the two apart by pulse beats Daniela telling the Jasons apart. But Crouch never asks the reader to track limbs.

Other works a reader will name: All of Me (two people fighting for one body), Jean de Florette (a village that knows about the water and says nothing).

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The reversal is told before it is sprung (7, into 8).**
"On Wednesday and Thursday nights Maylis had pushed Inguma off until 4, and Lesca had stayed at home. Thursday and Friday were the only dry days since the 24th." Then: "It was only two days. Bordenave would have called that weather. Maylis didn't."
The narrator works out that Lesca brings the rain, so "You're the water" at the bar confirms what the reader has been handed. The facts are already shown: Bordenave's cross, "It's a metre, and the road's dry", "This floor was dry yesterday".
Cut the sum and the last three sentences of chapter 7. Let the reader get it at the bar with the hall getting it wrong, and let Coralie's three-column chart in 9 be the proof.

**2. The rule of the shared body is never stated (3 to 13).**
"Lesca had the rest: the legs, the left hand and the mouth." (13)
That is the first plain statement, at the climax. Before it the reader infers the split from "Lesca's right hand" in one village and "the right hand ... was Maylis's" in the other. Farce needs the audience ahead of the people in it. In chapter 6, the funniest chapter, I was re-reading to place a hand.
Put it in the first meeting in the box (4), as an item, in Lesca's voice: who has what, and that the mouth is contested. It is a joke she would make and it pays for ten chapters.

**3. Chapter 7 has no scene in it.**
"On Thursday night she took a blanket down to the kitchen and got down on the floor between the table and the stove. At 2:51 Inguma came down the stairs ... and it found her. She pushed until 4 again."
A monster coming downstairs to find a woman hiding between the table and the stove is a comic image, and it is spent in two sentences of summary. Before it, Coralie stops being Coralie and reads out a leaflet: "He said his mouth tasted of coins. He coughed, and it came out pink on the sheet. His breath stopped halfway down, and then at his collar." Every symptom is a plant, and the next page ticks each one.
Play the kitchen-floor night as a scene. Cut the plant to one symptom and give it to Coralie in her own register.

**4. The same fight five times (3, 5, 8, 9, 13).**
"Lesca pulled hard at the jaw, and it went from side to side." (8) The next morning: "She got the little finger up, then the one next to it." (9)
Bed, stairs, bar, kitchen table, van. The bar is the peak. The kitchen bout comes one scene later with the same aim, stopping the words, and less to see.
In 9 keep Coralie pinning the wrist and "I do this all week", which is the point of the scene. Cut the finger-by-finger.

**5. Two comic scenes that stop, one of them unwritten (9, 12).**
"She had tried the café first, but the nine who had signed on Tuesday had watched Jeannot's hand go up. A vote was a vote." (12)
The café refusing her a pair of hands on a point of procedure is a scene, and the book's last line from the café ("Had the key and went for a walk, did you?") needs it. Without it the café mocks her. With it the café mocks her having refused to help, and the reader knows it. And in 9: "Larrère chaired them from the driver's seat of his car." After "I'm heating the whole square" the premise gets one more line before the blank sheet comes out. Coralie's oven version that evening is funnier than the meeting it copies.
Write the café refusal, short. Give the car-window meeting two more beats of its own absurdity before the threat, so the turn has something to turn from.

**6. "Either" at the hinge, and "Either" spent twice on the same gag (2, 9, 12).**
"'Either,' Maylis said. / 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. 'That's how the vote left it.'" (12)
She has just leaned her weight on the handle. The reader has watched her decide. The word then reads as the plot needing the gate shut. Chapter 1 has the answer: "It was out before she had time to think about it", of the one time she said what she wanted. Use that here in a clause: the wrong word out before she had time to think. Separately, she says "Either" to Inguma in 2 and again in 9, same shape, same result. Chapter 2 already has its joke (she has raked the leaves she was told to offer). Cut the "Either" there and keep 9, where the monster waits a full minute for its instructions.

**7. The joke's number is the death's number (1, 5, 14).**
"'Forty-one,' Coralie said." (14)
The last word of the darkest chapter is the number the reader knows from "I've paid for forty-one registered letters". "Put it in forty-two" and "what to do with forty-two people" sit in the same chapter. If the rhyme is meant, nothing pays for it. For half a second at the head count I thought of the post. Twelve is also carrying too much: room 12, 12 December, twelve years, twelve seats, twelve letters.
Change the letter count or the head count.

**8. Chapter 16 explains the book's blackest joke.**
"'Then it wasn't Inguma that came for her,' Madame Etcheto said. 'It was you.'"
Chapter 15 did it in two lines: "'Twelve. One, two.' Somebody pulled a chair in to a table." The reader who felt that does not need "when they called twelve, somebody sat down" said aloud the next morning.
Stop the exchange at "Not this door." Keep "Nobody emptied hers" only if it can stand without the explanation above it.

**9. Jokes with a note after them (5, 8).**
"It was what the café had said to anybody who came in late, ever since the June of the steel room." "Nobody in Peyrebarthe had ever heard Maylis's mouth forbid anything." "Maylis had let go of the teeth for that. She would have said it herself."
Have the café say "Still in the box?" to Maylis in chapter 1 or 2, so 5 needs no gloss. Cut the other two.

**10. Letter banter while a man waits to be carried (14).**
"'I put that handle in letter thirty,' Jeannot said. / 'I wrote to them too.' / 'I know you did. They took note.'"
It is 03:31, the water is at the knees, and "There was nobody to carry him." The key coming back with "It's no use to anybody now" is enough.
Cut the two middle lines.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. Coralie at the oven door, all three times (1, 9, 17), above all the one she cannot finish: "There's the question of the—".
2. Lesca in the hall of the dead: "You can put that in the minutes as apologies received", the man with his arm around his card, and "Twelve. One, two."
3. Jeannot's numbered points, from "One. Those are my boots" to "And six. Bugger off", and letter forty-two, which "doesn't thank you".
4. Kévin's fingers: learned in the bucket (2), counted in the flood (14), "I wanted six, Maylis" (17).
5. The square after the confession (15): the deputy turning the sheet over "to see whether there was more on the back", the man who claps, "I've got nothing on the sheet for this", "Somebody has to stack the chairs."
