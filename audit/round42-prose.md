# Critic's report: Prose

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. 42,115 words, 3,714 sentences. Counts below include dialogue unless stated.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.5.** "The trapdoor was twenty-five minutes away, on foot, in the dark, with the dip under water. The bank had ten." (10) The sum is exact and personal, but it is not on the page until chapter 3.
- **Tension: 7.5.** "On the sixth ring the breath wouldn't come." (12) Chapters 10 to 14 are as tight as the comparison. Chapters 4 to 9 are not.
- **Progression: 6.** "'Either,' Maylis said. It crossed the room, jumped onto the bed and sat down on her chest." (9) The same beat, with the same result, closed a scene in chapter 2.
- **Comedy: 7.5.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" (6)
- **Characters: 7.5.** "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'" (8) The people are told apart by how they talk, and Coralie carries the book.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Here,' he said. 'I know you are.'" (14) Monsieur Daguerre's running joke is paid in the flood without a change of voice.
- **Prose: 7.** "Jeannot had just sworn at her in six points, and he was alive." (10) A good sentence with the fault of the whole book inside it: the reader has just counted the six points.
- **The ending: 8.** "He went and got it. Then he stepped over the string in his rubber boots and began to dig." (17)
- **Clarity: 6.** "Inguma had gone to count, so Lesca was still there. Maylis had kept her teeth shut all evening to stop that." (8) The reader has to turn the rule over twice to follow the sentence.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. From chapter 10 I did not stop. In chapters 6 to 9 I read on trust.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who reads slowly, yes. Not on the friend who wants *Recursion* for the plane, and that friend is the bar.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, by a short distance, and for reasons that cutting fixes.**

1. **Above Crouch on exactness.** Crouch tells you a heart is pounding. This book has no stock feeling in it anywhere. Feeling is carried by a pillow, a shopping pad, two fingers on a wrist. "Then she laid the hand back on the blanket and closed the eyes with her thumb." (14) Crouch has no sentence that good.
2. **Below Crouch on ease, because the book has one gait.** Crouch changes gear with white space and line length. Here the mean sentence is 11.3 words and every chapter sits between 10.4 and 12.3. The flood (14) runs at 10.9 and the first breakfast (1) at 11.4. The one place the book changes gear is the ring count in 13 ("One ring." "Two." "Three."), and there it does Crouch's trick better than he does.
3. **Below The Good Place on trusting a reversal.** That show turns in one line and never explains it afterwards. "You're the water" (8) is one line, and misheard, which is as good as the show. But the narrator has already worked the sum in a ledger paragraph (7), and Maylis recites it again with the same dates (9). The same narrator explains jokes ("in six points") and glosses looks ("Lesca didn't know there was another half").

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. The narrator says what the scene has just shown

This is a habit of the whole book and it is what stands between this prose and ease. The scene lands, then a sentence tells the reader what landed. I marked about twenty-five. These are the clearest.

- (4) "She had written *Either* on the shopping pad on Tuesday, when she wanted the duck. It had always come when she needed it. It had never been true, and it wasn't true now. She did want one of them, badly." The word has just stuck in her throat. Keep the last sentence only.
- (5) "Maylis got that laugh three or four times a year. She had always laughed with Coralie. Lesca didn't know there was another half." Coralie has just looked across the table for it.
- (5) "She was still waiting for the woman who had ticked the box." This follows "Coralie hadn't moved from the fourth stair" and precedes "'Are you coming up?'", which say it.
- (5) "Maylis knew what Lesca wanted. Maylis had kept still for the village's sake, not for this."
- (5) "Nobody in Peyrebarthe had ever heard Maylis's mouth forbid anything."
- (6) "That was the second time he had asked her about that kitchen, and he still hadn't asked about the river." The narrator is pointing at a clue.
- (7) "Lesca had walked her down the track with a watch because she wanted to save Jeannot."
- (10) "Maylis had been given the whole night, but nothing in it told her which of the two Lesca had turned the handle for." This states the question that Coralie puts far better one chapter later, and takes the edge off the best scene in the book.
- (10) "Jeannot had just sworn at her in six points, and he was alive."
- (11) "For the first time somebody else had said that minute out loud in that room."
- (12) "She wasn't going to have him turn the handle that would send him to prison." The two sentences before it have said so.
- (12) "But she might not get another chance, and that frightened her more than asking did."
- (12) "She was awake, and this night was her own."

**What I would do.** Strike each. Where the gloss carries a fact the reader lacks, move the fact into the scene. The test for every sentence that begins with a character's name and a verb of knowing, wanting or meaning: has the reader already seen it?

The worst case is the twist itself. In chapter 7 the discovery that Lesca brings the rain arrives as accounts: "On Wednesday and Thursday nights Maylis had pushed Inguma off until 4, and Lesca had stayed at home. Thursday and Friday were the only dry days since the 24th. Last night Maylis had kept still. Lesca had got her out of bed at 6, and by noon it was raining again." Five level sentences, and the reader does the adding. Give it to her in the order she would see it, standing on the trapdoor: the dry cellar floor at Bordenave's yesterday, the jam jars in the rain today, whose legs brought her here. One sentence that turns, not five that list.

### 2. One gait

Sentence length does not change with pressure. 110 sentences in 3,714 run past 25 words (3%). Chapter 8 has none. 41% of all sentences open on *The, She, It, He, Maylis, Then, Lesca* or *Coralie*. *Then* opens 100. Cause and result come as separate short sentences joined by *Then* or *So*, which gives steps where the reader wants flow.

Compare two passages eleven chapters apart. (1) "She dropped the pad on the table and pulled the dish towel off the oven door. Maylis was still standing there in her raincoat with her hair wet. Coralie rubbed it dry with the towel, hard." (12) "She lifted it out with her left hand and fitted it in place. It went on with a knock." One is supper and one is the handle going onto the floodgate on the day of the flood, and they are the same sentence.

**What I would do.** Two things, both local. In the calm chapters allow one long sentence a page, so that the short ones mean something. The square emptying in 15 shows the book can do it: "She watched her sentence travel back through the square and down the road, row by row, at walking pace." In the fast chapters use what chapter 13 found. A count on its own line changes the rhythm without a single adjective. The six minutes at the dip (14) and the last page of 12 can take the same treatment.

### 3. Chapter 9 runs the steel-room scene twice, and the scene has become a template

Every scene in the steel room now goes: the chair, she stands, an exchange, a word sticks, the door opens, Inguma comes in. Chapter 9 does it twice in one chapter. The first (Sunday night) is ten lines whose only event is "'It's the ti—' Lesca said. She started again, and it stuck on the same word." It ends "The door of the box opened and Inguma came in over the step." The second (Tuesday night) has "'I w—'" and "to get—", then "The door of the box opened. Inguma came in over the step". The phrase "it stuck on the same word" appears four times in the book, twice in this chapter.

The same chapter repeats chapter 2. In 2 Maylis offers Inguma "the stars behind the cloud or the grass", says "Either", and it comes. In 9 she has "two jobs she could give it", says "Either", and it comes. And between the two, in 6, she named the roadsides without any trouble. A reader who remembers 6 will not believe 9.

**What I would do.** Fold the first steel-room scene of 9 into the second. Cut the *Either* from chapter 2 and end that scene on "It sounded like something she was reading off a label." In 9 cut "Each would keep it busy till morning, so it made no difference which", which explains the joke before the punchline. Then chapter 13's "Lesca waited for Either. It didn't come." pays one debt and not two.

### 4. The narrator reminds the reader of a scene just before using it

About twenty sentences in the pluperfect, usually dated, hand over the callback the reader would have enjoyed making.

- (6) "On Sunday this same woman had come to her own door in a blue dressing gown, with a potato in her hand. Here she was a widow." The black coat does it alone.
- (10) "On Tuesday Maylis had written out her own Friday night as a job."
- (13) "On Sunday, standing in the steel room, Lesca had tried to call it the tide, and the word hadn't come."
- (14) "At 10 Coralie had walked Madame Etcheto back to it." The reader has thought of nothing else for ten pages.
- (14) "She had stood in this corridor once before, with Coralie, outside the last door on the left."
- (16) "Lesca had made the rule in her first month, for Larrère." Chapter 3 said so in nearly the same words.
- (16) "She had handed her own sash back across this table in a plastic bag." Third telling: Larrère says it in 6, the narrator in 15.

The census of years is part of this. "Twelve years" appears 14 times, "eleven years / Decembers / times" 13, "forty houses" 12.

**What I would do.** Cut them. Keep a reminder only where two chapters and a change of village lie between the plant and the use.

### 5. The grammar of the shared body wobbles, and its tag is overused

The device is right: *the* mouth when it is fought over, *her* mouth when it is hers. Chapter 5 breaks it. "Maylis let her mouth speak without her" but "Maylis felt the mouth hang open"; "Maylis could feel her own face smiling at him" and, ten lines on, "The face went on smiling"; "The legs stopped inside the door" but "Maylis felt her legs lift her out of the chair". In all six Lesca is driving.

The tag that announces the device ends two chapters in the same words. (2) "Maylis hadn't told it to." (4) "She hadn't told them to."

In 5, 8 and 13 the bookkeeping is thick: about 19, 17 and 23 uses of *the* with a body part. In 13 it earns its keep ("It was one hand against the other. The one on the brake was the weaker, and it had got there first."). In 5 it slows a comic chapter.

**What I would do.** One rule, applied without exception: *the* for any part Lesca has, *her* for any part Maylis has. End chapter 4 another way. In 5, state once who has what at the head of each scene and then drop the article-and-part subject wherever a plain "Lesca said" will do.

### 6. The opening explains its own secret

(00) "She needed to see the end of that track before she turned anything."

It is the one sentence on the page that gives a motive, and the motive is what Lesca hides until chapters 11 and 15. The next sentence does the work by image: "She looked down that track while the wipers crossed the windscreen twice." The same page has "so she knew what it could take", which the six springs have already said.

The page is otherwise the cleanest in the book and the only one with a different gait (mean 14.9 words).

**What I would do.** Cut the sentence. Cut "so she knew what it could take".

### 7. People enter as a costume and a prop, and react by looking

144 sentences carry a ", with …" tail and 117 end on it. The entrance formula is name, place, garment, then *with* an object in a hand.

- (4) "Coralie stood in her work uniform with the coffee pot in her hand."
- (9) "On Tuesday Coralie stopped at the bedroom door in her uniform, with her keys in her hand."
- (11) "Lesca's mother stood in the doorway of the mayor's office in her coat, with her bag on her arm."
- (4) "Jeannot was on his stool in his jacket, with his glass in front of him."
- (5) "Jeannot was on his stool at the counter with a glass in front of him."

The last two are the same sentence in consecutive chapters.

The reaction shot is a look. *Looked* 94 times, all forms of *look* 134, *waited* 29. "X looked at A, then at B" seven times, three of them in chapter 17: "looked at the coins, then at the suitcase", "looked at the loaf, then at the way Maylis held her side", "looked at the string, at the cut grass, at the way she held the shovel."

**What I would do.** Keep the garment only where it tells (Maïté's blue dressing gown against her black coat; the uniform that "had dried on her twice"). Keep the looks that are jokes ("Larrère looked at the church", "looked at the radiator", "looked at the water jug"). Halve the rest. Let some people come in talking.

### 8. Every change is flagged as a first

*Nobody* 59 times, *never* 40, *every* 49. The narrator marks each step in Maylis by telling the reader it has not happened before.

- (5) "Maylis had never smiled at Jeannot Castagnet in her life, and the whole café was watching her do it."
- (6) "Maylis had never kept anybody waiting in her life, so she took her time over it."
- (5) "Maylis had stood beside that neck at this counter for eighteen years without once looking at it."
- (9) "For the first time, Maylis moved out of its way."
- (14) "Until tonight it had never been off her belt."

**What I would do.** Keep the two that are funny (the smile, the waiting). Cut the flag from the rest. An action that is new looks new.

### 9. One shape of speech in several mouths

The people are well told apart, which makes the leaks show.

- (1) Coralie: "A rib doesn't give you 112." (2) Madame Etcheto: "A rib doesn't laugh." Each answers "It's the rib" or "It was the rib".
- (10) Jeannot on her father: "and he never rang me once." (12) Larrère on her father: "and he never rang me once."
- Nearly everyone asks Maylis a two-box question so that she can fail to answer it: "At 5 this year, or at 6?", "Was the order the mayor's, or the president's?", "Is this a request, or a notification?", "Is that pot hot this year, or do I wait?", "Which is it going to be? Shut, or open?", "The gate, or Castagnet?" By chapter 12 the reader can see the machine.

**What I would do.** Give Madame Etcheto her own reply. Change one of the two "never rang me once" lines unless the echo is meant, and if it is meant, have Maylis notice. Take the *or* out of the Hontaube clerk's and Josiane's questions and keep it for the four people whose questions matter: Coralie, Larrère, Jeannot, Lesca.

### 10. Usage

- **British text with American words.** The book says *mum, pavement, torch, windscreen, pyjamas, bugger off*. It also says "baby carriage" (17, twice), "dish towel" (six times) and "truck" (11, twice; 14). Pram, tea towel, lorry.
- **Second person, in chapter 14 only.** "the way you carry a child", "the kind you turn by hand". The narrator addresses nobody anywhere else.
- **Clipped fragments, in chapter 14 only.** "Torch up. Right arm across her side." The rest of the book writes whole sentences under pressure, and is better for it.
- **Figures.** Readings off an instrument are in figures (pulse, clock, file) and counts are in words. That holds everywhere except the timer: "turned a white kitchen timer to three" (3), "set to one" (6).
- **The alarm.** "Coralie had to be up before 6" (1). The alarm rings at 06:00 (3).
- **Order of days.** Chapter 16 ends on Tuesday 15 December. Chapter 17 opens on Sunday 13. So the last meeting in the steel room (Sunday night) comes before Lesca's council has removed her, though the reader has already read the removal. "She didn't call anything to order" then reads as a consequence of something that has not happened yet.
- **Repeated sentences that are not refrains.** "left the way it had come" (1, 7). "heard the pillow go down on the single bed" (7, 8).

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **"She wanted it not to be her."** (14) and the paragraph it closes. It is the one place where the narrator's bluntness is the right tool, because nothing in the scene could show it.
2. **Two fingers on the inside of the wrist**, from the first page of chapter 1 to room twelve: "She held it for the full half minute." And "'Forty-one,' Coralie said." Do not vary the wording to avoid repetition. The repetition is the point.
3. **The mirrored sentences.** The reflector "screwed on at an angle" (10, then 12). "a clock ticking, close to the phone, the kind that hangs in a kitchen" (10, then 14). The hand flat on the clay (00, then 14). These are word for word on purpose and they are how chapter 14 hurts.
4. **The ways of speaking.** Sarthou without contractions ("I am reporting a line"). Jeannot in numbered points as far as "And six. Bugger off." Maïté breaking off ("In the— It's blankets."). Lesca's Marie-Jo speaking to the wall (3, 6) until "She said it to Lesca this time, not to the wall" (16), and Maylis's Marie-Jo with one chair: "'One,' she said, in the voice she called the numbers in. 'Just the one. Sit down on it.'" (17)
5. **The confession and its echo.** "The loudspeaker on the bank road said 'drowned him' after the square had finished hearing it." "A child asked what the lady had done. Somebody told the child." With it, a detail that should survive any tidying: the narrator says *nobody* 59 times and *no one* only in the typed sentence, "No one came to tell him." And the reply to all the stuck words: "'I won't.' It came out whole. They both heard it." (17)
