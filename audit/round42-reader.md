# Round 42: Reader

Read 00 to 17 in order, once, with nothing else open. I know nothing about floodgates, French town halls, Basque folklore or duck shooting.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.5.** One man against forty houses and forty-two beds, and I always knew which was which. "The trapdoor was twenty-five minutes away, on foot, in the dark, with the dip under water. The bank had ten." It loses half a point because the rib is sold as a killer and then forgotten (fault 6).
- **Tension: 7.** From chapter 10 to chapter 15 I did not put it down. "Then the screen said 01:12. She hadn't seen it change." Chapters 7 and 9 are slack by comparison.
- **Progression: 6.** Between chapters 5 and 12 the same two moves repeat: Lesca gets in and is put out, and Larrère says shut. "She pushed until 4 again."
- **Comedy: 8.** I smiled on most pages and kept smiling. "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'"
- **Characters: 8.5.** Coralie is the best person in the book. "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them."
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** The worst sentence in the book is heard as an order for a drink. "'She wants water,' one of the Lassalle sisters said."
- **Prose: 7.5.** It never made me stop to work out a sentence. "They ate the eggs out of the pan." It has one rhythm for seventeen chapters, and one trick it leans on: "Maylis hadn't told it to." "She hadn't told them to." "Maylis hadn't said it."
- **The ending: 8.** It costs her the job, the village and the marriage, and I believed each loss. "She heard the passage, then the front door, then the car door shutting on a pillow."
- **Clarity: 6.** I finished without knowing whether sending Lesca away changed anything. "Lesca was gone. The water kept coming."

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. I was in from the last line of chapter 2. I would have skimmed chapter 7 and the middle of chapter 9.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On the friend who loved The Good Place, yes, with the instruction to read to the end of chapter 2 before deciding. On the friend who reads Crouch for speed, no, not yet.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch, on two things.**

- The premise takes two chapters to move. Dark Matter has its man taken at gunpoint in chapter 1, with one idea in play. Here the "other you" is planted on the second page ("whether there is another you, somewhere, who sees the other one") and then waits under a village comedy until "her mouth said. Maylis hadn't said it." The short opening does Crouch's job well, but chapter 1 then asks me to hold eleven things.
- The rules. Crouch gives the rules of his box in one scene, from one person, and then only uses them. Here they come from a boy in a tractor bucket, an old woman, a mother at a stove and the other self, across five chapters, and I had to turn back twice.

**Level with Crouch** on chapter endings in ten chapters of eighteen. "Coralie Mounet in it", "'Are you coming up?'", "LESCA, MAYLIS", "The arms of the chair were warm" all made me read on at once. Chapters 7 and 16 end on nothing.

**Above Crouch** on the marriage. In Crouch the wife is the thing that can be lost. Coralie has a form behind the fruit bowl that she posted "for me". I was afraid for the marriage because she could leave for her own reasons, and she does.

**Level with The Good Place** on making the problem funny without shrinking it. This is the trolley problem, and the woman at the lever is a person who writes *Either* on a shopping pad. The joke and the disaster are the same word: "That was Either, with the river in it." The reversal at the bar ("You're the water") re-reads the whole warning plot, as that series' reversals do: the woman who came to warn the village is the flood.

**Below The Good Place** on how wrong each person is in their own way. Maylis, Lesca, Coralie, Larrère and Jeannot each are. Marie-Jo, Josiane, Sarthou and Bordenave are each one habit.

**Overall for this lens: 7.5. Good, and short of level.** The people and the last third are at the bar. The middle and the map are not.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Chapter 12. The real decision of the book takes six lines and I did not believe it.**

> 'Either,' Maylis said.
> 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. 'That's how the vote left it.'
> He was off the platform and down the steps before she could take it back.

At 5 on Friday she has daylight, the key, the handle fitted, Jeannot above ground an arm's length away, and Kévin's word that she is not to keep the gate shut for him. Everything Lesca asked for in chapter 4 is there. She says one word and lifts the handle off. Nothing stops her taking it back: he has only walked down some steps. The flood, room twelve and Coralie's leaving all hang on this moment, and Madame Darrigrand says so in chapter 17 ("At 5 on Friday it was light"). On the page it goes by faster than the phone call to Hontaube. The dip at 2:57 gets a page of her mind. This gets none.

What I would do: give this scene the weight the dip has. Put something real between her and the handle so that *Either* is a choice between two things she can name. Jeannot can stay on the boards. He can say he is going down to his hut whether the gate goes up or not. Larrère's folder is a hundred metres off and she knows whose name is in it. Let her feel the word coming, as she does in chapter 5 ("She felt the mouth get ready for the word"), and fail to stop it. Then let her stand there with the handle in her hand after he has gone, and tell me why she takes it off. The reader should know on this page that this was the hour.

**2. Chapters 7 and 9. The middle goes round twice.**

> On Thursday night she took a blanket down to the kitchen and got down on the floor between the table and the stove.

> 'Four to three,' Larrère said. 'It stays shut.'

Between chapter 5 and chapter 12 Lesca is in, out, in, out, by the same means: push, keep still, say the words, hold the jaw. Larrère refuses the gate on a Monday, a Tuesday and a Friday. Each scene adds something, but what I felt was "another round". Chapter 7 is two beats with padding: pink in the sink, and the walk to the trapdoor with a watch. Chapter 9 is seven scenes, and its best image, the card with her name on it among the dead, comes after I had started to skim.

What I would do: a cut, not a rewrite. Fold chapter 7's two beats into the end of 6 and the start of 8, and lose the chapter. In chapter 9 keep the kitchen chart, the meeting chaired from a car and the hall of the dead. Drop the short steel-room scene in the middle ("It's the ti—"): chapter 11 does it once, at the right moment, and that is enough.

**3. Chapters 7, 9 and 14. I never learned whether she is the water.**

> It was only two days. Bordenave would have called that weather.

> Maylis had given the bank ten minutes, and it had held for sixteen.

The book's biggest turn is that the woman bringing the warning brings the river. The evidence is two dry days. Maylis herself says it could be weather. Then Lesca leaves at 2:53 and the bank still breaks. So was the plan to sit awake in the van ever going to work? Does putting Lesca out buy six minutes, or nothing? Is Lesca right in chapter 16 when she says "I took my river over there with me", or is that guilt talking? I could not tell, and the ending needs me to know.

What I would do: one more proof before the night, on Bordenave's pad, since the café already keeps the score. And one thing I can see at 2:53 when Lesca goes: the rain thinning on the windscreen, or the pillar reading short of her ninety. "Sixteen" is already there as evidence. Have somebody say what it means.

**4. Chapters 00, 1 and 12. I could not draw the map.**

> On her left Jeannot was sitting in his cellar. Behind her, under the bank, were Sarthou and the Darrigrands' baby and old Bordenave, and the rest of the forty houses with their shutters closed.

The fields and the houses are on the same side of the same bank, two hundred metres apart. The whole problem is that water let onto the fields spares the houses. I never understood why the water on the fields does not simply run along into the Port. I also had to take on trust that opening a gate stops a leak two hundred metres away. This is the one piece of the book a reader must picture exactly, and I pieced it together from seven chapters.

What I would do: one plain paragraph in chapter 1, seen from the top of the bank, that says what lies between the fields and the Port and which is lower. A drawn map at the front would not hurt.

**5. Chapters 2 to 6. The rules of crossing come in pieces, and the one that matters is the faintest.**

> 'The thing on your chest is the way in,' Kévin said.

> That morning she had stayed sitting, and she had woken up over there with a mouth.

By chapter 6 I needed to know four things: keeping still takes you to the chair; staying seated when the other one gets up takes you into her bed; the thing then comes to fetch you home; giving it a count makes it late. They are all in the text, from four different mouths. "Stayed sitting" is given as a memory in half a sentence. I also had to work out for myself that the host keeps her right hand, which is the key to every shared scene, including the fight for the van. And I did not understand what Inguma is, leaves over riveted steel, until "like one part of the room touching another" in the last chapter, and I am still guessing.

What I would do: Maylis writes jobs as lists. Before she crosses in chapter 6, let her write this one on the pad, four lines, in her own words. It costs nothing and it is in character.

**6. Chapters 7, 14 and 17. The rib is promised as a death and nothing comes of it.**

> He was dead before the doctor had his coat off.

> Something gave in her side. She heard it go.

> 'Then you'll dig it. With that side. I'll strap it first.'

Coralie tells me a pushed rib goes through the lung and kills a man in minutes. Two nights later Maylis coughs pink. On the night she hauls a heavy man up a ladder, hears something go, runs a kilometre, wades to her hips and pushes wheelchairs for an hour. On Monday she is digging a grave, and the nurse who gave the warning straps her up and leaves. I spent the flood waiting for her to drop. The book did not notice.

What I would do: either lower the claim in chapter 7, so that the man goes to hospital for a month, or pay it. Paying it is better. A Saturday on a stretcher in Bayonne would rhyme with Lesca's, and it would make "nobody was in the house" true in a second way.

**7. Chapters 3, 5 and 11. Lesca's village is thin, and its losses arrive late.**

> The company had taken her mother's field with the rest, and the flood fields were a lake now.

That is in chapter 11, in the middle of a paragraph. Chapter 5 had already used the lake ("In my village there's a lake where the village field was") and I did not know what it meant. The four houses knocked down, the ponies, Kévin's six months and Marie-Jo's sash in a plastic bag all come as asides in chapters 6 and 11. So when Lesca says in chapter 9 "I've worked out what I'd be giving up, and I've written it down. Nil", I could not weigh it. I had seen her kitchen, her landing and a stone.

What I would do: show the lake once, in chapter 3, when she stands at the stone or drives to the Castagnet roof. One paragraph of what she sees where the fields were, and who she did it to.

**8. Chapter 1. Too much to carry before I have a reason to carry it.**

> 'One. The water down there is mine. A duck comes down onto standing water. On Sunday night I had a hand's width of water on those fields.'

By the end of chapter 1 I am holding the thing on the wardrobe, the rib, the steel room, the stone and its date, a mayor in a car, a handle that sticks, a gate that lets water both ways, forty-one letters, a shopping pad and a village festival in 2010. Jeannot's speech about whose water it is was the first thing I skimmed. I did not yet know why the direction of water through a gate would matter.

What I would do: keep the forty-one letters and the joke ("Put it in forty-two"). Move the lesson on the gate working both ways to chapter 6, where his complaint needs it.

**9. Chapter 14. The numbers that matter drown among the ones that do not.**

> Three minutes to get there. Two to get him up the ladder, if he climbed. Three back. That made eight, and then the handle

> It was fourteen metres to the back of the van. She counted them.

She measures everything, and the book uses that well ("Don't give me the measurement"). But on the night I am given six hundred metres, four hundred metres, six minutes, four hundred steps, six hundred metres again, two hundred, twenty, two hundred, sixty steps, a hundred steps and fourteen metres. I stopped reading them. Three of the numbers carry the meaning: 2:57, 3:11 and 3:20.

What I would do: keep the clock, and the two distances at the dip. Cut the rest.

**10. Chapters 3 and 4. Maylis, Maïté and Marie-Jo.**

> 'Saved who?' Marie-Jo said.

> 'I have a verb,' Maïté said.

Three women whose names start with M and A, two of them at one table in the scene where I first meet them, with a Josiane and a Jeannot close by. I mixed up the widow and the bingo caller until chapter 6.

What I would do: rename Marie-Jo.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The shopping pad.** Every use of it, from "I don't cook Either. There's no recipe for it" through the two boxes marked *You* and *Somebody else*, to "I'll ask it once more, with no box."
2. **Chapter 10, whole.** Lesca's night told flat, with the reflector going out after four minutes. Then the phone that rings nine times, "Who's dead?", and six numbered points ending "Bugger off."
3. **Bingo, both halls.** The coffee over Josiane's shoes, "Twelve. That's my door", Madame Etcheto finishing the prayer over her duck, and then the same tables with the dead at them and "'Twelve. One, two.' Somebody pulled a chair in to a table."
4. **Coralie at the phone log.** "2:51. I've known that for twelve years", and the answer to why she never asked: "Because you'd have told me."
5. **The confession and its silence.** "Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him", the loudspeaker saying "drowned him" late, the man who claps, and "Somebody has to stack the chairs."
