# Critic's report: Thriller lens

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** They are concrete and counted, and the reader can say them back: "Behind it were forty houses, and the care home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the fields there was one man, at his duck hut, and no one came to tell him." They lose a point because the second stake, a woman losing her body and her bed to her double, is settled by chapter 9 and never comes back until 2:52 on the night.
- **Tension: 6.5.** It peaks where a clock is on the page: "The last line on the chart said 36. Coralie sat on the edge of the bed with her phone in her hand. The doctor's number was on the screen, with her thumb over it." Between bingo night and the van (chapters 8 to 11) nobody is doing anything to the heroine.
- **Progression: 6.5.** The dates and Bordenave's pad move the book forward a day at a time. The 2014 night moves it back: "*First light. CASTAGNET Jean recovered from the cellar of his duck hut. Trapdoor locked on the inside. Watch stopped at 03:11.*" is printed in chapter 3 and again, word for word, in chapter 6.
- **Comedy: 7.5.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.'" I smiled through most of the village. I stopped smiling in the bingo hall, where the joke is laid over the one line the plot turns on.
- **Characters: 8.** Coralie is as good as anybody in the comparisons: "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them."
- **The switch between dark and comic: 7.5.** At its best it is a joke that fails in front of you: "'The date,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'There's the question of the—' She stayed bent over for a moment with her hand on the door." At its worst (chapter 8) the comic scene swallows the dark one.
- **Prose: 7.5.** Fast, plain, never in the way of the action: "At the end of the track the reflector went on shining in her headlights for four more minutes. Then the water rose up the door and covered it, and the red light went out."
- **The ending: 7.5.** It costs, and nothing is given back: "'There are two hundred chairs in this square. I counted them out of the hall at 7. […] Somebody has to stack the chairs.'" Half a point off for chapter 16, and half for the bill Lesca is let off (fault 4).
- **Clarity: 6.** The reader has to hold six rules at once and is asked to work out the plot from them in the middle of a farce: "Inguma had gone to count, so Lesca was still there. Maylis had kept her teeth shut all evening to stop that."

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. From the last page of chapter 11 to the end of chapter 15 I did not stop. I would have stopped once, for the night, at the end of chapter 8.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who watches The Good Place, yes, today. On a friend who reads Crouch, not yet: I would have to say "it gets going at the stairs", and a book that needs that sentence is a 7.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Overall, for this lens: below Crouch on propulsion and fear, above him on what the choice costs. A 7 against a bar of 8.**

What puts it below:

1. **The pursuer stops pursuing.** In Dark Matter the other Jason has the life from the first chapter, and every chapter after it closes a door. Here Lesca states her aim at the end of chapter 3 ("*and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it*"), nearly gets it in chapter 5, and is then beaten twice by Coralie (the landing in 8, the pinned wrist in 9). From Sunday 6 December to 2:52 on the 11th she does not take the body once. For five days and three chapters the heroine is threatened by a river on a timetable and a man with a cardboard folder.
2. **Too many rules, and the climax waives two of them.** Crouch gives one rule and squeezes it. This book has the chair, staying seated, Inguma fetching, the counting, the words that stick in the steel room, the falling pulse, the rib and the hall of the dead. The reader keeps up, with effort. Then staying awake turns out not to matter (Inguma lands on the van at 2:51 anyway), putting Lesca out turns out not to matter ("Lesca was gone. The water kept coming."), and the one who would not be taken home walks out of the hall of the dead on a joke.
3. **The reversal is thrown away.** "You're the water" is this book's "this is the Bad Place": the rescuer is the cause, and everything before it re-reads. The Good Place gives that line a silent room. This book gives it a hall that thinks she is thirsty, proves it with two dry days, and tells it three times (the end of 7, the bar in 8, the chart in 9).

What puts it level or above:

1. **The choice is not undone.** Crouch's heroes keep the family. Here the count is "Forty-one", the pillow goes out in the car, and the man she saved says "I was in no danger, and I'll say so to anyone." That is harder than either Crouch ending and it is the reason to read the book.
2. **Fear for the marriage.** Crouch makes you afraid for a marriage by showing you one good evening in a kitchen before he takes it. Chapter 1 does exactly that with the oven door. Chapter 11 goes past him: a wife who has known the minute for twelve years and never asked, "Because you'd have told me."
3. **Chapter ends.** Nine of seventeen end on a changed situation (1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 11, 12, 13). That is Crouch's rate. The soft ones are 8, 10 and 16.

**Where the reader is afraid, and of what.** Of Lesca's foot on the first stair, with Coralie waiting on the fourth (chapter 5): that is the most frightening page in the book, and it is frightening for Coralie, who does not know who is coming up. Of the pulse going down the chart (6). Of the taste of coins (7, 12). Of the fire door nobody comes through (14). I was never afraid of Larrère, and I was not afraid of Lesca after chapter 9.

**Where a Crouch reader puts it down.** After the first scene break of chapter 1, when a paralysed woman with a thing on her chest gives way to a postman. At the end of chapter 8. In the first half of chapter 11, on the morning of the flood, during the tape rehearsal. He would be wrong each time, but he would do it.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The double is disarmed by the middle of the book, and nothing replaces her. (Chapters 8 to 11. Structural.)**
"'I do this all week,' she said." Coralie pins the left wrist to the kitchen table, and with it the book's antagonist. Lesca's last word is "Friday", and her last threat is "On Friday night I'm coming across with you, and I won't be sent home", but for the five days between she is a voice in a steel room who cannot finish her sentences. She has become sympathetic (chapters 10 and 11 are hers) before she has finished being dangerous. A thriller needs the reader to believe on Thursday that she can win on Friday.
*Do:* give Lesca one uninvited seizure in daylight between the 6th and the 11th, without sleep and without the chair, and make it cost something that cannot be handed back. The place is the association's vote in chapter 9. Larrère holds "*Labat, Kévin.*" against the glass and Maylis goes silent: let the low voice say "Open" a second time, out of her mouth, with the boy on the ladder behind the car. Now Lesca is stronger than on Sunday, Kévin's six months are real, Larrère has heard her defy him, and Maylis has lost the mouth awake. The weak right hand ("That's as hard as I can") should also fail her once, visibly, at a job, before the night.

**2. The afternoon she could have opened the gate is settled by one word and no thought. (Chapter 12. Structural.)**
"'Either,' Maylis said. / 'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. 'That's how the vote left it.' / He was off the platform and down the steps before she could take it back. / She lifted the handle off." It is 5 in the afternoon, it is light, the handle is on, the man she is afraid of drowning is standing behind her above ground, and afterwards he sits in his van with the engine running while she walks over to his window. This is where the forty-one beds are lost. The book knows it (Madame Darrigrand says so in chapter 17) but the page does not make me feel why the hand comes off. I read it as the writer needing the gate shut.
*Do:* write it as hard as the 2:51 scenes, because it is the same paralysis in daylight. Give her the three breaths from the opening. Put her weight on the handle and let it not turn. Let her count Larrère's car, the vote, Jeannot's boots on her boards, the eighteen clicks she would have to make in front of both of them. Then "Either". The reader must see that she cannot, not that she does not.

**3. "You're the water" is under-proven, landed three times, and changes nothing. (Chapters 7, 8, 9, 14.)**
"It was only two days. Bordenave would have called that weather." So would I. The largest turn in the book rests on a dry Thursday and Friday, is guessed by Maylis on the trapdoor at the end of 7, shouted across a bar to a room that misunderstands it in 8, and laid out as a chart in 9. Then the plan built on it comes to nothing twice: she stays awake and Inguma comes anyway (the kitchen floor in 7 and Madame Etcheto in 12 had both told us it finds you anywhere), and she puts Lesca out and "The water kept coming."
*Do:* three things. Land it once: cut Maylis's reasoning at the end of chapter 7 so the bar is the first time the reader hears it. Prove it in scene: when Inguma takes Lesca at the kitchen table, let Coralie, with the wrist under her hands, hear the rain stop on the window. And tell the reader before the night when it is too late for putting Lesca out to help, so that Friday has one clear fight and not two blurred ones. Lesca can say it in the steel room on Tuesday, and it would be the first sentence about the water that comes out whole.

**4. Lesca is promised a bill and let off it. (Chapters 13 and 15.)**
"'The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies,' she said, in the voice she used for the prefecture. 'She has a ceremony at 11, and she will not be taking this seat.'" Chapters 2, 9 and 13 all say that the one Inguma cannot take home dies ("She knew what that cost. She had seen the card with her name on it."). She lets go, lands in the hall anyway, opens her own fingers and leaves on a line of committee wit. Then: "Bayonne has had you for three hours and found nothing." The body that was "colder each time" is fine. In a thriller a stated price is paid or traded, and I think the book means it to be traded: "Twelve. One, two. Somebody pulled a chair in to a table." But I cannot tell whose chair.
*Do:* make the trade the rule, and make it legible in one sentence. The place laid beside Castagnet is filled; it is that chair that is pulled in; Lesca hears where the sound comes from. Then she stands at the microphone knowing her seat was taken by a woman of ninety-four, and "Then it wasn't Inguma that came for her. It was you." is a fact she has already seen, not a thing she is told. Leave something wrong with the body as well. The hand that stays a fist would do.

**5. The second pair of hands is turned away for a reason that does not weigh enough. (Chapter 12.)**
"Kévin had said he would do the six months. She wasn't going to have him turn the handle that would send him to prison." Six months for a boy who has offered them, against forty-two beds and Coralie. No reader of thrillers accepts that sum, and it comes one page after the Hontaube telephone call has already removed two helpers by form.
*Do:* the real reason is already in the scene, two lines lower: "Coralie's working tonight, and she's short of seats." Cut the scruple. Make it a choice she knows she is making: a man at the handle, or a van at the care home. She sends the seats to Coralie. Then the three trips Kévin's van makes up the hill are paid for with the handle, and Marie-Jo's "He says you sent it" in chapter 17 carries that weight.

**6. On the day of the flood the book leaves the flood. (Chapter 11, first half.)**
"He looked at his sheet. He left the kiss on it." The rehearsal is funny and the eight strips of tape are needed ("The others have died since then, in those beds, of the things people die of."). But chapter 10 ends on Friday morning with the plan changed to "*before dark*", and the next hundred lines are a mother who will not sit down, a young man with a clipboard and a swept doorstep, in the village where nothing can happen tonight.
*Do:* cross-cut chapters 11 and 12. Maylis rings Hontaube; Lesca stands on the tape. Maylis fits the handle at 5; Coralie holds up the third sheet at 6:45. Maylis sets herself to stay awake; Lesca straightens her legs and turns onto her back. The two Fridays are the same day and the book has not once played them against each other at speed.

**7. One hand against the other, five times. (Chapters 3, 5, 8, 9, 13.)**
"They had one nose between them, so now neither of them could breathe." By the time of "It was one hand against the other. The one on the brake was the weaker, and it had got there first", the reader has watched the wrist, the stair post, the jaw and the mouth. The bingo version was played for a laugh ("Tooth"), which makes it harder to be frightened by the same wrestling in the van.
*Do:* cut one. The kitchen in chapter 9 can keep Coralie's weight on the wrist and lose the fingers and the nose. Let the handbrake be the only time the right hand does something clever.

**8. Larrère's folder is shown three times and is too small a lever for a thriller. (Chapters 6, 9, 12.)**
"'That's prison, for eels.'" A blank sheet, an undated letter and a car window are the whole human obstruction between the heroine and the gate, and in the end they obstruct nothing: what shuts the gate is her own word. His real card, that he rang from the Castagnet kitchen, is played for the reader and never against her.
*Do:* show the folder twice, not three times (the sheet against the glass in 9 goes, or becomes the scene in fault 1). And let him use the kitchen once as a threat, before the night: he has already asked twice what Maïté told her.

**9. The night of 2014 turns from a refrain into a recap. (Chapters 3, 4, 6, 10.)**
"Then Lesca told her about the night, in order." By chapter 10 the reader has had the council version, the file, the steel-room version and the file again, and has known since the opening that she looked down the track and rang. What chapter 10 adds is three things: she rang off after one ring, Coralie's car was in the yard, and a kitchen clock was ticking behind Larrère. They are the three that matter.
*Do:* keep the typed lines in chapter 6 only, where Maylis reads them as a job sheet. Cut chapter 4's telling to the date, the death and the nets. Then chapter 10 is the first time the reader stands on that platform since "It rang once", and the reflector is new.

**10. The first scene break loses the reader the first page won. (Chapter 1.)**
"'One. The water down there is mine. A duck comes down onto standing water. On Sunday night I had a hand's width of water on those fields.'" Page one is a woman who cannot move with a thing of leaves and rivets on her chest. Page three is a paragraph of drainage from a man in a van. Everything in the square is paid for later, but the reader does not know that yet, and the countdown (eighteen marks on a rake) is the only thread holding him.
*Do:* cut the square by a third. Keep the signature, the window that goes down a few centimetres, "I'll tell him" and "Put it in forty-two". Move "That gate lets water through both ways" to the platform in chapter 5, where the reader is looking at the gate.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The opening, and what it holds back.** "It rang once." Then the answer, ten chapters later: "His number rang once and nobody answered. She didn't wait for the second ring." And the reflector that goes on shining for four more minutes.
2. **Coralie and the phone (chapter 11).** "Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. 02:51." / "Why not?" / "Because you'd have told me." The minute on the clock radio, explained by the one person who was never asked. It is the best turn in the book.
3. **The dip (chapter 14).** "If she turned that handle, Jeannot drowned and her hand had done it. If she went on, whatever the river did behind her was the river's doing. She wanted it not to be her." And then the arithmetic that convicts her: "Maylis had given the bank ten minutes, and it had held for sixteen."
4. **Room twelve, from the corridor to the hall.** "'Did she answer you? Through the door?' / Coralie read out the next name." The pulse taken "for the full half minute". "Forty-one." Do not add a scream, a voice behind the door or a rescue attempt on the page.
5. **The confession and what follows it (chapter 15).** "Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him." The sentence going back through the square at walking pace, the man who claps, the medal handed over the tape, and Marie-Jo's chairs.
