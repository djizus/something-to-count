# Critic's report: Characters

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `22.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "It was twenty-five minutes on foot to that hatch. The bank had ten." (16) The sum is clear and has faces on both sides of it. What is missing is the keyholder's own stake in it: Coralie is under that bank and Maylis never once feels it on the page.
- **Tension: 7.** "The telephone began to ring in her hand." (17) Chapters 16 to 19 hold. From 9 to 14 the threat is a gauge reading in the last line of a chapter while the people do procedure.
- **Progression: 6.** "she no longer meant to wake in anybody's bed but her own" (17). Maylis's one large change of mind is reported in a subordinate clause.
- **Comedy: 8.** "'Opened what? There's a name over it. It reads that she opened him.'" (2) The people are funny by being themselves at a table.
- **Characters: 7.** Coralie is a 9: "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." (13) Lesca is an 8. Maylis is a 5: her part, eight times, is "'Either,' she said."
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Five,' he said. He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." (19)
- **Prose: 7.** "She priced the slip. It was a day old and worth a prosecutor, and the signature on it was not hers." (9) The verb appears fourteen times and stands where a thought should be.
- **The ending: 6.** "The door stayed shut, and Inguma did not come back." (20) That is the last the two women have of each other, two chapters before the book stops.
- **Clarity: 6.** "On Monday there was frost, and Maylis woke in Lesca's bed and drove Lesca's body, with Lesca awake in the right hand, for as long as Inguma took to count one cut of a verge." (14) Five chapters open by restating the rule, and the reader still has to keep two of every villager.

Would I have finished it unpaid: yes, from chapter 6. Before 6 I was reading on trust.
Would I press it on a friend: on the friend who loved the committee meetings in The Good Place. Not on a Crouch reader.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Above Crouch.** His wives are destinations: Daniela in Dark Matter exists to be got back to, and is given her one act of choosing in the last pages. Coralie chooses in chapter 13, against her own interest, with reasons from her trade, and again in 15 ("Because you'd have told me"), and again in 22. Dark Matter's other Jason steals the life and the wife; here the other woman tries exactly that, in writing ("and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it"), and the wife herself sends her down the stairs. That is better than Crouch.

**Below Crouch on one thing.** He makes a reader afraid for a marriage by having the husband want it on every page. Lesca wants Coralie and says so. Maylis, who has her, is never shown wanting her. The only scene in which Maylis's marriage is alive from her side is the two of them unable to look at each other outside room twelve (3).

**Below The Good Place.** Chidi's indecision is comic because we watch him suffer it: the stomach, the reasons, both answers visible. Maylis's "Either" is a blank, given flat. The Good Place also changes its people by putting two of them in a room for a whole episode. The two women here share five rooms, and after chapter 7 none lasts two pages. Where the book is level with it: the reversals that re-read people. The telephone in 15 turns eleven years of Coralie across a landing into a decision she made. The kitchen clock in 21 re-reads Larrère in every scene he has had.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The central choice is made in a clause, and Coralie is not in it. (19)**
"On foot it was one or the other, and she went to the man."
This is the mirror of Lesca's night and the thing the book exists for. Lesca is asked for twelve years which she was counting. Maylis leaves the woman she lives with under a bank with six minutes in it and goes four hundred metres the other way, and the text gives no reason, no cost, and does not name Coralie in the sentence. The reader cannot tell whether she chose Jeannot, obeyed five words that were Lesca's, or simply took the shorter distance. Write the dip from inside her. Coralie is on an open line a page earlier: keep her there. Let Maylis know what she is walking away from and go anyway, and say why to herself in her own terms. Everything in 22 then has a cause.

**2. The two women never finish with each other, and Lesca never sees the bill. (20, 22)**
"*Nil.*" (15) and "Then that one is mine, and I'll answer for it." (11)
In a fortnight Lesca opens Maylis's hand, signs her into a resignation, sends Jeannot to his tonne out of spite, writes over the mayor's head in her name, propositions her partner with her mouth, and says five words that put the river in room twelve. She answers for none of it. Their last meeting is the first page of 20, before the confession, before the letter is dated, before Coralie goes. Maylis there takes the blame herself ("I said both. That was mine. So was the van.") and the door shuts. Lesca never learns that Maylis lost the post and the bed; Maylis never learns that her nine rings made Lesca say "I drowned him". The relationship the book is built on does not pay. One more room, after both Saturdays, with the *Removed* column filled in. It is the structural change the ending needs.

**3. Coralie leaves on a charge the page does not support. (22)**
"You said both. I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it."
The reader has just watched Maylis let a telephone ring nine times, walk the track against an order, ask Coralie back at the home's door, and haul a man out of a hole by his collars. "Both" was the least passive thing she has said in the book. Coralie then says "I chose you, Maylis, over somebody who asked" and goes in the same scene. It reads as symmetry imposed: the one who decides gets the pillow, the one who dithers loses it, whatever she did last night. Coralie has two true reasons and uses neither: she wants Bayonne for herself, and Maylis left her in the water for Castagnet. Madame Darrigrand says the second ("fetching a man out of a dry hole"); Coralie must. Cut the ruling on "both".

**4. Maylis's wish for the other life is announced and cancelled off the page. (11, 16, 17)**
"'Have Friday,' Maylis said. 'I'll have yours.'" and "She thought Lesca hard and lucky, and it was the luck she wanted."
To want Lesca's Saturday she must leave her own body in a van with its eyes open, Coralie "thirty metres from the patch" and a man under a hatch. That is a monstrous wish from the woman Jeannot calls "the one straight thing on that dyke", and the book never makes her look at it. Coralie looks ("then there's me, as a distance") and is answered "You're here. I don't have to write you down." Then the wish is withdrawn in half a sentence in 17. Give the decision a scene in 12 in which she says it to Coralie and hears it said.

**5. Maylis is a tic for half the book. (1 to 10)**
"'Either,' she said." Twenty-two uses of the word; "priced" fourteen times.
Sarthou's "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade" (9) is the joke at full height, and the trouble is that he is right. The reader is never shown the two answers she is not giving or what each would cost. The book knows how: "The getting out was hers. Maylis could hear what that would be, said across a table to a woman in a tunic" (7), and "she was more afraid of her own stairs than she had been of the winch" (8). Those two sentences are the whole of her interior before chapter 16. Do it at the shopping pad in 1 and at "Is it here?" / "No" in 12. She first wants something in chapter 10 ("I want that"). That is too late for a lead.

**6. Lesca's confession is repaid by nightfall, and Larrère takes the weight. (20, 21)**
"He said if the gate stayed shut it made no odds where he was."
She says "I drowned him" at eleven. By two Marie-Jo has used her first name; by seven the widow has her up a ladder; at midnight "Coralie came in with the pillow under her arm." In between, the widow reveals that the shut order came from her own lover in her own kitchen, which moves the bad faith of that night onto him. And on the landing Lesca admits to trying to take the other Coralie's bed and to Madame Etcheto's death, and "Coralie asked nothing about the gate." She is softened. Keep the pillow; make one thing stay shut. The widow should not hand her the gutter the same day, or Coralie should ask about the woman in the other bed before she crosses.

**7. Arlette contradicts the premise. (10 against 7 and 11)**
"My daughter has never said that word... Not at four years old, when it was an apple or a pear. She says which."
Lesca: "I gave that word up at thirty-one." The two were one woman until June 2014, and the book's claim is that one night made the difference between them. A mother who remembers a decisive four-year-old makes them different from birth and the night irrelevant. It is the best line Arlette has and it has to change: she should remember the child who said it, and the winter it stopped.

**8. Lesca's village is cast in subordinate clauses. (13, 20)**
"In her own village she had sent that boy to prison for his nets and had then given him Maylis's job."
A mayor who jailed a boy and then hired him has a relationship worth a scene. Kévin in that village plugs in a loudspeaker and looks up "as he had once looked up at a lens". Monsieur Darrigrand gets his scene (6) and Sarthou his (15), and both are good, so the gap shows. Write the Kévin scene or take the prison out.

**9. Two Coralies, one voice, one verdict. (8, 11, 13)**
"I'd have this one again." / "I'd keep that one in the house, whoever she is." / "I'd have the one who answers."
Each Coralie prefers the other woman, in nearly the same sentence. Once it is a discovery; three times it is a diagram. And twelve years of running a home and living with a mayor have left the director with the care assistant's diction, gestures and jokes. Let the director give an order the assistant would not, and cut one of the three verdicts.

**10. The father is a standard, never a person. (throughout; 18)**
"Your father" nine times, from four mouths. "he did not look up from his numbers... She stopped at neither of them."
He is the root of both women: the key, the L, the four turns short, the scar. Everyone uses him against Maylis, and the book gives neither daughter one memory of him speaking. When Lesca walks past him at the table of the dead, nothing is at stake because nothing was established. One remembered sentence of his, early, in her head and not in Larrère's.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. Coralie and the telephone (15): "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." through "Because you'd have told me." And Lesca's answer to "which", twice: "I can't find which part of it turned." Do not resolve it.
2. The landing (13): Coralie refusing the woman she prefers, and the right hand going out towards the door.
3. The first sitting in the steel room (7): Lesca presiding beside an empty chair. "'Those in favour.' 'There's two of us.'"
4. Jeannot Castagnet whole: the numbered grievances, "And six. Bugger off." (16), "It's Jeannot. Jean's for the stone." (13), and getting down into the grave in his waders.
5. Kévin's hand: "Five" in the yard (19) and "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything as much." (22)
