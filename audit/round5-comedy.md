# Critic's report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `22.md`, in order, in full (57,500 words). Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Behind it are forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. … And out on the meadows there is one man." (8) The sum is stated in chapter 2 and never changes size until chapter 17. The stake that tightens in between is the comic-scale one, the undated resignation, and it is the better engine.
- **Tension: 6.** "She had gone over for the name of whoever could order it opened by day, and had come back with Guy Larrère's, twice. A day was gone on it." (10) From 10 to 15 the plan is a search for a signature the reader already knows will be refused.
- **Progression: 6.** "Inguma always came for the woman who had gone home with the other, and it stayed away only for as long as its count lasted." (18) That sentence is also in 8 and in 13. The book says its rules again each time it uses them.
- **Comedy: 7.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade.'" (9) Funny, regularly, in one register. The bar wants 8.
- **Characters: 8.** "'It's Jeannot. Jean's for the stone.'" (13)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Five,' he said. He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." (19) From 16 on it is clean every time. Before 16 it is mostly not attempted.
- **Prose: 7.** "She priced the slip. It was a day old and worth a prosecutor." (9) Good line, and the verb is on fourteen lines of the book.
- **The ending: 8.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (22)
- **Clarity: 6.** "Jeannot Castagnet had sent the mairie twelve. He had been dead for twelve years less eighteen days." (2) Chapter 2 asks a reader to hold two villages, two letter counts and two weathers before giving a reason to laugh or to care. After 7 the mechanics are clear because they are repeated.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. In doubt in 2 and 4, certain from 5.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On one who reads Penelope Fitzgerald or Magnus Mills, yes. On the reader the bar names, no. That reader waits until the end of chapter 1 for a laugh and until chapter 8, 30% in, for the book's comic premise to run for a day.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below The Good Place on comedy overall. Level with it on making the moral problem funny. Above it, in the last six chapters, on the switch.**

**Below: volume and range.** The Good Place lands a joke every few lines and changes kind as it goes: pun, slapstick, absurdity, insult, character. This book has one kind, deadpan literalism, and the narrator never once raises its voice to signal it. Nobody is loud but Jeannot. Chapters 2, 4, 11 and 15 have one or two laughs each. A reader who does not hear deadpan hears a solemn book about a flood.

**Below: each wrong in their own way.** Eleanor, Chidi, Tahani and Jason are four different jokes. Here one joke, the person who treats life as paperwork, is shared by Madame Ducasse, Sarthou, Tastet, the woman at Hontaube, the recording at Pau, the young man from the prefecture, Larrère and Lesca herself. The people a reader remembers are the ones with a joke of their own: Kévin and his fingers, Jeannot and his numbered points, Coralie and the oven, Monsieur Daguerre and "Here", Madame Etcheto and the care plan.

**Level: the ethical problem as comedy.** The Good Place makes the trolley problem funny by making it literal. This book makes it funny by putting it to a committee: "'Opened what? There's a name over it. It reads that she opened him.'" (2) The stone that cannot be given a verb, the motion "That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it" (6), and "That was Either, with the river in it" (22) are the dilemma itself, played as procedure, and none of them makes it small.

**Above: the cost.** Nobody in The Good Place stays dead. Madame Etcheto does, and Monsieur Daguerre still calls "Here" at the prefect's date in the next chapter. The show never had to keep a joke alive over a real body. Chapters 19 and 20 do.

**Against Crouch.** He is not funny, so the comparison is speed. Dark Matter has its premise in play inside thirty pages. Here the first crossing is at 21% (chapter 6, twenty minutes long) and the first full day in the wrong body at 30% (chapter 8). Chapter 8 is where the book becomes funny in the way only this book can be.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

### 1. The farce is loaded over three chapters and never fired (7, 8, 13). Structural.

> "'No,' Lesca said. 'There's nothing in it for you to mind. I look at that man because I can't believe my luck in him.'" (13)

By the loto the village has decided, in writing, on Lafitte's score pad, that the commune's girl thinks she is the mayor and is after Castagnet: "That's the pot explained." Larrère warns her off the Castagnet house, and he is the one with a reason to want it empty. That is full farce geometry: a wrong belief, held in public, lying across a real secret. Then nobody acts on it. Coralie is talked out of it in one line. Maïté never believes it. The best consequence in the book happens offstage and is reported by the man it happened to: "He sat at home last night like a dog in a doorway, and nobody could get near the house." (8)

**Do:** stage that Sunday night. Jeannot stays up off the meadows to guard his wife from Maylis, and so keeps Larrère sitting in the back lane with his lamps off. The village's wrong idea then does real damage to the true affair, the reader sees the lane a chapter before Maylis understands it, and the middle gets a comic scene with a consequence. Then let Coralie believe the list for the length of the landing scene in 13 before Lesca answers her.

### 2. The mayor is not funny until chapter 6, and chapter 4 reveals what chapter 2 already told (2, 4). Structural.

> "It had rained there all that day, the first of the eighteen days of rain that were to come before the flood, and neither woman knew it." (2, last line)

> "That village had not been spared December 2014. It had not had it yet." (4, last line)

Maylis is a comic character on her first page: *Either*, the rake, the postman. Lesca's comic nature, that she has a motion for everything, is first shown at "A motion was the one form she had for a thing that had to be decided" (6). Until then her chapters are frost, a stone, a widow and a file. So the first five chapters alternate funny and cold, and the cold ones carry the mechanics. Chapter 4 then closes on a deduction the narrator handed over two chapters earlier.

**Do:** cut the last paragraph of 2 so that 4 owns its reveal, or keep it and cut the gauge-file scene of 4. Bring Lesca's tic into 2: have her deal with the night's visitation as she deals with everything, on a sheet with a heading. Run 2 and 4 short enough that she is in Maylis's bed by the end of 4.

### 3. Coralie is the wit of the book and is allowed to be witty once (1, then 22). Structural.

> "'I don't cook Either,' she said. 'There's no recipe for it. Soup or duck.'" (1)

> "'I've waited a long time to be asked for one thing in this house.'" (7) "'I've waited long enough to know it when I see it.'" (12) "'I've asked you one question for sixteen years and drawn you boxes for it.'" (22)

She does Larrère into the oven in chapter 1 and it is the first real laugh. She tries it again in 22 and cannot. Between them she has twenty chapters of one reproach, said in 6, 7, 8, 12, 13, 17 and 22. The aborted "I'll be brief. The—" is a rule of three with only two terms.

**Do:** give her the oven once in the middle. In 9, the morning after the soup, she does the one she had soup with, to Maylis, in the mayor's voice. It is funny, it makes Maylis jealous of herself, and it makes the failure in 22 the third time and not the second. Pay for it by cutting the reproach speeches in 7 and 12 down to their best line each ("then there's me, as a distance" stays).

### 4. One comic engine drives eight people (throughout).

> "'There's nothing here that lets me un-receive it. There's no column.'" (12)
> "'I cannot file a forecast.'" (8)
> "'I have Either,' Tastet said, gently. 'It's written, Madame le maire.'" (10)
> "'I've got a tray for each, and a book.'" (17)
> "'I've nothing on the sheet for this.'" (20)

Each is good. Together they are one joke with five mouths, and by the Hontaube call the reader is ahead of it. The joke is the book's subject, paper against deed, so it stays. But it belongs to Ducasse and Sarthou.

**Do:** drive the others by what they want and let the paperwork go. Tastet's engine is already written and it is fear: "as a man tries a door he hopes is locked" (11). Play him on that alone. Let the woman at Hontaube be eager and useless and no clerk; "They say they'd come" is the start of that. The young man from Pau has his own diction ("That was sort of the point") and should keep to it on the stage in 20.

### 5. The rules are said again every time they are used, and the restatement sits where a scene should start (8, 11, 12, 13, 18, 19, 20).

> "Inguma always came for the visitor and took her home. It stayed away only while it had counting left to do" (13)

> "The words were Lesca's. Maylis had been to the tonne twice already. At five on Friday morning she had rung the man and let it ring nine times…" (19, second paragraph)

The rules were given once, as comedy, in 7: three points, a vote, "'There's two of us.'" That is the legible version and the funny one. Each swap chapter then opens on a formula sentence that carries the rule inside a joke: "for as long as Inguma took to count a lake." After that the prose versions in 8, 13 and 18, the "could see each other and could not lie" clause in 11, 15 and 20, and the whole second paragraph of 12 are the same information with the timing taken out. The worst is 19: at the peak of the book the narrator stops for a paragraph to summarise 16 and 17.

**Do:** keep the formula openers. Cut every prose restatement after chapter 8. Cut the second paragraph of 19 entire, and the "He had come out of a dry cellar half an hour before, by the neck" sentence later in it.

### 6. Chapter 11 is a sketch with no consequence, and chapter 15 primes a prop it never pays (11, 15).

> "'She's there, so she isn't absent,' Guy Larrère said. 'Then she's prevented.'" (11)

> "A biscuit tin stood on the sideboard behind him. A Breton girl was printed on its square lid, and a thick rubber band went round it both ways." (15)

The sleeping chair in 11 starts well ("'That's sleepwalkers. She isn't walking.'") and ends when she wakes and strikes the vote out. Nothing is lost, so the scene stops; it does not build. Its steel-room half trades information. Chapter 15 is five scenes of five kinds before the one that matters, and the Bayonne visit shows a tin of notebooks, with a hand laid on the lid, that never comes back.

**Do:** in 11, let the vote stand. The bull loses its money while she sleeps, and she has to win it back in 15 against the same hands. In 15, pay the tin or cut the visit: the notebook for 2014 goes to the lieutenant on the stage in 20, or Sarthou is asked to carry the flag in two lines by telephone.

### 7. The narrator explains jokes that have landed (8, 10, 13, 16, 22).

> "'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything as much.'" (22)

Chapter 19 did this in eleven words and it is the best beat in the book. Chapter 22 says what it meant. Also:

- "Maylis could not put a bell on him. For as long as she had held a rake that man had talked down at her through the top of a car window… Nobody had ever told her that he could be stopped." (10) The left hand turning the dial back has already said it.
- "She said it to have the last word." (8)
- "Coralie had heard the two of them, one after the other, out of one mouth." (13) Her next line says it better: "from whichever of you is in."
- "She had last heard those two words from him across a grave." (16) It is unclear, and it stands between "Bugger off" and the end of the chapter.

**Do:** cut all five. In 22 keep Kévin's flight ("I didn't like it. You can see what's under you") and cut the hand.

### 8. Runners run past their payoff (10, 13, and throughout).

> "At home she would have been told both through the top of a car window." (10)

The car window is in 1, 3, 5, 8, 9, 17 and 22. It pays twice, when it goes "all the way, into the door" (8) and when "There was no car round him" (22). Chapter 10 then explains the contrast three times in one chapter. Lafitte's list is read out in 8 and read out again from the top in 13. "Your father" is said to Maylis thirteen times, by Larrère, the member from the high meadows, Jeannot, Arlette and Madame Etcheto. "Priced" is on fourteen lines; it pays in 12 ("and *paid*") and in 22 ("less than a morning's load for the tip") and is furniture between.

**Do:** in 10 keep "No window came down" and cut the other two. In 13 Lafitte reads only the new entries. Halve "priced" and "your father", keeping the first and last of each.

### 9. Three places where a joke costs a dark moment (14, 16, 21).

> "'It would have been right for both lines.'" (21)

Maïté's verb is *left*, which is the word on the card. That echo is the best re-reading in the book and works because nobody remarks on it. Lesca's reply then makes the left-and-right pun out loud, in the widow's kitchen, in the one scene that cannot take it.

> "'Your mayor. Monsieur Lesca. I've had him on the phone twice.'" (14)

The walk that measures her guilt at twenty-five minutes is stopped for a gag already used in 2 and 10 and used again in 18.

The third is the grave line in 16, fault 7.

**Do:** "It would have been true of both." Cut "Monsieur Lesca" from the weighbridge and keep "'There's a lake at the end of this road.'" End 16 on "She listened to all of it."

### 10. Four jokes and two facts a cold reader will not get (8, 13, 16, 22).

> "'It takes a small m,' she said. 'She'd know,' said Lafitte" (8)

It needs the French rule for titles, which the book does not give. "'Bronze, was it? Or yellow? I've been given both.'" (8) takes two readings to parse as two reports of one pot. "Those in receipt of an invitation may take it out from under the other three" (13) is explained a sentence too late. "Inguma had sat on her once more, in a chair in the hall" (22) is the only account of Maylis's last visit to the steel room, and I do not know when she slept. And Jeannot and Kévin both say "four winters" (9, 22) of a woman who wrote about the winch in 2011.

**Do:** one clause each, or cut.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The association at the winch (5), whole.** Four men and an umbrella on a platform built for one, the ewe of seventy-one, "Then the State has the key", "It wants grease", and then "Either" minuted as "The keyholder has no preference." It builds, tops itself and stings.
2. **Madame Etcheto's care plan (3), and its line through 17.** "She said it had been told twice and given two lots of stars, and it got through them early and came at one. … Staff to wait. Staff not to join in." And at the sandbags: "'Nineteen,' Madame Etcheto said. 'That's few. It counts quickly.'"
3. **Kévin's fingers.** The loader bucket in 3 ("'I'd fly'") and the yard in 19 ("'Five'"). Do not add a word to 19.
4. **Nine rings (16).** "'Who's dead?'" through "'And six. Bugger off.'" Lesca rang once; this is what ringing nine times gets you. It is the dilemma answered as a joke, and the reader's relief is the same as Maylis's.
5. **The square after the sentence (20).** The second loudspeaker saying "drowned him" late, the man who claps, Tastet turning the sheet over to see whether it goes on, "A child asked what the lady had done, and was told", the medal handed back over the tape, two hundred chairs, "'I can't sign a summons to nowhere.'"
