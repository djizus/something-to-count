# Round 5: the book read for its prose

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `22.md`, in order, in full. About 57,500 words in 108 scenes. Nothing else was opened.

Counts given below were made on the text. Where a count is approximate it says so.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Behind it are forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. [...] And out on the meadows there is one man." (8) The sum is exact and the reader can do it. The reader does not learn there is a date until the last line of 4.
- **Tension: 6.** "The telephone began to ring in her hand." (17) From 15 the book does not let go. From 9 to 14 the tension is carried by forms, files and who has the mouth, and the river is a gauge reading at the foot of a chapter.
- **Progression: 6.** "On Thursday night, in the steel room, Maylis had told Lesca that on the Friday of the flood she would stay sitting in the chair. If she managed it, she would wake in Lesca's dry bed" (12). A chapter that opens by briefing the reader again does not trust its own forward motion.
- **Comedy: 8.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade." (9)
- **Characters: 8.** "'I don't cook Either,' she said. 'There's no recipe for it. Soup or duck.'" (1)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 9.** "'Five,' he said. He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." (19)
- **Prose: 7.** At its best: "Then it went quickly, as things do that are filed." (16) At its worst: "Inguma always came for the woman who had gone home with the other", printed in 8, printed again in 18, and printed in 13 with one word changed.
- **The ending: 8.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (22)
- **Clarity: 5.** "But Lesca meant to stay sitting as well, and whichever of them woke first took the other home with her." (12)

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. I would have put it down once, somewhere between 9 and 12, and picked it up again. From 15 I could not have stopped.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who reads for sentences, today. On the reader the bar describes, not yet. I would have to tell them to hold on until the end of 4, and again through 9 to 14, and a book that needs that speech has not met the bar.

## 2. Against the comparisons

For prose: **above Crouch sentence for sentence, below him on how fast a sentence is understood. Level with The Good Place on the running joke that turns serious, below it on how quickly a joke lands. Net: below the bar.**

1. **Fear made from objects. Above.** Crouch's habit is the one-line paragraph and the fragment that names the feeling. This book never names fear and gets more of it: "The reflector burned in the lamps at the end of the track for four minutes more. Then the water came up the door to it and put it out." (16) Nothing in the comparison novels is written that well.
2. **The rule. Below.** Crouch states the rule of his device and then runs chapters on it. This book states its rule in 6, sets it out as three numbered points in 7, and then restates it in 8, 11 (twice), 12, 13, 15, 18 and 20, in nearly the same words. A reader who has understood feels talked down to. A reader who has not is not helped by the same sentence a fifth time.
3. **Tense. Below.** Crouch writes in the present, with the thing happening. This book has "had" 975 times, once in every 59 words, and by my count a quarter to a third of the narrative paragraphs in 4, 15 and 18 open in the pluperfect. Events are regularly reported after they are over. For a reader who came for a thriller, that is the single largest difference in feel.
4. **The joke that comes back as something else. Level, and in two places above.** "Here", "Five", "I'll be brief" and "still in the box" are each set as a joke, repeated as a joke, and returned once as something that is not one. The Good Place is best at exactly this. Monsieur Daguerre calling "Here" to the roof of a van in a flood is as good as that series gets.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. The briefing layer: the rule restated, the last scene recapped (8, 11, 12, 13, 15, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22)

> "Inguma always came for the woman who had gone home with the other, and took her back to her own bed. It stayed away only while it was counting what it had been given to count." (8)
>
> "Inguma always came for the visitor and took her home. It stayed away only while it had counting left to do" (13)
>
> "Inguma always came for the woman who had gone home with the other, and it stayed away only for as long as its count lasted." (18)
>
> "to where each could see the other and neither could lie" (11, and again in 15) / "where the two women shared one chair and, once on their feet, could see each other and could not lie" (20)
>
> "Lesca gave her the rule again, since it settled the matter." (11)

The rule is given well twice: in 6, when Lesca puts the twenty minutes in order, and in 7, as three points that Maylis prices one by one. After that it is boilerplate, and the wording is so close each time that the reader recognises a pasted paragraph. The sentence that opens 6, 8, 10, 13 and 14 already says who is in whose body and what Inguma is counting, in one line, with a joke in it. The paragraphs repeat what the refrain has just done.

The same layer supplies recaps of the previous scene: the first paragraph of 9, the second of 12, the first three of 17, the first of 21, the second of 22. The worst is in 19. Between "Go to the tonne first" and the telephone being answered there are over a hundred words retelling 16 and 17 ("At five on Friday morning she had rung the man and let it ring nine times [...] At dusk she had walked his track against an order"), while a telephone rings in her lap.

**What I would do.** Keep 6, 7 and the refrain. Delete the rule paragraphs in 8, 13 and 18, the clause in 11, 15 and 20, and "Lesca gave her the rule again". In 19 cut the third paragraph to its last two sentences: "Now the one woman who had ever opened that gate on him had taken her mouth to tell her not to. Not first." Cut the second paragraph of 12 to the one new fact, that Lesca is owed a handover. If a cold reader then loses the rule, the repair belongs in 7, once.

### 2. Payoffs happen off the page and are reported afterwards (9, 17)

> "On Tuesday the river had reached the line, and Pau had sent a yellow. Larrère had told her that the gate stayed shut and that the Port knew it had a river. He had been told the week before, through his own car window, that he would say exactly that." (17)
>
> "Maylis had tried Sarthou with Friday week before she signed his form. He had asked on what information, and she had had no name to give him that was not her own." (9)

In 8 Lesca tells Larrère to his face what he will say on Tuesday week. That is a planted prophecy. Its fulfilment is the first hard evidence in Maylis's village that the other woman knows what is coming, and the one moment at which Larrère might have believed it. It is given as backstory, in three pluperfect sentences, three days late. Tuesday 8 December, the day the river reaches the line, has no scene on Maylis's side at all. Chapter 15 spends that Tuesday with Lesca.

The warning to the gendarme is the same fault. The interview in Sarthou's kitchen is played without it, and it is then reported in two sentences. "Sarthou had believed the river sooner. He had laid sandbags on his own step that week, with the commune's sand, and had written the commune a bill for his labour" is a comic beat and a character beat, also reported.

**What I would do.** Write Tuesday the 8th on Maylis's side as a short scene: the yellow envelope, the car window, the sentence said aloud, and Maylis hearing it. Three hundred words. It pays off 8, it puts the river into the stretch where the tension sags, and it lets 17 open on Friday without catching up. Move the warning to Sarthou inside the interview in 9.

### 3. At the hinges the narrator tells the reader what it meant (16, 18, 19, 20)

> "She found that she did not want Maylis to have what she had carried for twelve years, and it was the first thing she had wanted for her." (18)
>
> "She had said both, and it had been a van's answer." (19)
>
> "She was ashamed, and she had no minute to put it in." (20)
>
> "She thought Lesca hard and lucky, and it was the luck she wanted." (16)
>
> "Maylis was going to open the gate, and she was going to open it for Coralie." (18)

For two hundred pages this narrator names objects and leaves feelings alone. At the four places where the book turns, it steps in and names the meaning. These are the sentences a reader should have been left to say to themselves. In 18 the reader already has everything: the hand going to the key, the list of what Lesca came for, "Not one of them would drive a van", and then the five words in Maylis's voice.

**What I would do.** In 18, cut the "She found that" sentence, so that "Not one of them would drive a van." is followed by "She let go." In 19, cut "She had said both, and it had been a van's answer." Coralie says it better in 22 ("That was Either, with the river in it"), and her line is worth more if the narrator has not said it first. In 20, cut "She was ashamed, and she had no minute to put it in" and keep the sentence before it, which is the hardest in the book: "Lesca had gone over to stop a drowning, and she had moved it from a cellar to a bed." In 16, cut "She thought Lesca hard and lucky".

### 4. Two women, one "she", and one name used for both (8, 9, 10, 12, 13, 14, 17, 18)

> "'Either,' Maylis said. / The word was hers, and she had got to her own mouth first. She shut the jaw on it and held." (8)
>
> "'I want that,' she said." (10)
>
> "Lesca knew that reach. She had made it herself on that platform, as a telephone went back into her pocket." (18)
>
> "'What are you sat here for, Lesca?'" (17, Jeannot to Maylis)

The narrator's convention, Maylis for one and Lesca for the other, is the only handle the reader has. In the shared-body scenes "she" changes owner inside a paragraph, and the limbs are "the right hand", "the mouth", "Maylis's legs" and "her left" by turns. From 9 onwards Jeannot calls Maylis "Lesca", so in 9, 12, 17 and 22 the mayor's name is spoken to the other woman. The beat that causes it is good ("He had left the little off. She heard it go."). Every later use pays for it.

**What I would do.** In any paragraph where both are in one body, never let "she" stand for both; repeat the name. It is eight or ten substitutions a chapter in 8, 10, 13, 14 and 18. In 10, "'I want that,' Maylis said." For Jeannot, keep the dropped "little" in 9 and let him use no name at all in 12 and 17. He is a man who would withhold one. His "Little Lesca" in 19 then lands harder.

### 5. Too many figures, and three pairs that look like mistakes (throughout; 2, 9, 15)

> "the first of the eighteen days of rain that were to come before the flood" against "that was the nineteenth day of rain" (both 2)
>
> "Four winters you've held that key and looked the other way" (9) against "I've had your name on every chit in that mairie since 2008" (9) and "In 2011. I wrote twice more." (1)
>
> "I've had that for twelve years." and, a few lines on, "I told myself for eleven years that it was a coincidence." (15)

The moral of the book is a sum, so its numbers are its instrument. There are too many for the reader to know which ones bear weight: forty letters, forty-one, forty-two, twelve letters, nineteen turns, four turns short, three minutes, eleven minutes, nine strings, eight trees, nineteen bags, ninety-one players, room twelve, sixteen years, seven years, six years. "Twelve years" appears 27 times and "eleven years" 15. The six figures that carry the choice (forty houses, forty-two beds, ten minutes, twenty-five minutes, one ring, nine rings) stand in a crowd.

Each of the three pairs above can be reconciled with effort. A reader will not make the effort. They will conclude that the author lost count, in a book about counting.

**What I would do.** Fix the three. Make 2 say "the first of nineteen". Make Jeannot say "Four winters of nets you've looked the other way". Give the landing no number after 6 ("since that February"), so that twelve belongs to the flood and eleven to the commemorations. Then, in any scene where one of the six weight-bearing figures appears, take the ornamental ones out.

### 6. Every scene shuts on a capper, and the sentences share one shape (throughout)

> "After the third letter she had bought grease and stopped writing." (1)
>
> "She had written it on the clipboard before she said it." (1)
>
> "The window went up." (1, 3, 9, 17)
>
> "Lafitte turned the pad over and dealt." (8)
>
> "He was reading before she had found the door handle with her left hand." (9)

Each of these is good. Of the 108 scene endings, about a quarter are a line of dialogue built as a last word, and most of the rest are a short flat sentence with one object in it. Three scenes end on a gauge reading. Inside scenes, paragraphs end the same way: the first scene of 1 has at least five paragraphs that close on a dry aside. 447 sentences open with "She", 197 with "He" and 71 with "Then", and the default sentence is two clauses joined by "and".

By 10 the reader hears the beat before it lands. It also flattens the difference between a comic scene and a grave one, since both end with the same click. Where the prose leaves the shape it is at its best: the winch in 8 ("It gave all at once, and the pawl missed its tooth, and Maylis let go with the right hand, since that give had put her into the rail a week before"), the vigil in 17, the run in 19.

**What I would do.** In each chapter, end one scene in the middle of an action, with no last word. Inside scenes, take the capper off every second paragraph that has one. The long sentence should not be saved for emergencies.

### 7. Everyone has the narrator's wit, in the same form (throughout)

> "That's Either again, with more words." (Coralie, 1)
>
> "That's the mairie." (Jeannot, 12)
>
> "That's the file." (Larrère, 17)
>
> "That's not a no." (Maylis, 11)
>
> "That's two answers." (Marie-Jo, 13)
>
> "That's what late is now." (Marie-Jo, 22)
>
> "That's the pot explained." (the owner's wife, 8)

Forty lines of dialogue open with "That's". Père Honoré (no contractions), Sarthou (the passive voice of a report), Jeannot (numbered points) and Arlette ("I'm not asking") each have a way of speaking. Underneath, most speakers retort the same way: they rename what has just been said and stop. In their short lines Coralie, Marie-Jo, Maïté and Arlette blur, and the same stage direction is handed round them: "with her back turned" for Arlette (4), Maïté (7) and Coralie (8, 22).

**What I would do.** Ration the renaming retort to Coralie, whose work in the book is to name Maylis's evasions. Marie-Jo counts; let her retorts be counts. Maïté withholds; let her say less. Give the back-turning to one woman.

### 8. The narrator stops to guide a visitor, and twice leaves both women to brief the reader (1, 2, 13)

> "the mairie, the building with the flag, where the village kept its council and its registers and opened the door two afternoons a week" (1), followed twelve lines later by "'The one with the flag. It's where a village keeps its mayor.'"
>
> "It went to the bull, which was a frame in the shape of a bull with fireworks tied along it, carried alight through the crowd by a man in a harness." (2)
>
> "There was a second Peyrebarthe [...] the first of the eighteen days of rain that were to come before the flood, and neither woman knew it." (2)
>
> "A mayor, it turned out, signed a great deal." (13)

Everywhere else the narrator is close and dry. In these it is a guide. In 1 the gloss is given by the narrator and then by the joke, and the joke is weaker for coming second. The bull is described nineteen chapters before it is lit, and described again, better, when it is ("under a frame of welded rod"). The last paragraph of 2 is in a voice the book uses nowhere else, and its final clause hands the reader the discovery that 4 ends on ("It had not had it yet.").

**What I would do.** Where a character's line carries the gloss, cut the narrator's. Describe the bull in 21 only. In 2, keep "Her name was Maylis Lesca too", which is the one briefing the reader needs, and end the chapter on Lesca's own observation, which is already there: "That morning she had woken under dry tiles to the sound of rain on a roof." Cut "it turned out".

### 9. Tics

- **"priced".** Fourteen times for Maylis, three of them in 9. It is a signature at five uses and a tic at fourteen. Keep 1, 3, the handover in 12 ("at forty euros for the month") and the bag in 22.
- **"found that she".** "Maylis found that she could not look at Jeannot Castagnet" (7), "Lesca found that she was smiling at him" (8), "Lesca found that she had her right hand shut" (15), "She found that she did not want" (18), "found that she had brought the pad" (22). It puts a filter between the reader and the act. In 7 and 22 a body is acting ahead of a mind and the phrase is earned. Cut the other three.
- **"with his mouth a little open".** Larrère in 10, 15 and 21, building to "Nothing came." Lesca is given the same phrase in 14 ("with her mouth a little open"), which spends it. Reword 14.
- **Causal "since".** "since it was the one she used" (1), "since she could" (8), "since that was the hand that signed" (8), "since Lesca had ticked it" (8), "since it settled the matter" (11), "since nothing on wheels could get there" (22). About twenty in narration. It gives the narrator a clerk's voice, which suits Lesca's chapters and not Maylis's.
- **Measures.** "the width of" nine times, "at the height of" nine, "for as long as" thirteen. "A hand" is the book's unit and should stay. Halve the widths and heights. The oven door opened "the width of two fingers" (1, 22) borrows the phrase that belongs to Coralie's pulse-taking and blurs it.
- **"Then"** opens 71 sentences, a good share of them the last sentence of a paragraph.

### 10. Private lines and small slips

- **7.** "'That's one,' she said. 'It's free.'" and "'Then three's by the hour,' Maylis said." Maylis is pricing the rules. I had it on the second reading.
- **14.** "She would have liked not to have a twelfth of December at all. / On the night she would have to drive it." Two one-line paragraphs set for emphasis, and neither is clear. Drive what, in whose van?
- **19.** "He did not count to twelve." The seats of the minibus, presumably. Say so or cut it.
- **22.** "Maylis paced it, from the hatch to that door." She is standing on a pavement in front of Madame Darrigrand. In 10 the same verb is literal ("She paced it without meaning to. It was four steps"). Use "reckoned".
- **17.** Kévin asks "The other one. Has she a night like this?" He was last heard on the subject in 7, saying "Nobody comes home with you." He has been converted between chapters.
- **2.** "She read it through to the T, and went in." Good once the reader sees it is the last letter of CASTAGNET. Many will not.
- **20.** "Monsieur le préfet" is the only French form of that title in the book, against "the prefect" everywhere else. If it is meant, it needs a second instance. If not, change it.
- **Otherwise the copy is clean.** British spelling throughout, with no American forms. No semicolons. Colons only in clock times. Three dashes, all for speech cut off. "Telephone" in narration, and "phone" only in three young or outside mouths. "Sat" and "stood" as participles only in speech.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Lesca's night in 16, whole**, including the unnamed "she" that lets Maylis and the reader stand in it. "They lit the rain. At the far end they lit the back of a white van, and low on its left-hand door a round reflector, screwed on crooked, burning red."
2. **19 from 03:31 to the end.** Kévin's five. Nine open, ten open, eleven open, twelve shut. The hatbox on the wardrobe. The hall with three rows of chairs and the urn on. "'Forty-one,' Coralie said."
3. **The refrain that opens 6, 8, 10, 13 and 14.** "On Monday it rained, and Lesca woke in Maylis's bed and drove Maylis's body, with Maylis awake in the right hand, for as long as Inguma took to count a lake." One sentence gives the weather, the world, the driver, the passenger and the clock. It is the reason the paragraphs in fault 1 can go.
4. **The two set pieces of procedure.** Seven members and one keyholder at the winch in 5 ("'It wants grease,' he said. 'It's had grease since 2013.'"), with "through the gap" eight times. The eleven untimed minutes in 10: "At four minutes he glanced at the timer, and went on with more confidence."
5. **The returns, none of which is explained.** Coralie's oven-door voice in 1 and its failure in 22 ("'I'll be brief,' she said [...] 'The—'"). Maïté's verb in 21 landing on the card from 4: "It was *left*. He was left out there." Larrère's "I'll be brief" answered by "Nothing came." "Still in the box" retired for "gone for a walk". The sentence in 20 going back through six thousand people "at a walk, row by row".
