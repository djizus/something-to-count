# Round 5 — Reader

Lens: an ordinary reader with no special knowledge of anything in the book. Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `22.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else opened but the brief.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes — 7.5.** They are plain and human once stated: "Behind it were forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." (2). They are recited as a sum for fourteen chapters before I am made to see them.
- **Tension — 6.** Two books. Chapters 16 to 19 are a 9: "The reflector burned in the lamps at the end of the track for four minutes more. Then the water came up the door to it and put it out." (16). Chapters 9 to 14 are a 4: "'The form for the road signs,' she said." (12).
- **Progression — 5.5.** The book says it itself: "She had gone over for the name of whoever could order it opened by day, and had come back with Guy Larrère's, twice. A day was gone on it." (10). Between chapters 8 and 15 the only thing that advances is the gauge reading in each chapter's last line.
- **Comedy — 7.** Funny in procedures: "There's nothing here that lets me un-receive it. There's no column." (12). Rarely funny in a way a reader in a hurry would catch.
- **Characters — 8.** Coralie carries it: "I've had five answers with one soup, and one of them was an instruction. … I'd have this one again." (8).
- **The switch between dark and comic — 8.5.** In the flooded corridor: "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'" (19). And Kévin's "'Five,' he said. He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." (19).
- **Prose — 7.** Clean and consistent. It withholds by habit, and the reader pays for it: "She read it through to the T, and went in." (2).
- **The ending — 8.** It costs: "She heard the passage, and the front door, and the car door shut on a pillow." (22).
- **Clarity — 5.** The book has to re-explain its own rule seven times, and the re-explanations are themselves hard: "But Lesca meant to stay sitting as well, and whichever of them woke first took the other home with her." (12).

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes, but I put it down once, at chapter 12, and came back out of loyalty to Coralie, not out of fear of the river.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who reads slowly and likes village novels, yes, with the words "it takes off at chapter 15". On the friend who reads Blake Crouch, no. They would be out by the end of chapter 1 and certainly by chapter 9.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below the bar for the reader the bar names; above it in the last third.**

1. **Crouch demands nothing of the reader; this book demands close reading on every page.** In *Dark Matter* the man is abducted within twenty pages and the rule of the box is one sentence I never need again. Here the premise is in play by the end of chapter 2, which is fast enough, but the rule of the crossing needs three numbered points (7) and six further recaps, the antagonist's reason for his order has to be assembled from three chapters, and a dozen pieces of paper have to be tracked. A Crouch reader does not do that work.
2. **Crouch makes the double dangerous to the last page; here the double stops being a threat at chapter 13.** "That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it" (6) is the best thriller sentence in the book. Coralie declines it on a landing with seven chapters to go, and after that neither woman is a danger to the other. What replaces it is better than Crouch — Coralie prefers "the one who answers" and says so — but it is a sadness, not a fear.
3. **The Good Place's reversals re-read everything before them; this book has two that do, and they are level with it.** "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." (15) re-reads every "No one came to tell him" and the hour of every haunting. "Under the time it said Castagnet, house." (19) re-reads the two bowls, the folder under the bread, the car in the lane. Those are as good as the comparison. Where it is below: The Good Place makes the trolley problem a joke the audience laughs at aloud; here the laugh is a nose-breath at a register. And where it is above both: neither comparison would have drowned the old woman in room twelve and then sat her, alive and moving her lips, in the front row of the next chapter.

### What I did not understand

- **Why Larrère orders the gate shut.** I can find three part-reasons (his unmetered pump, the association's meadows, the doctrine that "a gate is a door in a bank") and none he owns. See fault 2.
- **Why the flood of 2014 arrives in the other village twelve years late, to the centimetre.** The book raises it and drops it. See fault 5.
- **The loto of the dead.** Why Lesca's name is called, whether she was dying, and whether her refusing the chair is what put Madame Etcheto in it. See fault 6.
- **"Eighty-eight. Eight, eight."** (7). Called behind the steel wall. I never learned whose number it was.
- **Whether giving Inguma a count keeps it from coming or only from fetching.** See fault 3.
- **Which papers were in the chest pocket at the end.** "the two slips and her card" (19, 22). I had to go back to chapters 8 and 17 to name them.
- **What raising the gate does to Jeannot's water.** In chapter 1 I needed a second pass to see that raising drains the meadow, that he wants a hand of water left, and that this is what forty-one letters are about.

### Where I was bored, lost or skimming

- Chapter 1 from the registered letter to the trestles.
- Chapter 9, the mayor's office and Sarthou's kitchen.
- Chapter 11, the budget.
- Chapter 12, the pawl form, the signs form, the withdrawn letter.
- Chapter 14, a second committee minute and a walk I was told the result of.
- The council vote in chapter 15, until Coralie picks up the speech.

### Where I could not stop

- End of 4 ("It had not had it yet.") through 6.
- The soup and the fourth stair (8).
- The landing (13).
- From "Two fifty-one" (15) straight through to "Forty-one" (19), and on through the microphone (20). Five chapters at one sitting.

### What I will remember in a month

"Either" and the eggs. Seven men and an umbrella on a platform built for one. The reflector going out. "Who's dead?" on the ninth ring, and "And six. Bugger off." Kévin wanting six fingers. Monsieur Daguerre calling "Here." "And I drowned him" arriving late from the second loudspeaker, and one man clapping. Jeannot getting down into the grave in his waders.

I will not remember the pink slip, the pump, the signs, the merger with Hontaube, or any of the seven meetings but the one at the winch.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The middle third runs on paper, not on peril. (9–12, 14)**
"He blotted the sheet and laid it in the folder, on the slip." (9). "'The form for the road signs,' she said." (12).
After chapter 8 the plan is "get an order signed", and for five chapters the scenes are an undated resignation, a pawl order, a signs form, a withdrawn letter, a register column and a budget line. The river rises only in each chapter's closing sentence. There are six crossings between the two villages (6, 8, 10, 13, 14, 18) and by the fourth the pattern is known: she says something out of character, the village writes it down, Inguma fetches her. Nobody is in danger in these chapters, and I stopped being afraid of anything.
*Do:* take six chapters down to four. Keep one full day each way (8 and 10) and the loto (13). Cut chapter 14 as a chapter: move the laugh in the minute into chapter 10's day so that chapter 15's charge sheet still stands, and keep the timed walk as half a page inside 15. Merge 11 and 12 around the two scenes that matter, the steel-room bargain and Coralie reading the handover. That brings the telephone at 02:51 to the midpoint, where a reader who came for a thriller needs it.

**2. I cannot say in one sentence why the antagonist gives his order. (9, 12, 17)**
"With the gate shut his pump stayed dry, and she priced a shut gate, to him, at a summer of water he did not pay for." (9). Then: "Now he was lifting his own pump out of the river's way." (12). Then: "A gate is a door in a bank. You don't open it because the house is full." (17).
The whole climax turns on "it stays shut". The pump motive is given in a sentence I had to read three times, and then he lifts the pump onto blocks, which removes it. What is left is a doctrine and a folder. In Lesca's 2014 he gave the same order and I am given no reason at all.
*Do:* give him one reason, in his own mouth, that an ordinary reader can repeat: it is his land the river goes onto, and he will not be the president who flooded three hundred hectares on the word of the woman who rakes his parking space. If the pump stays, let Jeannot say what it is in plain words at the counter. If it does not earn that, cut it.

**3. The rule of the crossing wobbles, and is restated seven times because the book knows it. (3, 7, 8, 9, 11, 12, 13, 18, 20)**
Lesca: "You'll come at your hour and see me sat. Then you'll count every load of gravel…" (8). Maylis, the next night: "You'll count the squares on every card Marie-Jo has printed for the loto. Each card, each square. You're not to come till you've done." (9) — and yet she is in the chair that night and in Lesca's bed by morning.
Madame Etcheto's count keeps the thing from coming. Lesca's lets it come and delays the fetching. Maylis uses Etcheto's wording and gets Lesca's result. I spent chapter 10 wondering how she had got in. The line "Inguma always came for the woman who had gone home with the other" appears in near-identical words in 8, 13 and 18.
*Do:* one wording, used by everyone who means to cross. Then delete the recaps in 8, 12, 13 and 18. If the rule is right the first time, the chapter openers ("woke in Maylis's bed and drove Maylis's body") are enough to place me.

**4. Maylis's wanting is told, and her giving it up is one sentence of narration. (10, 11, 16, 17)**
"At five that morning a man had sworn at her down a telephone, alive, and she no longer meant to wake in anybody's bed but her own." (17).
The book's own question is "What do you want, Maylis?". Its one answer before the last chapter is "I want that" (10), over a medal and a register. In chapter 11 she will abandon her gate, her village and a drowning man for a sash. I did not believe she wanted it that much, so I was never afraid she would do it, and when she changes her mind it happens between chapters. In *Dark Matter* the wrong life is tempting on the page.
*Do:* let her say what she is taking, to Lesca's face, in the room where neither can lie, and make it bigger than a desk: people stand up for her, a thing asked is done by evening, nobody mentions her father. Then put the renunciation in scene. It is already there, in chapter 16, on the ninth ring. Let her hear herself decide.

**5. The book asks why the flood crossed over, and then stops asking. (6, 7)**
"'Then it had rained two days before you did anything.' … 'Then it's the winch.' … 'I want somebody found to blame.' / 'We had a dry winter here in 2014,' Maylis said. '… Nobody was to blame for a dry one.'" (7).
Lesca's "It's mine" is a good wrong answer and Maylis's arithmetic kills it in four lines. After that the same river arrives twelve years late, day for day, and no one wonders again. A reader of the comparison books expects a rule, even a silly one. I kept waiting for the lamps, the rib, or the bed to turn out to be the cause.
*Do:* either let "nobody was to blame" be the answer and have Lesca accept it aloud, late, as part of what she puts down at the microphone; or give the rib and the middle of the bed one more plain sentence so that Madame Etcheto's "You've been lying where it sits since Monday" (3) is understood as the cause it seems to be.

**6. The loto of the dead is the book's strangest image and I could not read it. (7, 15, 18, 20)**
"One chair stood empty, at a place laid for whoever died that night." (15). "'Lesca, Maylis,' the voice called…" (18). "'Twelve. One, two.' / A chair was pulled in to a table. / They both knew whose it was." (20).
They knew. I guessed. Was Lesca dying because she sat on Inguma? Did her sending "apologies" leave the chair for room twelve? That is the difference between an accident and a trade, and the book's moral weight rests on which. Lesca says she "moved it from a cellar to a bed", which suggests a trade, but the van in the dip suggests an accident. And "Eighty-eight" in chapter 7 is never placed.
*Do:* decide which it is and give it one sentence in chapter 20. Place the eighty-eight or cut it.

**7. Chapter 1 loses the ordinary reader after its first page. (1)**
"'Then it's in the minutes. And it isn't the council's winch, it's the association's, and nobody has written to the association. I was in the room, yes. I was in the room as mayor.'"
The thing on the wardrobe is a first page a Crouch reader would envy. Then come a registered letter numbered 41, a winch with two owners, a duck hide, a tide gate, trestles for a loto, Saint Catherine's, and seven named people, in rain. The fact the plot needs most — the mayor and the president are one man — goes by in that speech as a joke. The chapter ends on a woman looking at a wardrobe she has already looked at.
*Do:* cut the hall and the trestles from chapter 1 (the "still in the box" line can live in the café doorway, where it already is). Say the mayor-is-president fact once, flatly, in narration. End the chapter on the stone in her head, which is the only thing in it that points forward.

**8. Seven meetings. (2, 5, 6, 10, 11, 14, 15)**
"Marie-Jo turned the timer to three and stood it in front of him." (2).
The timer, "through the chair", the abstention, the minute: delightful in 2, perfect at the winch in 5, earned in 10 when he runs dry at eleven minutes. By the budget nap (11) and the second commemoration committee (14) I was reading the dialogue tags only. Chapter 20 ends with a summons to an eighth.
*Do:* four sittings: 2, 5, 10, 15. The dyke vote in 6 can lose its vote and keep Darrigrand's doorstep. The budget in 11 goes.

**9. Too many documents for a reader to hold. (8–17)**
"She pushed them back." / "with the two slips and her card." (19, 22).
In play at once: letter 41, the pink leaf, the yellow leaf, the undated resignation, the pawl order, the signs form with two motions on the back, the brigade complaint and its withdrawal, the handover on the shopping pad, Coralie's application, the typed order, the letter to the prefect, Sarthou's bill, letter 42. Each is a nice idea. Together they are a filing exercise, and the three that carry feeling — the pad, the undated letter, number 42 — are crowded by the rest.
*Do:* keep those three and Coralie's envelope. Let the signature that "has an office and a secretary" be discovered on one paper, not three.

**10. The last chapter pays ten debts in a row. (22)**
"The café door stood open as Maylis went by. Somebody inside said that the Lesca girl had gone for a walk."
Larrère and Sarthou, the tow, Kévin's flight, Arlette and the mare, Madame Darrigrand, the home, Maïté's bus, the café, Marie-Jo, Coralie, the priest, Jeannot. Twelve farewells at about fifteen lines each. Madame Darrigrand's "You were a kilometre off, fetching a man out of a dry hole" is the village's verdict and deserves the room that the tow and the bus fare are taking. Maïté leaving a husband who was just pulled out alive is a whole scene done in a suitcase.
*Do:* cut the tow and fold Kévin's "I wanted six" into the yard. Give Darrigrand and Coralie the space. Leave Jeannot and the second shovel exactly as they are.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 19, whole.** The clock times, "Both", the van in the dip, the key thrown back along the crown, "'Here.' / 'I know you are.'", Kévin's five fingers, room twelve, the list, "Forty-one."
2. **Lesca's night and its answer (16).** One ring and the reflector going out; then nine rings, "Who's dead?", six numbered points and "Bugger off."
3. **"Either", start to finish, and Coralie with it.** Eggs for Either, the two boxes ticked in pencil, two fingers on the wrist, "I'd have this one again", "I chose you, Maylis, over somebody who asked", "That was Either, with the river in it", the car door shut on a pillow.
4. **The microphone (20).** "I knew that he was." The second loudspeaker saying "drowned him" late, the man who claps, the child who is told, Marie-Jo's "Somebody has to stack them", and Madame Etcheto, alive, asking where it was last night.
5. **The winch inspection (5) and Jeannot Castagnet as written.** "It wants grease." / "It's had grease since 2013." His numbered points, "Jean's for the stone", letter 42 that "doesn't thank you", and the last line of the book.

## Verdict

Not yet at the bar. From chapter 15 to the end it is as good as the comparisons and in its cost better. Before that it asks a reader for patience and close attention that neither comparison ever asks, and for five chapters in the middle it gives paper in return. Shorten the middle, give the antagonist a reason I can repeat, and fix the rule once, and the reader the bar names will reach chapter 15. As it stands, that reader does not.
