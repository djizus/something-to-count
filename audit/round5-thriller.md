# Critic's report: Thriller

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `22.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

Verdict against the bar: **below Crouch as a thriller for the first three quarters, level with him from chapter 15 to chapter 19, and above him in what the replayed night turns out to cost.** A Crouch reader finishes it only if they get past chapter 14, and most of the faults below are about getting them there.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes, on credit earned by chapters 6 to 8. I would have skimmed 10, 12 and 14 to get there.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who watches The Good Place, yes. On a friend who reads Crouch, not as it stands: I would have to tell them to hold on until chapter 15, and that is the sentence a thriller cannot afford.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch on propulsion.**

- **Speed of premise.** Dark Matter has its man abducted in chapter one and waking in the wrong world by about page 30. Here the clock is set at the end of chapter 4 (10,000 words, 17% of the book) and the first crossing is the opening of chapter 6 (12,100 words, 21%). That is tolerable. What is not is chapter 1 after its first page: the thing comes down off the wardrobe in 400 words, and the next 2,300 are a registered letter, a rake and trestle tables. The only threat carried through that day is a stone with her name on it, mentioned once.
- **Chapter ends.** A Crouch chapter ends on a changed situation. Eleven of these do (3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 11, 16, 17, 18, 19). The rest end on a gauge reading or a still image: "the river stood a metre ten under her father's line" (10), "ninety under her father's line" (12), "the top of the wardrobe, and it was bare" (15). A gauge reading is a clock, and it is not an event.
- **The middle.** Crouch's middles escalate by failed attempts, each costing more than the last (the box worlds in Dark Matter, the resets in Recursion). This book has three attempts in a fortnight: tell the café (8), ask Pau (10), write to the prefect (13). The first backfires beautifully ("I'll go down Friday week because you've said it"). After the third, nobody tries anything from Sunday 6 to Friday 11.

**Its architecture is Dark Matter's**, and a Crouch reader will see it by chapter 6: a steel box from a physics experiment, two versions of one person split by a binary event, the high-status lonely one crossing to take the modest one's bed and spouse. The book has to win where it differs: the twelve-year lag that turns a memory into a forecast, two people in one body fighting over a hand, and the village.

**Level with Crouch on the fear for the marriage, by a different route.** Crouch makes you afraid of the wrong Jason in Daniela's bed because she does not know. Here Coralie knows by chapter 12 and refuses on the landing: "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." (13) That removes the thriller dread and replaces it with something Crouch does not have, a spouse with her own judgment. The trade is worth it, but the dread is gone from chapter 13 on.

**Above Crouch, and level with The Good Place, on the reversal.** Recursion ends on a clean reset and Dark Matter on an escape. This book replays the night with the better intention and moves the drowning "from a cellar to a bed". The cellar was dry, the bank held sixteen minutes and not ten, the gate was never turned. Then it refuses the comfort: "I'd sign your sum." "No." (20) And Coralie's telephone (15) re-reads the first sentence of the book the way the first-season turn re-reads The Good Place: 02:51 was never the demon's hour, it was the time of an unanswered call.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The safe solution is available to the heroine all Friday and she never tries it or is stopped from it. (17) Structural.**

The plan is stated in chapter 7: "That gate is opened on the Friday afternoon, on the ebb, in daylight, by order, with the meadows cleared and nobody on them." Everything it needs is planted. Maylis holds the key. Jeannot does not go down until dusk ("by five"). Arlette has said what the ponies take: "I'd want an hour's warning." (12) All that is missing is the order, and the only price of going without it is this: "If that gate moves tonight before I ring, the letter gets tomorrow's date." She loses the job on Sunday anyway.

So a thriller reader spends chapter 17 shouting at her to walk onto the platform at two in the afternoon. The book never shows her think of it. That reads as an oversight by the makers, where it should read as her failure.

Fix: put it on the page, one of two ways. Either she goes to the platform on the afternoon ebb and is physically prevented (the handle gone from its box, Larrère's two pump men on the dyke, the winch stopped at nineteen with one arm and nobody to help). Or she stands on the platform with the handle in her hand, the meadows empty, and does not turn it because nobody has told her to, and the reader watches the last "Either" happen. The second is the stronger: it makes the night her fault before it starts, and it is the scene Coralie's "That was Either, with the river in it" is waiting for.

**2. The middle third advances the flood in last lines only, and the best day of the countdown is skipped. (9 to 15, and 17) Structural.**

Chapters 9 to 15 are 17,700 words, 31% of the book. In them the river rises in closing sentences while the scenes are about an undated resignation, a letter that cannot be un-received, a road-signs form, a budget line and a laugh in a minute's silence. Then: "On Tuesday the river had reached the line, and Pau had sent a yellow. Larrère had told her that the gate stayed shut and that the Port knew it had a river. He had been told the week before, through his own car window, that he would say exactly that."

That Tuesday is the day the café's joke stops being funny. The woman they wrote up as "Madame le maire" called the level and the mayor's words a week ahead. It is the turn from farce to dread, and it is given as backstory.

Fix: stage Tuesday 8 December in Maylis's village. The river at the red line, the yellow in the tray, Larrère saying the predicted sentence through the window and hearing himself say it, Lafitte turning the score pad over, Sarthou filling bags. Pay for it by cutting the sleeping-chair sitting in chapter 11 and the Marie-Jo doorstep in chapter 14.

**3. Room twelve is found, not fought for. (19) Structural.**

The death is reported forty minutes after it is decided: "Twelve wouldn't open. The water was on the door, on that side, and I couldn't shift it, and I had the corridor full." That is Coralie's own choice between one and many, made at twenty past three, the same sum the whole book is about, and it happens off the page while Maylis pushes wheelchairs. The reader learns a name is missing only when the list has one line left.

Fix: when Maylis reaches the door at 03:19 and Coralie's "mouth moved, and shut", let it open: "Twelve won't open." Then both women, and the reader, carry that door for forty minutes of chairs. Nothing else in the sequence needs to change, and the scene in the room stays exactly as written.

**4. The theft has no teeth. (6, 7)**

The most frightening line in the book is the motion: "That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it." One chapter later the book disarms it: "Three. Inguma fetches. It comes for whichever of us has gone visiting and takes her home, always." From then on the reader knows the worst Lesca can do is borrow a day. The same applies when Maylis says "Have Friday. I'll have yours."

Fix: let permanence be possible and let one of them find it. The material is already there. Madame Etcheto gives it stars and sand; Arlette says "It wants more than that, a great deal, and small." A count that cannot be finished is a stay that does not end, with the other body lying eyes open in a hospital at Bayonne. If Lesca knows that count by chapter 13 and has not yet used it, the landing scene is frightening, and what she gives up in the van in chapter 18 is a life and not a visit.

**5. The choice at the centre is one sentence long. (19)**

"She had said both, and it had been a van's answer. On foot it was one or the other, and she went to the man." Six hundred metres back is the winch and forty houses; four hundred on is a hatch. This is the moment the book exists for, and it gets no reason and no weight. Lesca's letting go in chapter 18 is given a full page; Maylis's choice is given a clause. Nor does she ever put her hand to the winch the book has been loading since page three: "She had not turned it a centimetre", and its failure at nineteen is delivered second-hand in Jeannot's three points.

Fix: hold the clock at 02:59 for one paragraph in her terms. She prices things; let her price this and find it will not price. Then let the reader see which way her feet go before she knows. Keep the winch failing in Jeannot's hands, but let her hear it stop from the crown.

**6. The narrator spends chapter 4's ending in chapter 2.**

Chapter 2 closes: "There was a second Peyrebarthe, where no river had come up in 2014 and nobody had turned that handle against one. Jeannot Castagnet was alive in it, and had posted forty letters. It had rained there all that day, the first of the eighteen days of rain that were to come before the flood, and neither woman knew it." Chapter 4 closes on Lesca working out the same thing: "That village had not been spared December 2014. It had not had it yet." The second is the best act-one ending in the book and the reader has had it for two chapters.

Fix: cut the last paragraph of chapter 2 and end on "That morning she had woken under dry tiles to the sound of rain on a roof." The reader will get there half a step ahead of Lesca, which is the right distance.

**7. The swap chapters announce their own outcome, and the rules are recited instead of trusted. (8, 10, 13, 14; recaps in 8, 11, 12, 13, 18, 20)**

"On Monday it rained, and Lesca woke in Maylis's bed and drove Maylis's body, with Maylis awake in the right hand, for as long as Inguma took to count a lake." The same sentence, with the nouns changed, opens chapters 10, 13 and 14 ("awake in the right hand" appears five times). It tells the reader before the scene starts that the visit will end and roughly when. The fetching rule is then restated almost word for word in chapters 8, 13 and 18 ("Inguma always came for… It stayed away only…"), and the steel room's rule four times ("could see each other and could not lie").

Meanwhile the rule that matters at the climax is left soft. In chapter 3: "It hasn't come for you, girl. It comes to that bed." In chapter 17 it comes down on the roof of a van, after she has given it sandbags to count, and the reader is not sure whether it should have.

Fix: rules once, in chapter 7, in Lesca's three points. Open each swap chapter inside the borrowed body with no forecast of its length, so the fetch arrives as an interruption. Before the van, one line that the count delays the fetching and has never delayed the visit.

**8. Lesca's village has nothing at risk until chapter 15. (2, 4, 11, 14, first half of 15)**

Every other chapter is set where the flood is twelve years over. What the reader is asked to fear there is "a single item, which was whether the mayor should open her mouth on Saturday." The real danger in that house, that Coralie has known since the morning after, is withheld from the reader as well as from Lesca, so it cannot generate fear before it lands.

Fix: do not move the telephone. Seed one hard thing before it. In chapter 4, when Coralie says "I thought it was the shouting", let her look at her watch, or say the time. A reader who suspects Coralie knows something will read every one of her doorway scenes as a threat.

**9. Cliffhangers are settled in summary. (8 to 9, 20 to 21)**

Chapter 8 ends: "Coralie stood on the fourth one, waiting to be answered." Chapter 9, second paragraph: "She had used it to say that she would see to the range first, and she had seen to it until Coralie's light was out." Chapter 20 ends on Madame Etcheto's "Where was it?" and chapter 21 opens: "At the minibus Lesca had told Madame Etcheto that Inguma had been kept elsewhere all night."

Fix: play the stairs. It is the first time Maylis has her mouth back with the question still hanging, and it is thirty seconds of scene. Play the minibus answer, or cut the question.

**10. Lesca's ending is cheap beside Maylis's, and one fact is told four times to reach it. (15, 16, 20, 21)**

"Rang once" is revealed by Coralie (15), shown entire (16), confessed to the square (20) and confessed again to Maïté (21), where the only new thing is the verb. Then, an hour after "Last night I sent it to her", the pillow comes back across the landing. Lesca's other consequences are appointments after the last page: "Bayonne will take it from you on Monday at nine", "an extraordinary sitting on Tuesday at six". The woman who rang once ends the book with her wife and without her demon. The woman who rang nine times ends it with a spade.

That asymmetry may be the point. If it is, the pillow is too quick to carry it.

Fix: end chapter 21 on the lamp lit across the landing and the door open, and let the reader count the strokes with her. Or keep the pillow and take something else on the page: Tastet's seven names voted, the sash handed over. And cut the Maïté kitchen to the verb and the telephone on the wall.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie's telephone.** "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." And: "Nine minutes to three. I've had that for twelve years." (15) The best reveal in the book, and it is delivered as a nurse reads a chart.
2. **The last page of chapter 17.** The reflector seen a second time, "Nobody had given her this one", the fingers counted at 02:49, the weight on the roof, the telephone.
3. **What the night comes to.** The dry cellar with a stove lit, sixteen minutes and not ten, her own van across his track, "Forty-one." And room twelve as written: two fingers on the wrist for the half minute, the line through the last name, a hatbox on the wardrobe and nothing else.
4. **The confession and what follows it.** "Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him." The sentence going back through the crowd "at a walk", the man who claps, "Madame Castagnet will lay the wreath", and Marie-Jo's two hundred chairs.
5. **Kévin's hand.** "Five." in the yard (19) and "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything as much." (22)
