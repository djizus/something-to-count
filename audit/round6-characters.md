# Round 6 — Characters

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full, and the brief. Nothing else.

Verdict against the bar: not there. A 7 where the bar is 8. The partner is better than anything Crouch has written in that seat and the village is level with the comparison. The two leads are below it, and the book is the two leads.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes — 7.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." By the night itself both pans have faces. It stops at 7 because nobody either lead loves is ever in danger on the page: Coralie stands in water to the knee with a list.
- **Tension — 6.** "There's nothing here that lets me un-receive it. There's no column." That is what chapters 7 to 10 are tense about: a letter, a form, a sitting. The threat a Crouch reader came for, the other self in the bed, is finished at the end of 9 ("You'll go down those stairs") and never comes back.
- **Progression — 6.** "she no longer meant to wake in anybody's bed but her own." Maylis's want for the other life is born in 8, traded in 8 and withdrawn in a subordinate clause at the top of 12.
- **Comedy — 8.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade."
- **Characters — 7.** "'Have Friday,' Maylis said. 'I'll have yours.'" The lead gives away her bed and the woman in it in five words and the narration does not go in with her. Coralie and the village are 8 to 9. The leads are 6 to 7.
- **The switch between dark and comic — 8.** "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'" A running joke carried into the flood and still a joke, and worse for it.
- **Prose — 7.** "It came to less than a morning's load for the tip." Against that, four women leave rooms on the same sentence (fault 9).
- **The ending — 7.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." Maylis's ending is paid for on the page. Lesca's is invoiced and collected off it (fault 3).
- **Clarity — 6.** "On Wednesday it froze, and Maylis woke in Lesca's bed and drove Lesca's body." By the rules chapter 5 lays down, she can only have got there by staying seated on purpose and by setting Inguma the loto cards to count. The book shows neither and says neither.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. From the end of 4 I was not going to stop. Chapter 7 is where I would have put it down for the night.

**Press it on a friend?** On one who likes village comedy, yes. Not on the Crouch reader the bar names. That reader wants to be let inside the person the book ends on, and is not.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, by the margin of the two leads.**

**Above: Coralie against Daniela.** In Dark Matter the wife lives a month with the wrong Jason and does not know. She is what is fought over. Coralie knows in twenty minutes, with two fingers on a wrist ("Who is that?"). She is offered the better version of her partner, says so ("I'd have you tomorrow"), and sends her down the stairs on a principle taken from her own trade. She also wants something that has nothing to do with either woman. Crouch has nobody like her.

**Level: the village against The Good Place.** Each is wrong in their own way and funny by it. Jeannot numbers his grievances, Sarthou cannot file a forecast, Madame Ducasse has no column, Larrère will be brief, Madame Etcheto has reported a colleague to the care plan. The reversal also re-reads what came before it, as that series does: 02:51 turns out to be the minute of a telephone call, Coralie turns out to have had it for twelve years, and the landing in chapter 2 means something else.

**Below: the leads.** Crouch is crude about it. Jason tells you every third page what he stands to lose, so you are afraid for the marriage. The Good Place makes Chidi's indecision a stomach ache you watch. Maylis's "Either" is heard twenty-one times and entered once ("The getting out was hers", chapter 5). At her three decisions the narration stands outside her. Lesca is open: the motion with a bed in it, "could not have said what her breath did". So until chapter 11 the reader prefers Lesca, as Coralie does, in a book that ends on Maylis.

The lens questions, answered:

- **Distinct?** Yes, by trade language. One prices and one moves motions. Best in 5. They converge on the house voice by 15.
- **Wanted-for?** Lesca from chapter 4. Maylis not until 11.
- **Softened or excused?** Lesca, twice (faults 2 and 3).
- **Seen from outside?** Maylis, as a blank and not as a woman. No body is described anywhere, and "the commune's girl" belongs to the village, never to the narrator.
- **Who would be missed?** Jeannot, Madame Etcheto, Kévin, Monsieur Daguerre. Not Tastet, Père Honoré or Lafitte, who are a function and one line each.
- **Which relationship carries it?** Maylis and Coralie, on one question asked for sixteen years. It pays in 17, at a price the book blurs (fault 6).
- **Does the reader care who lives?** Jeannot after 11, Madame Etcheto after 12. Neither lead is ever at risk. The one moment, a name read off a card at the dead's loto, is over in four lines.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

### 1. Maylis decides three times and the narration is outside her each time (7 to 8, 8, 14)

- The crossing. Chapter 7 ends on Larrère's car in the lane. Chapter 8 opens: "Maylis woke in Lesca's bed and drove Lesca's body". The night she first stays seated on purpose does not exist.
- The trade. "'Have Friday,' Maylis said. 'I'll have yours.'"
- The choice. "She had said both. On foot it was one or the other, and she went to the man."

**What is wrong.** The third is the moral choice the book is built round, and its whole reason is half a clause one line earlier ("as the other one had"). Coralie is six hundred metres behind her under the bank and is not in the sentence. The second gives away sixteen years and is not weighed. Maylis prices lights, a grave and a packed bag, and never this. The first is missing. Lesca's equivalent decision gets all of chapter 4.

**What I would do.**
- Write Tuesday night at the end of 7. She has an undated resignation in a folder and has just spent Jeannot's good opinion. She stays in the chair and sets the count herself.
- In 8, at the box room she paces as "four steps", let her price what she is offering to swap.
- At the dip, give one paragraph in which the home is named and she turns her back on it.
- Consider keeping her want alive as far as the dyke. She crosses out the handover before breakfast on Friday, so in 13 only one woman wants the chair, and the scene the book has been building is a scuffle over an arm.

### 2. The landing is never raised between the two women (9 to 10)

Chapter 9: "Maylis went for her own mouth, to say that it was not she who asked. She could not get one word into it." Their next meeting, chapter 10, opens: "'He goes down on Friday because you told him not to,' Maylis said."

**What is wrong.** It is the worst thing done to Maylis in the book. Her body is walked to her partner's door at midnight and used to ask her into bed. She has a room where neither of them can lie, and she opens on the duck hide. Lesca is let off, and Maylis stops being a person for the length of that scene.

**What I would do.** Open the steel room in 10 on it. One question would do: would you have got in? Lesca cannot give a wrong answer in there.

### 3. Lesca's bill is presented and paid off the page, and her confession to Coralie is redacted (15 to 16)

"Bayonne will take it from you on Monday at nine." "For an extraordinary sitting on Tuesday at six, on one item, and the item is the mayor." Then the last sight of her: "Coralie came in with the pillow under her arm." Before it: "A fortnight ago I was in her bed for twenty minutes."

**What is wrong.**
- Eleven years of landing close thirteen hours after the microphone, on "an hour". Monday and Tuesday never happen. Lesca's last page is a win.
- She tells Coralie about the hand and leaves out the door, the midnight a week later. The book's whole charge against this woman is that she rang once and then told the true half for twelve years. Here she does it again and the narration does not mark it.

**What I would do.**
- Make her say the Saturday, and let Coralie's hour be over that.
- Either end 16 on the lamp lit across the landing and the door open, or keep the pillow and give Lesca one short scene after it: Monday at nine, or the Tuesday sitting with seven hands up.

### 4. Madame Etcheto alive is thrown away in a recap (15 to 16)

Chapter 15 ends: "'It never came last night,' she said. 'I said them, and I lay there for it till it was light. Where was it?'" Chapter 16 begins: "At the minibus Lesca had told Madame Etcheto that Inguma had been kept elsewhere all night. She had not said where, and the old woman had looked at her until the wheelchair was turned."

**What is wrong.** Lesca is face to face with the woman she drowned six hours earlier in the other village. It is the only place where either lead meets the cost while it is still breathing. It is told in two sentences of pluperfect.

**What I would do.** Play it, at the wheel of the chair. Madame Etcheto is the one person in either village who would understand the true answer.

### 5. The mower belongs to Maylis and is told by Marie-Jo in a Lesca scene (9)

"The lists closed at six, and I was in the sub-prefecture at ten to, with a gap at number three. I'd rung you at four and at five." Then, as narration: "In 2020 Maylis had been on the mower, with the telephone going in her pocket."

**What is wrong.** This is where "Either" first cost somebody something, and it is a telephone she let ring. The book has three telephones: this one, "It rang once", and "It rang nine times". It has hidden the first in an interval at a bar, in the other woman's head, as backstory in dialogue.

**What I would do.** Give it to Maylis, early, in 3 or 5, from inside: the mower, the pocket, the calls at four and at five. Then chapter 11 is a woman who did not answer a telephone learning to let one ring.

### 6. Coralie leaves for three reasons, so the night at the gate costs Maylis none of them cleanly (17)

"That was Either, with the river in it." "I want that floor. I've wanted it since March". "and I posted it for me."

**What is wrong.** The letter went into the box in chapter 9, before the flood, "for me". If she was going anyway, the gate cost Maylis nothing at home. If she was not, the letter is a prop. The true exchange is "I could. I'm not going to", and it is buried third.

**What I would do.** Make "Both" the cause, on the page. Bayonne is twenty minutes off. Have Coralie say she would have driven in from this house until that telephone call.

### 7. "Come back across the landing" arrives with nothing before it (12)

"'Come back across the landing.' / Coralie stood with the list in her hand. / 'Say it again.'"

**What is wrong.** Sixteen years of not asking end between two sandbags, with no beat that shows what unlocked it. The reader is where Coralie is and needs it said again. Then events cancel it, and 17 has to argue over whether it counted ("You did, and I said seven").

**What I would do.** Tie it to the nine rings. That morning she learned what happens when you go on asking. Give one sentence of her hearing the words before she says them.

### 8. The two women's relationship stops at the top of 15 and they never part

"Lesca had nothing to set against that, and set nothing." "The door stayed shut, and Inguma did not come back."

**What is wrong.** After this neither knows what the other did with it. Maylis never learns that "I let it go to nine" was said into a microphone five hours later. Lesca never learns that the other Coralie left. It is the only relationship in the book that no other book has. It gets about five pages across five meetings, and no last one.

**What I would do.** Let that scene end as a parting, in a room where neither can lie: each says what she will do next. Lesca already has the line and wastes it on the dead in 13: "a third sheet to put right".

### 9. Four women, one way of leaving a room

- Coralie: "Don't tell me what he was." "Don't give me the minutes." "Don't give me the measurement." "I'm not asking today."
- Arlette: "Don't put me in that." "I'm not asking why once," and "I don't want telling." "I'm not asking where you were," and "I've been told."
- Maïté: "Don't do it in the square." "Nor did you."

**What is wrong.** The men can be told apart by ear: numbered points, the file, "brief", "Here". Coralie, Arlette, Maïté and Marie-Jo share one rhythm: a refusal to be told, the last word, a door. Marie-Jo says of herself, "I'd have gone on at you for eleven years. I'd have brought it up at every loto", and never once goes on.

**What I would do.**
- Keep it for Arlette, whose it is.
- Let Marie-Jo talk.
- Take out two of Coralie's three "Don't give me".
- Leave Maïté her handbag and her silence until 16.

### 10. Nobody Lesca loves ever doubts her (4, 10, 13)

Père Honoré: "I baptise with water. I do not know where it has been." Coralie, told there is a second Peyrebarthe: "I thought it might be. It's twelve years late for anybody here."

**What is wrong.** In Maylis's village the crossing costs her a list on Lafitte's score pad, a folder, and a partner who resists properly ("I've nursed people who had two of themselves. They didn't write it down this neatly."). In Lesca's it costs nothing. A priest, a mother and a trained carer take a second village as they would a weather report. Lesca's Coralie has spent eleven years telling herself that 02:51 was a coincidence. She ought to be the hardest to convince and is the easiest.

**What I would do.** Let her disbelieve it in 10. She has grounds: she has twice sat by that bed with a telephone. "I'll tell them whatever name gets you an ambulance" (13) is the right note, and it comes three chapters late.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Coralie on the landing (9).** "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." And the end of it: "Then she can say so in the morning."
2. **Coralie and the call log (10).** "Because you'd have told me." "Nine minutes to three," and "I've had that for twelve years."
3. **Jeannot at five in the morning (11).** Nine rings, six points, "And six. Bugger off." and then "She listened to all of it."
4. **Madame Etcheto's line through three chapters.** The care plan in 3, the nineteen bags and "I'll have them put me back at ten" in 12, Coralie's two fingers on a dead wrist for the full half minute in 14.
5. **Kévin's hand.** "Five" in the yard in 14, and "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything as much" in 17.
