# Critic's report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** Countable from the second chapter and never changed: "Behind it were forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." (2)
- **Tension: 7.** All of it is in the last third. "02:59. The telephone on the seat began to ring. It said G. Larrère." (14) Nothing before chapter 11 is at that pitch.
- **Progression: 6.** The book tells the reader what a chapter will do before doing it: "On Saturday it rained, and Lesca woke in Maylis's bed and drove Maylis's body for twenty minutes, with Maylis awake in it and pulling at the right hand, since nobody had given Inguma anything to count." (4, first sentence)
- **Comedy: 6.5.** Six scenes are at 8. Between them it is one joke in one voice. The best of it: "She reported him. She said it had been told twice and given two lots of stars, and it got through them early and came at one." (3)
- **Characters: 7.** The secondary cast is funnier than the lead. "I've held hands for a living since I was nineteen. I know when one's holding and I know when one wants to be let go." (5)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** The darkest passage in the book is answered by its loudest joke, and the joke is the moral: she let it ring nine times, and got "And six. Bugger off." (11)
- **Prose: 7.** Plain and exact, and it points at its own callbacks. "It had been gone over every night for twelve years, and it was worn as smooth as a step." (11)
- **The ending: 7.5.** It costs on one side what it pays on the other: "Coralie came in with the pillow under her arm" (16) against "the car door shut on a pillow" (17).
- **Clarity: 5.** The book has to stop and audit its own mechanism: "Lesca sat on the edge of the bed and put the twenty minutes in order. Both women had been sitting in the one chair. Maylis had woken first, and had gone home to her own bed." (4) A joke that has to be worked out is not laughed at.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. I would have wavered in chapter 2 and again in chapter 4, and not after chapter 5.

**Pressed on a friend?** Not on the friend the bar describes. Someone who came for The Good Place would say it is not funny enough, soon enough. I would give it to a reader of dry village comedy today.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below The Good Place on comedy. Level with it on the switch and on what the running jokes are for.** Crouch has no comedy to compare; what matters there is that the comedy costs the book his speed in chapters 2 to 4 and repays it in chapter 14, where "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'" makes a flooded corridor worse than any Crouch set piece would be, because the reader has laughed at that man four times.

Three things put it where it is.

1. **Range.** The Good Place gives six people six different ways of being wrong out loud. Here eight people are wrong in one way: there is a form, and the form has no place for this. Larrère, Sarthou, Madame Ducasse, Tastet, the woman at Hontaube and the young man from Pau all make that joke. It is a good joke and the design is right, since the whole village evades through procedure what the two women evade through a word or a true sentence. But it is one register, and the only people who are loose, loud and wrong (the café table, Jeannot on the telephone) get perhaps six pages.
2. **The premise is not delivered as a joke.** The Good Place makes its rules funny in the act of stating them. This book states its rules straight in chapter 2, straight again in chapter 4, and only then funny, in chapter 5, when Lesca chairs a sitting of two with nobody in the chair. The engine the premise promises, two women in one body with a hand each, in front of people who know one of them, does not run until chapter 6 and stops after chapter 9.
3. **The running jokes are the argument.** This is level. "Either" becomes "Both" becomes "That was Either, with the river in it." "I'll be brief" becomes eleven unbelled minutes and then a man at a garden gate with nothing to say. "No one came to tell him" is re-read by a call log, and the same clock ticks down a telephone twelve years apart. That is the way The Good Place's reversals work, and the construction here is as tight.

**Does it make the moral problem funny without making it small?** In design, yes. The lever problem is run twice. The first woman pulls it and gets a medal. The second answers "Both", pulls a man by the collar out of a cellar that was "as dry as a kitchen", never touches the lever, and the village retires one joke about her and issues another. That is a comic structure and it does not shrink the death. But the laugh in it is only available afterwards, and it is given to a bereaved neighbour as a reproach ("fetching a man out of a dry hole"). In the moment the book is not funny about its problem; it is funny beside it.

**Where a joke weakens the dark:** once, in chapter 14 (fault 10). **Where the dark smothers a joke:** chapters 2, 4 and 10, where the comedy is rationed to one formal scene each and the rest is mechanism. **Is the switch clean?** Yes, and partly for a bad reason: the narrator's voice never rises, so it never has to come down. The amplitude is narrow. The reader is never let go far enough into laughter for the fall to be long.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The comic engine runs for three days and its biggest scene is cut into pieces. (9; also 6, 8)**
"Maylis got the mouth. It took all she had, and it was hers for three words. / 'I was thinking,' she said. / 'That's two answers,' Marie-Jo said. 'I waited six years for one.'"
This is the book's premise working at full strength: one mouth, two answers, a third person who has waited years for either. It lasts four lines and the scene ends. The loto has the whole cast in one room, a priest calling numbers, both women behind a bar with a hand each, and it is delivered as five separate sketches divided by section breaks, each of which stops instead of feeding the next. The physical comedy that chapter 8 found (the right hand turning the timer a centimetre, the left turning it back; the knees told to bend and told to stay) is absent from the bar, where "Maylis was awake in the right hand and got the use of it for cups" and nothing is done with cups.
*Do:* rebuild the loto as one unbroken scene. The bar is where the village brings its questions, and every one gets two answers out of one face. The right hand undoes at the trestle what the left does. Sarthou's announcement, Marie-Jo's 2020, Lafitte's suspicion and Jeannot's ham should stack, each making the next worse, until Inguma's cold arrives into a scene already out of control. Take the pages from chapters 2 and 4.

**2. The rules are told three times, and the funny telling comes last. (2, 4, 5)**
Chapter 4: "Lesca had still been sitting, so she had woken inside Maylis's body, in Maylis's village, and had been able to move it." Chapter 5: "Two. Whoever stays sitting goes with the one who wakes."
The second is a comic scene and does everything the first does. The first is a paragraph of minutes written by the narrator on the reader's behalf. Chapter 2 has the same fault at greater length: the gauge sheets, the envelope, the lake and the frost are each a careful inference, and the chapter has one funny scene in it (the verb for the stone). A reader who came for comedy has had two chapters of five that are mostly plumbing.
*Do:* cut the chapter 4 audit paragraph entirely and let the reader be as lost as Coralie until Lesca presides. Cut the closing gauge-sheet section of chapter 2 to its last two sentences. Let chapter 5's sitting be the only place the rules are stated.

**3. The chapter openings announce the turn, and Maylis's first choice happens between chapters. (4, 8)**
"On Wednesday it froze, and Maylis woke in Lesca's bed and drove Lesca's body, with Lesca awake in the right hand, for as long as Inguma took to count the squares on every card Marie-Jo had printed for the loto."
The woman who says "Either" waking up as mayor is the best comic reversal the book has, and it arrives as a headline. Worse, chapter 3 planted a joke for it: "'Either,' Maylis said. / Inguma had nothing to start on." She could not even choose what to give a demon to count. The payoff, the first night she does choose, gives it a job and stays seated, is not on the page. Laughter needs surprise and this formula removes it. As a refrain it works in chapters 6 and 9, where the reader already knows.
*Do:* end chapter 7 with Maylis saying the words and choosing the loto cards. Open chapter 8 on "Madame le maire is to get up." Open chapter 4 inside the bed, without the summary. Keep the formula for 6 and 9.

**4. The farce of misreading is left for the reader to decode, then dropped. (5, 6, 7)**
"'Now she wants him kept in,' the owner's wife said. / 'That's so we'd not think it,' said Lafitte."
The café has a theory about Maylis, the pot and Castagnet, and nobody ever says what it is. Larrère has another ("What did you say to her?"), which depends on an affair the reader has so far been given two bowls on a drainer and a suitcase for. Farce works when the audience is ahead of the characters. Here the audience is level with them or behind, so the window going "all the way, into the door" plays as a puzzle. Then the device is abandoned: Lafitte's list, headed "Madame le maire", gets two entries and "Lafitte turned the pad over and dealt." It is never read again. By the loto the village's story has changed to something else.
*Do:* have one person in the café say the theory in plain words. Show the mayor's car in the back lane in chapter 5, before the window scene, not at the end of 7. Bring the list back at the loto with a dozen entries and have it read aloud to the hall; it is the village's file on her, to set beside Larrère's folder and Jeannot's forty-one letters.

**5. Six officials, one joke. (6, 9, 12, 13, 15)**
"There's nothing here that lets me un-receive it. There's no column." / "I cannot file a forecast." / "I've got a tray for each, and a book." / "I've nothing on the sheet for this."
By chapter 13 the reader can supply the line before the character does. It tells most in Lesca's chapters (2, 4, 10, 13), whose comedy is entirely tables, timers and sheets, and those are the four chapters I least wanted to continue from.
*Do:* keep the joke for Larrère, Sarthou and Madame Ducasse, who own it. Make Tastet a coward, which he already is ("'It's the radiators,' he said, to the chair. 'I have a throat.'"), and let his comedy be fear, not procedure. Halve the young man from Pau. Give Lesca's village one loose-mouthed voice of its own, as Maylis's has the café.

**6. Maylis is the lead of a comic novel and is not funny in herself. (throughout)**
"Maylis priced the lights at a day for two arms and a tractor." (3) "It came to less than a morning's load for the tip." (17)
Her one comic habit besides the word is pricing things, and it is never turned on her or heard by anyone. She is the straight woman in every scene she is in; the laughs belong to Coralie, Jeannot, Kévin and Madame Etcheto. Lesca is funny (she calls "Those in favour" in a room of two). So the pair is lopsided, and the word that has to carry Maylis is said three times in chapter 5 alone, which blunts it before its good turns in 7 and 8.
*Do:* let her price aloud. Pricing is how she avoids choosing: she costs both options and stops. Said to Coralie, to Larrère, to Lesca, it makes her funny in speech and it is the same failing as "Either". Cut the breakfast "Either" in chapter 5; the two ticked boxes do it better.

**7. A room where two versions of one woman cannot lie, used only for silences. (5, 8, 10)**
"'Tell me your name is Guy Larrère.' / Maylis opened her mouth to oblige her, and nothing came out of it."
That is the device demonstrated, and it is the last laugh it produces. After it the rule yields "Lesca declined that" and "she kept them shut on the rest". Two people with one face who must tell each other the truth is a comic situation before it is a grave one, and the book goes straight to grave.
*Do:* in chapter 5, before the flood is mentioned, let the rule force something petty and true out of each about the other's life: the shed and the mower, the sash and the timer. Then the same rule closing Lesca's mouth in chapter 8 has something to fall from.

**8. The loto of the dead is described before it is staged. (10)**
"It was a loto, and the players were the village's dead. … One chair stood empty, at a place laid for whoever died that night."
The book's best dark-comic invention is introduced as a caption with its rule attached, in a paragraph, mid-scene. Three chapters later it is staged properly and Lesca declines her seat in the prefecture voice. By then the reader has been briefed and the hall is no surprise.
*Do:* in chapter 10 give only what chapter 8 gave: the voice behind the steel, a number called whole and then in parts, light under the wall, Maylis looking too long at something Lesca cannot see. Hold the hall, the father and the empty chair for 13.

**9. The narrator points at the callbacks. (14, 15, 16)**
"It buzzed, twice, against his chest, as it had on the dashboard of a tractor under the plane trees." / "She gave him his three minutes and more, with no bell on them." / "He had moved again and again that the stone be given a verb, and they wanted to see him get one."
The comedy of this book is recognition. Each of these sentences does the recognising for the reader. "'I'll be brief,' he said" followed by "Nothing came" needs no gloss.
*Do:* cut the pointing clause wherever a callback is labelled. There are about a dozen ("as Sarthou laid his", "as she had seen it done", "as he had once got into her bucket").

**10. One routine runs long with the water rising. (14)**
"'It's stopped,' he said. 'Your winch. I've three things to say about it. First, I counted, as you do, by the click…'"
Two lines earlier the water in the corridor "was at her knees. It had been at her shins" and a man is waiting to be carried. Jeannot, who has "no breath", then delivers a hundred-word numbered turn. It is the one place where the running gag outranks the situation.
*Do:* keep "It's up a hand. A hand." and "I put it in number six." Cut the first two points. He can number things again in chapter 17, on dry ground, where he does.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Room twelve's care plan** (3): "Staff to wait. Staff not to join in." and its return in Coralie's mouth in 17. The funniest thing in the book becomes the grief without a word changed.
2. **Nine rings** (11). The whole of Lesca's night, then Maylis lets the same number ring, and gets six numbered points ending "Bugger off." Do not shorten the rant.
3. **"I'll be brief."** The oven (1), eleven minutes with no bell and "there was less of it than he had thought" (8), the garden gate (16), the oven again and "The—" (17). All four.
4. **The winch inspection** (3), entire, from "In seventy-one it was a ewe" to "The keyholder has no preference."
5. **"Apologies received"** (13) and **"Twelve. One, two." / "A chair was pulled in to a table."** (15). Also Monsieur Daguerre's "Here" exactly as often as it now occurs.
