# Round 6: Prose

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full (44,981 words). Nothing else in the directory was opened. Counts below are from a word search of the seventeen chapters.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." (ch. 2) The stakes can be counted, which is their strength, and they arrive as a sum four chapters before anybody on the page is afraid of them.
- **Tension: 6.** "On Saturday it rained, and Lesca woke in Maylis's bed and drove Maylis's body for twenty minutes, with Maylis awake in it and pulling at the right hand" (ch. 4, first sentence). Chapter 3 ends on a hand that turns over by itself. This sentence answers it before the scene is played.
- **Progression: 6.** "The association resolved to seek an estimate." (ch. 3) The premise is fully in play at chapter 4, about 8,000 words in. Chapters 6, 8 and 9 then repeat one move, a day in the other woman's body, with the same opening sentence each time.
- **Comedy: 7.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade.'" (ch. 7) The jokes are built from procedure and they escalate properly. There are about eight scenes of it in seventeen chapters.
- **Characters: 7.** "'I don't cook Either,' she said. 'There's no recipe for it. Soup or duck.'" (ch. 1) Coralie is alive from her first line. The two women are told apart by their verbs: Maylis prices things, Lesca moves and minutes them.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Five,' he said. He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." (ch. 14) A joke set in a loader bucket in chapter 3 is paid in a flooded yard, with nothing changed but the place.
- **Prose: 7.** "The reflector burned in the lamps at the end of the track for four minutes more. Then the water came up the door to it and put it out." (ch. 11) Sentences like this are a 9. The book as a whole is a 7 because one sentence shape and a small set of gestures do all the work, and because the narrator explains its own climax.
- **The ending: 8.** "She heard the passage, and the front door, and the car door shut on a pillow." (ch. 17), against "Coralie came in with the pillow under her arm." (ch. 16). One pillow comes back and one leaves. It costs a death, a job, a medal and a marriage.
- **Clarity: 5.** "Then Inguma was on Lesca. It came unseen and sat on her breastbone, and the tiles over it were dry." (ch. 4) The reader is told which of two villages they are in by the state of a roof.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. From chapter 10 I could not stop. Chapter 2 and chapters 7 to 9 I read on trust.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who reads Penelope Fitzgerald or Claire Keegan, yes, today. On a friend who reads Blake Crouch, no. That friend stops in chapter 2.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Above Crouch on the sentence. Below Crouch on the page. Level with The Good Place on signature lines, below it on secondary voices.**

1. **Above: nothing is named that can be shown.** Crouch tells the reader what his narrator feels, in a one-line paragraph, and moves on. This book has no such line anywhere. Twelve years of a marriage are carried by a pillow, a landing four steps wide and a shopping pad with boxes on it. The manuscript has no exclamation marks, no semicolons, and three dashes, all for speech that breaks off. That discipline is better than the comparison.

2. **Below: Crouch's reader never reads a sentence twice.** His unit is the short paragraph that states exactly what has just changed. This book's unit is a flat declarative with the meaning left in an object. When the object is a dry roof tile, or a number that already means two other things, the meaning is lost and so is the reader. A Crouch reader forgives plain sentences. They do not forgive having to work out where they are.

3. **Level, then below: The Good Place lets you hear how each person is wrong in one line.** This book does that for six people: "Either", "I'll be brief", "It's in the minutes", "Here", "That has been stated", and Jeannot's numbered points. That is level. Below those six, the villagers share one idiom. Jeannot, Arlette, Madame Etcheto and Larrère all say "I'll not", and with the speech tags covered they cannot be told apart.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. The first crossing is given away in a subordinate clause (ch. 4; also 6, 8, 9)

> "On Saturday it rained, and Lesca woke in Maylis's bed and drove Maylis's body for twenty minutes, with Maylis awake in it and pulling at the right hand, since nobody had given Inguma anything to count."

Chapter 3 ends: "Under Coralie's fingers her right hand turned over, and she had not told it to." That is the best chapter ending in the first half. The next sentence the reader meets tells them who turned it, for how long, how it felt to the other woman and why it ended. The scene that follows is then a demonstration of something already known. This is the moment the premise comes into play, and it is the moment a Crouch chapter would keep the reader exactly as ignorant as the character.

The same sentence opens chapter 6, chapter 8 and the second half of chapter 9. From chapter 6 it works, because the reader knows the rule and the only new thing is what Inguma has been set to count ("a lake", "the squares on every card", "every signature Lesca had put to a paper as mayor"). In chapter 4 it costs the book its premise scene.

**Do:** cut the first sentence of chapter 4 and the summary half of its second paragraph. Open on "She lay on her back under a paper lampshade, with rain on the tiles." Let Lesca find out, at the reader's speed, that the room has two pillows and a rib that catches. Keep the refrain from chapter 6.

### 2. What crosses between the women is reported afterwards, not shown (ch. 3, 5, 6, 8)

> "'You owe the commune for a tank of diesel,' Lesca said, since the tow had come to her in the chair on Sunday night." (ch. 6)
> "With the tow, a list had come to her in the chair, and a telephone going on a mower." (ch. 8)
> "In the chair, before she stood up, she had been given one of Lesca's afternoons." (ch. 5)

Each woman receives the other's memories in the steel chair. That is the engine of the book. It is played in scene twice, in chapter 2 (the card that says *left*) and chapter 11 (the night at the gate), and both are the best pages in their halves. Everywhere else the narrator announces, at the moment a fact is needed, that it "had come to her in the chair". It reads as a patch each time.

The cost shows in chapter 8. Lesca asks Marie-Jo, "If you'd come to my kitchen and asked me to stand, and I'd said I'd think about it, and let the date go by. Would you be speaking to me now?" The reader has been given a list and a mower in one clause and cannot know what is being asked. The story behind it (the 2020 election, the unanswered telephone) is told in chapter 9, a chapter too late.

**Do:** play one more crossing in scene, in the chair in chapter 5, and put the tow and the mower into it. Then the later uses need no explanation at all, and the "had come to her" clauses can go.

### 3. Chapter 2 is where the book is hardest to read, and it is the chapter that decides whether it gets read (ch. 2)

> "She had turned that winch herself for years. It had opened her right palm once, on the night of the flood, and the palm had healed with a seam across it that she felt each time she closed her hand on a pen. It had never put her into the rail. Whoever was in the waterproof had broken a rib, and she had all of hers."

Chapter 2 has eight scenes in 3,187 words and one flashback inside another scene. It uses "had" 26 times per thousand words, half as much again as the book's average of 17. The reader is asked to build a second village, a flood, a council, a stone, a widow, a lake and a marriage, almost entirely in the pluperfect, before either woman has done anything. Every scene in it is needed later, so cutting is the wrong repair.

**Do:** change the tense, not the content. The other woman's afternoon at the winch should run as chapter 11 runs, in the simple past, as an event Lesca is inside, and only then be refused as hers. Cut the paragraph near the end beginning "She went back through each memory that had come to her in the night", which lists again what the chapter has shown. The last two lines ("That village had not been spared December 2014. It had not had it yet.") then land on a reader who is still fresh.

### 4. The reader is told which village they are in by the weather (ch. 4, 8, 13)

> "Then Inguma was on Lesca. It came unseen and sat on her breastbone, and the tiles over it were dry. The clock radio read 06:20. Coralie was coming round the bed in her dressing gown with the telephone in her hand, and Inguma got down. 'Twenty minutes,' this Coralie said."

This is the first return from one world to the other, and the pattern for every later one. The signals that Lesca is home are "the tiles over it were dry" and the word "this". Rain for one village and frost for the other is a good code, and a reader who has it will enjoy it. A reader who has not yet got it has nothing else. The naming rule (Maylis for one woman, Lesca for the other) does not help here, because both women are in the sentence.

**Do:** give each return one hard fact of place in its first sentence, in the narrator's flat manner: whose bed, which landing. "She was in her own bed, with one pillow on it, and the tiles over her were dry." The weather then confirms what the reader knows and does not have to carry it.

### 5. Forty means three things, and twelve means five (throughout)

> "'Behind it are forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds.' [...] 'Put it in forty-two,' Maylis said." (ch. 6, same scene)

"Forty" and its neighbours appear 63 times: forty houses, forty letters, the forty-first letter, letter forty-two, forty-two beds, forty-one carried out, forty euros a month. "Twelve" appears 53 times: twelve years, the twelfth of December, the twelfth commemoration, room twelve, twelve letters in the other village, and the number called for the dead. "Nineteen" (the turn where the winch sticks, the day of rain, the sandbags) is a rhyme and is heard as one. Forty is a collision. In a book whose plot is a sum, and whose reader is asked to hold that sum for sixteen chapters, a number has to identify one thing.

**Do:** leave the houses and the beds, which the speech needs. Move the letters to another range. Room twelve and the called number "Twelve. One, two." must stay together; the twelve letters in Lesca's village can become another figure at no cost.

### 6. The central question is asked four times and answered three (ch. 10, 11, 13, 16)

> "She had the sum. It was forty houses, forty-two beds, a car in the yard of the home and a van at the end of a track, with ten minutes against twenty-five. She went behind the sum for the woman who had made it, and found a handle, turning. She had the sum and nothing else." (ch. 10)
> "'I don't know. I had the sum. I had all of it at once, with my hand on the handle, and I can't find which part of it turned.'" (ch. 16)

Did she open the gate for forty houses or for Coralie? Coralie puts it in chapter 10 and forbids an answer. Lesca then tries both answers alone on the bed and finds she does not know. The narrator restates the question in chapter 11 ("nothing in what came told Maylis which of them turned the handle") and answers it for Maylis in chapter 13. When Maïté finally asks "Which were you counting?" in chapter 16, to the woman's face, in her own kitchen, the reader has heard "I don't know" already. The strongest asker gets the answer third-hand.

**Do:** keep Coralie's speech whole. Keep the two sentences Lesca says aloud on the bed. Cut the paragraph quoted above, and the sentence in chapter 11. Then "I don't know" is said once, to the widow.

### 7. The narrator explains the climax while it is happening (ch. 13, 14, 15)

> "Maylis had tried the man twice that day, and her plan now was the gate." (ch. 13)
> "Maylis was going to open the gate, and she was going to open it for Coralie." (ch. 13)
> "She had said both. On foot it was one or the other, and she went to the man." (ch. 14)
> "Lesca had gone over to stop a drowning, and she had moved it from a cellar to a bed." (ch. 15)

For twelve chapters the narrator states what is in the room and leaves the meaning to the reader. In the three chapters that matter most it starts handing the meaning over. The first two sentences tell the reader what a hand going to a belt means, one paragraph after the hand has gone to the belt. The third repeats the sentence before it. The fourth is the irony of the whole book, set out as a line to be quoted, directly after Maylis has delivered the same irony better in her own report ("Forty-one came out of the home. Madame Etcheto didn't.").

**Do:** cut all four. Nothing else needs to change around them.

### 8. Four villagers speak with one mouth (ch. 3 to 17)

> "'I'll not be the president who flooded three hundred hectares'" (Larrère, ch. 7)
> "'I'll not come up out of it for a figure.'" (Jeannot, ch. 12)
> "'I'll not sleep in a chair.'" (Madame Etcheto, ch. 12)
> "'I'll not hear it.'" (Arlette, ch. 15)

"I'll not", "I'd not", "you'll not" and "they'll not" appear eighteen times, spread over Jeannot, Arlette, Madame Etcheto, Larrère and Lafitte, with "sat" for "sitting" and "and all" beside them. The construction is a fair way to put country speech into English. Given to everybody, it removes the difference between a poacher, a landowner who is also the mayor, a pony breeder and a woman of ninety-four whose first language is Basque. Père Honoré and Sarthou, who are each given their own grammar (no contractions for one, the passive of a report for the other), show what the others lack.

**Do:** leave it to Jeannot, who has most of them and whose letters it suits. Larrère is a notable and speaks as one: "I won't be the president who". Madame Etcheto should sound translated from another language, with shorter sentences and the verb early. Arlette can keep the bluntness without the contraction.

### 9. A small set of moulds does too much of the work (throughout)

> "Maylis weighed that as she would have weighed a bale, with both hands." (ch. 15)

Three moulds recur until they are heard.

- **The "as she would have" simile**, five times: "as she would have tried a bank", "as she would have watched a man walk on a frozen pond", "as she would have read it off a chart", "as he would have carried a sack of maize to a hide", and the bale. Four come from the person's own work and earn their place. The bale is decoration at the moment of the moral exchange, and Maylis has never baled anything in this book.
- **The look.** "Looked" appears 107 times and "look", "looks", "looking" 42 more, about one every 300 words. In the flood chapter it is twelve. Most beats between two lines of dialogue are a look or a hand.
- **The hand as a ruler.** "Hand" and "hands" appear 249 times. The right hand is the book's subject and that count is mostly earned. But "a hand" is also the unit of measure: "a hand of water" (nine times), "stood a hand open", "a hand from her chin", "a roof a hand above her head", "up a hand". When the same paragraph has a woman awake inside a right hand and a gate raised a hand, the motif blurs.

About 27% of the book's sentences begin with She, He, It or one of three names, and most then run subject, verb, object, "and", second clause. That evenness is the voice and it is what makes room twelve unbearable. It is also why chapters 7 and 8, where a blackmail and a forced resignation happen, read at the same temperature as a count of road signs.

**Do:** cut the bale. Remove a third of the looks, starting where the dialogue already says who is being addressed. Keep "a hand" for water and measure the other things in centimetres or fingers, as the book already does elsewhere.

### 10. Slips of usage and consistency

Each of these is small. Together they are what a copy-editor would send back.

- **Inguma is named before Lesca has the name.** In chapter 2 it is "the thing" throughout. In chapter 4 the narrator, in Lesca's point of view, calls it Inguma in the first sentence, and again when it fetches her home. That evening she asks her mother for "words for a thing that sits on a sleeper" and is told the name. The narrator knew it a day before she did.
- **"Jeannot" for the dead man.** Chapter 9 states the rule: "It's Jeannot. Jean's for the stone." Chapter 2 breaks it in advance: "Jeannot Castagnet had sent the mairie twelve. He had been dead for twelve years less eighteen days." With "Jean" in the second sentence of that pair, the line is sharper and the rule holds.
- **"Phone" twice, "telephone" 71 times.** Kévin says "The phone goes off on the hour" (ch. 3) and the lieutenant says "Bring the phone you had that night" (ch. 15). Kévin's can stand as his age. The lieutenant's reads as a slip.
- **"The other one" as the narrator's name for Lesca** appears only in chapter 14 ("It was the other one's sum", "as the other one had"). Everywhere else in Maylis's chapters the narrator says Lesca.
- **Twice or three times by the bed.** Coralie says "I've sat by that bed twice with the telephone" (ch. 10) and "Twice I've sat by that bed" (ch. 13). By then Lesca's body has lain with its eyes open on the Saturday (twenty minutes, and Coralie rang her mother), the Monday and the following Saturday.
- **"which it was prison to net at a gate"** (ch. 3) is French word order in English words. "which it was a prison matter to net" or "which a man could go to prison for netting".
- **"She had every word of her own voice."** (ch. 3) I read this three times and do not know what it says about Madame Etcheto.
- **"Pau wants the count by Friday week"** (ch. 6). Pau stands for the prefecture seven chapters before chapter 13 says "the prefecture at Pau".
- **"Monsieur le préfet"** (ch. 15) keeps its accent and its French; "the prefect" is English everywhere else, while "Madame le maire" is French everywhere. Pick one rule for titles used in address and apply it to both.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 11, entire.** The night at the gate told as something worn "as smooth as a step", the reflector put out by the water, one ring against nine, and Jeannot's six numbered points ending "And six. Bugger off." followed by "She listened to all of it."
2. **Chapter 14 from the ramp to the last line.** The numbers on the doors coming up in the torch, Coralie's two fingers on a wrist for the half minute, the pencil line through the last name, and "Forty-one" said over an empty chair.
3. **Coralie's speech about the telephone in chapter 10.** "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." and "Nine minutes to three. I've had that for twelve years."
4. **The winch inspection in chapter 3.** "Through the gap" eight times, the ewe of seventy-one, "It wants grease." / "It's had grease since 2013.", and "The keyholder has no preference".
5. **Every device that is set as a joke and paid as a loss.** Kévin's five fingers (ch. 3, 14, 17), Monsieur Daguerre's "Here" (ch. 9, 14, 15), the voice through the oven door that Coralie cannot finish (ch. 1, 17), "Put it in forty-two" (ch. 1, 17), the pillow (ch. 9, 16, 17). Any cull of repeated phrases must go round these.
