# Round 6: Reader

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else opened.

Lens: an ordinary reader with no special knowledge of rivers, French village government, Basque folklore or physics.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." I could repeat the whole problem to a stranger after chapter 2, and it never got smaller.
- **Tension: 7.** "The telephone began to ring in her hand." Chapters 10 to 14 I read in one sitting. Chapters 7 to 9 let the river go out of sight for days at a time.
- **Progression: 6.** "At five that morning a man had sworn at her down a telephone, alive, and she no longer meant to wake in anybody's bed but her own." A plan I had never properly understood is cancelled in half a sentence. Things change in every chapter, but in the middle I could not say what each woman was now trying to do.
- **Comedy: 7.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade.'" I smiled on most pages and laughed out loud five times. That is good. It is not the joke rate of The Good Place.
- **Characters: 8.** "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them." Coralie is the best thing in the book, in both villages.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 9.** "He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." A boy counts his fingers in a flooded yard, hoping it is a dream. The running joke becomes the worst moment without changing a word of it.
- **Prose: 7.** "Then the warmth went out of the arms of the chair, as it goes out of a bench when the person beside you stands." Plain and exact when it describes. When it has to tell me who is doing what inside one body, it is so compressed that I read sentences twice.
- **The ending: 8.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it."
- **Clarity: 5.** "So the knees were told to bend and told to stay, and they locked." I worked out who told them what on the second reading.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. I would have put it down once, in chapter 2, and picked it up again the next day. I read chapter 8 twice to follow it.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On the friend who reads novels slowly, yes, today. On the friend who lent me Dark Matter, no. She would stop in chapter 2 and I would not blame her.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, for this lens.** The book leaves more behind than a Crouch novel does, and it is harder to get into and harder to follow than either comparison. An ordinary reader is judged lost before she is judged moved.

1. **Crouch tells you the rule in a sentence you can repeat, and tells it early. This book makes me earn it.** In Dark Matter I know what the box does the moment it is explained, and I never again have to wonder what the hero is trying to do. Here the rules arrive in chapter 5 ("I make it three points"), which is the right scene and a funny one, but I had guessed for four chapters first, and from chapter 8 the rules are being used faster than I can apply them. In the middle of the book I did not know who intended to be in whose body on the night that everything depends on.
2. **Level with The Good Place on the reversal that re-reads everything.** "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." The hour the thing has come at since page one is the hour of a telephone call. I went back to the first line of the book. The verb for the stone turning out to be *left*, the word on the card, does it a second time.
3. **Above Crouch on the marriage.** Crouch makes me afraid for a marriage by showing one good evening and taking it away. This book shows me the same woman in two houses, one where she is asked nothing and one where she has moved across a landing, and makes the visitor want the wrong one. I was more afraid on a staircase in chapter 6 than at the gate.

### The Reader's questions, answered directly

**What I did not understand**

- Who meant to do what on the night of the eleventh, from chapter 8 to chapter 11.
- Who was driving the mayor's body in the council room in chapter 8.
- Why the flood comes to the second village at all, twelve years on, with the same readings. The book asks this in chapter 5 and never returns to it.
- What happens to a visitor who is not fetched home. I learned in chapter 13 that she could die of it. I should have been frightened of it from chapter 4.
- Why the thing leaves "by the door" in chapter 15 before the confession, when the confession is what I expected to settle it.
- What Maylis gains by forcing Jeannot to withdraw his complaint in chapter 7.
- "Pau." For a page of chapter 6 I took it for a person. "The code", "the brigade" and "the firework bull" I got from context, late.
- "The tow" in chapter 6 and "a telephone going on a mower" in chapter 8. Both are explained a chapter or more after they are used.

**Where I was bored, lost or skimming**

- Chapter 2, the committee and the speech of river levels.
- Chapter 8, from the committee to Marie-Jo's doorstep: lost, not bored.
- Chapter 10, the vote on whether the mayor speaks.
- Chapter 13, the tape and the seating plan, with a telephone ringing in the previous chapter.
- Chapter 17, the first scene, with the telephone held up against the folder.

**Where I could not stop**

- Chapter 4 from the first line to the motion.
- Chapter 6 entire.
- The landing in chapter 9.
- From Coralie holding the speech in chapter 10 to "Forty-one" at the end of chapter 14, without a break.

**What I will remember in a month**

- One ring, and nine.
- 02:51.

- A car window going all the way down.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. The middle of the book hides its own plan (chapters 7 to 11)

> 'Have Friday,' Maylis said. 'I'll have yours.'

Chapter 7 ends with a car in a lane. Chapter 8 opens with Maylis already in the other woman's bed, driving her body. I never see her decide to cross. I never see her stay in the chair or give the thing its count, and I am not told why she goes. This is the woman who has never wanted anything, and her first act of wanting happens between chapters.

Then the trade. Lesca will take Friday night at the gate, Maylis will take Saturday and the sash. Lesca herself says it cannot work ("Your own body lies in a van on that dyke with its eyes open, and nobody is at the gate"), Maylis answers "Then I'll sit", and the scene ends. Chapter 9 gives me a handover "in case Lesca kept the chair". Chapter 11 tells me "Maylis still meant to wake in Lesca's bed on Saturday, as mayor". Only in chapter 15, at "the chair they had fought over", did I understand that the two of them had been racing for one seat, and that Maylis had been prepared to leave her own village's gate unattended to win it. That is the most shocking thing she does in the book and I read past it.

**What I would do.** Write the missing scene and put it at the end of chapter 7: Maylis in the chair, after the office and the six lines and the gap, choosing to stay seated, and saying what she gives the thing to count and why. Then, in the last scene of chapter 8, have each woman say in one plain line what happens at nine minutes to three on the eleventh if she wins the chair and what happens if she loses it. Four sentences. After that the handover, the cancelled plan in chapter 12 and the fight in chapter 13 all carry the weight they are built for.

### 2. The central choice takes three sentences (chapter 14)

> She had said both. On foot it was one or the other, and she went to the man.

This is the moment the book exists for. A woman who has answered "Either" for sixteen years has six minutes, a gate behind her with the woman she loves under the bank, and a man in front of her. She chooses, and I get a sum and a clause. I did the arithmetic myself afterwards (four hundred metres on, six hundred back, the Trafic for the return) to check that it was a real choice and not a mistake. It is a real choice. I should not have had to check.

**What I would do.** Stop the clock at the dip for one short paragraph. Give her what is behind her (the lights of the home, the telephone ringing with the mayor's name on it) and what is in front (the reflector), and give me her reason in her own terms: nine rings, a man who answered, five words left in her mouth. Then she runs. Keep it under a hundred words. The reason has to be hers and it has to be on the page.

### 3. In chapter 8 I cannot tell who is driving

> Lesca told her knees to bend, so as to sit down and take the first item.
>
> Maylis had the knees.

The chapter opens by telling me Maylis drives Lesca's body with Lesca awake in the right hand. Then the committee scene says "Lesca gave the minute", "Lesca stood with her fingers on the back of the mayor's chair", "Lesca stayed on her feet", "'Item one,' Lesca said". Sometimes the name means the mind, sometimes the body the room is looking at. The laugh in the minute's silence, which is the best joke in the chapter and wrecks a widow, took me two readings to assign.

**What I would do.** One rule for the whole book: a name always means a mind. The body is "the mayor", "the keyholder", "the hand", "the mouth". Then go through chapters 4, 6, 8, 9 and 13 and apply it without exception. Before the laugh, one clause telling me that Maylis is standing a minute's silence for a man she saw at a counter yesterday.

### 4. Chapter 2 asks too much before it has given enough

> 'On the twenty-fifth the river stood a metre sixty under the red line, which is the height of the doorsteps in the Port. On the eighth of December it was at the line. On the night of the eleventh it was ninety centimetres over and rising, and that was the nineteenth day of rain.'

In one chapter I meet a second heroine with the first one's name, five council members, a flood, a memorial, a roof, a lake, an envelope, a flashback, a steel room and a file. The flood, which is the event the whole book turns on, first reaches me as a recited list of measurements. And I do not yet know what the gate does: that is explained in chapter 3 ("Raised in a flood, it would take the river off the bank and let it out over the meadows, and over whatever was standing on them"). So the first telling of the choice lands on a reader who cannot picture it.

**What I would do.** Move the two-sentence explanation of the gate into chapter 1, where the mayor has it raised and Jeannot loses his water; it belongs there. Cut the speech in chapter 2 to three figures: the houses, the beds, the man. The rest of the levels can wait for the café in chapter 6, where the same speech is better. Lose Tastet's lines and Madame Ducasse's from the committee; they can be introduced when they do something.

### 5. I did not know that crossing could kill her

> It was a loto, and the players were the village's dead.

The hall of the dead arrives in one paragraph of chapter 10, told and not shown, with "a place laid for whoever died that night". Until Lesca's name is read off the card in chapter 13, the worst that crossing had threatened was an ambulance. So when she sits down on the back of the thing in the chair, which is the bravest act in the book, I did not know what she was risking. I worked it out after she had survived it.

**What I would do.** Plant it in chapter 5. When Lesca gives her three points, let there be a fourth that neither can account for: the voice behind the wall calling numbers. Let Arlette or Madame Etcheto say, in their own flat way, what becomes of a sleeper it does not fetch. Then her mother's short count of ponies in chapter 13 is a rescue I understand while it is happening, not on the next page.

### 6. The reason for the flood is asked once and dropped (chapter 5)

> 'Then it had rained two days before you did anything.'

Lesca believes she carried the flood across. Maylis proves in three lines that she did not. Lesca says "I want somebody found to blame", Maylis says "Nobody was to blame for a dry one", and the subject never comes back. But I went on asking: why this village, why twelve years to the day, why the same readings? Dr Kessler, the only person who might know, leaves in chapter 1 and is never thought of again. A reader of Crouch expects the engine to be accounted for, or to be told by someone she trusts that it cannot be.

**What I would do.** Close it in the steel room in chapter 15, where neither can lie. The material is already there: "The bank held sixteen minutes. You gave it ten, and so did I." Pau said eighty-five where the file said ninety. Let one of them say what that means: it was never the same flood, and it was never hers to bring or to stop. One exchange.

### 7. Chapter 13 keeps me from a ringing telephone for too long

> He paced from the steps to the stone, tore off two strips and laid a cross on the planks beside it.

Chapter 12 ends on the best cliffhanger in the book. Chapter 13 then gives me a rehearsal, a seating plan, eight names, a mother's visit and a bedtime before it returns to the van. Arlette's words are essential and the eight names matter. The young man with the tape I skimmed, wanting the dyke.

**What I would do.** Move the tape and the front row to Thursday, into chapter 10, where the square is already being built and I am not waiting for anything. Open chapter 13 on Arlette in the mairie.

### 8. The book refers to things before it has told me them

> With the tow, a list had come to her in the chair, and a telephone going on a mower.

I did not know what the tow was until a letter in chapter 8 and a line in chapter 12. I did not know what the mower was until the bar in chapter 9, a chapter after Lesca asks Marie-Jo about it on a doorstep, so the doorstep scene meant nothing to me when I read it. "Pau wants the count" in chapter 6 names a town as if it were a colleague. This is a habit, and it is different from a plant. The suitcase on the wardrobe is a plant: I can see it and wonder. The tow is a reference to a story I have not been given.

**What I would do.** Put the loto bar before Marie-Jo's doorstep, or give the mower one sentence in chapter 8. Give the tow one sentence in chapter 6. First time, "the prefecture at Pau".

### 9. I do not know what Maylis wants from Jeannot in chapter 7

> She would have done it again that afternoon, to anybody.

She threatens a man who has taken her side for years. It costs her his regard, and the loss is beautifully done ("He had left the little off. She heard it go"). But I could not see what the withdrawn complaint bought her. The mayor's hold on her is the undated letter, not the complaint. And the scene in which her name becomes the only one in the gendarme's file comes after the blackmail, not before.

**What I would do.** Put Sarthou's kitchen before the café. Once I have seen "the one name in this file is yours", I know exactly what she is buying and what she pays for it.

### 10. Too many figures, and most of them do not pay

> 'That river is a metre twenty under the line this morning, and on Friday week it will be ninety over.'

A metre sixty under, a metre fifty, a metre forty, a metre twenty, seventy under, sixty over, eighty, eighty-five, ninety, ninety-two. Forty letters, forty-one, forty-two, twelve, seventeen. Eleven years, twelve, sixteen. Nineteen turns, nineteen days, nineteen sandbags. The numbers that pay are unforgettable: one ring and nine, forty-two and forty-one, 02:51, five fingers. The rest blur them. Three nineteens made me look for a meaning that is not there.

**What I would do.** Keep the levels to "under the line", "at the line", "over the line" and the one figure of ninety, except in the mouths of people who would say them (the mayor, the gendarme). Change two of the nineteens.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The call log (chapter 10).** "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." And Coralie's reason for never asking: "Because you'd have told me."
2. **Nine rings (chapter 11).** The whole of Jeannot's answer, numbered, ending "And six. Bugger off." It is the funniest speech in the book and it is the moral argument of the book, and it does both by being a man annoyed about ducks.
3. **The landing (chapter 9).** "Then I'll have waited for the right one." Including the hand that gets as far as the door, and "Then she can say so in the morning."
4. **Five (chapters 3, 14, 17).** Kévin in the bucket, Kévin in the water, Kévin on the step of his van. With Monsieur Daguerre's "Here" beside it at every turn. Do not explain either.
5. **The kitchen clock (chapters 11, 14, 16).** Heard down a telephone twelve years ago, read off a screen as "Castagnet, house", and then hanging on a wall over a woman who has never said. Nobody in the book points at it until Maïté does.
