# Critic's report: Thriller lens

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

Verdict against the bar: as a thriller the book is at 7. Chapters 12 to 15 are at 9 and beat the comparison. Chapters 8 to 10 are at 6 and lose a Crouch reader. It is not yet "as good or better" taken whole.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Behind it are forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. … And out on the meadows there is one man." (6) The sum is clear, countable and personal. But neither woman is ever in danger herself, and what Maylis stands to lose until the last night is a job.
- **Tension: 6.** "On Tuesday the river had reached the line, and Pau had sent a yellow." (12) The first proof that the prophecy is true arrives in the pluperfect, a chapter late. The river rises off the page for a week.
- **Progression: 6.** "'Then ask Larrère.' 'I can't ask him.'" (8) That exchange is the whole of the campaign against the flood between chapter 7 and chapter 12.
- **Comedy: 7.5.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade." (7)
- **Characters: 8.** "I chose you, Maylis, over somebody who asked." (17)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.5.** "'Five,' he said. He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." (14)
- **Prose: 7.5.** "The reflector burned in the lamps at the end of the track for four minutes more. Then the water came up the door to it and put it out." (11) Against that: the action sentences at the climax need reading twice (fault 7).
- **The ending: 8.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (17) Maylis's side costs a death, a job, a name and a marriage. Lesca's side is paid within twelve hours: "Coralie came in with the pillow." (16)
- **Clarity: 5.5.** "'For her. In case she has my Friday.'" (9) against "Maylis still meant to wake in Lesca's bed on Saturday, as mayor" (11). On the eve of the night I could not have said what either woman planned to do.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes, but on credit through chapters 8 and 10. From the nine rings in chapter 11 I could not have stopped.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who reads for people and sentences, yes. On a friend who reads Crouch, not yet: I would have to tell them to push through chapter 2 and wait for chapter 12, and a thriller you have to apologise for is not at the bar.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, on the middle.** A Crouch protagonist does something irreversible in every chapter to get the one thing he wants, and the world answers harder. Here the flood is announced at the end of chapter 2, fought for one day in chapter 6, and then left alone until the orange envelope. Chapters 8 to 10 run on who gets the bed and who gets the sash. Those are good questions, but they are not on the clock.

**Below, on the rule.** Dark Matter states its rule once and makes the third act out of that rule's worst consequence. This book states three rules in chapter 5 and uses them as stated. The one late escalation, Lesca sitting down on Inguma's back, needs two new rules to work, and they arrive in the same chapter (fault 7).

**Below, on the reversal.** The Good Place has one turn that re-reads every scene before it. This book has two good partial ones: 02:51 turning out to be the minute of the call, which re-reads the first sentence, and "she had moved it from a cellar to a bed" (15), which re-reads Lesca's twelve years. Neither re-reads the whole.

**Level, on chapter endings.** Eleven of sixteen end on an open action or a reversal, in one plain sentence where Crouch would use a fragment: "Under Coralie's fingers her right hand turned over, and she had not told it to." (3)

**Level, then early, on fear for the marriage.** Crouch frightens the reader by putting the other man in the wife's bed. This book puts the other self at the foot of the stairs with the wife on the fourth step, and then lets the wife decide: "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak." (9) That is better than Crouch's Daniela ever gets. It also retires the threat eight chapters before the end.

**Above, on the night and its cost.** Crouch's climaxes are chases. Chapter 14 is a dilemma with timestamps, a real remainder and a named dead woman. Neither comparison has a chapter that costs as much.

**Where the reader is afraid.** For Coralie's bed at the end of 4 and 6. For Jeannot from "Come down and count me" (9). For Madame Etcheto from "I'll have them put me back at ten" (12). For everyone from 02:53 to "Forty-one". Never for either woman's life, and never for Lesca's secret, since nobody is hunting it.

**Where a Crouch reader puts it down.** The committee scene in chapter 2. The seven men at the winch in chapter 3, if chapter 2 has not hooked them. Chapter 8, which has five scenes and no river. The first half of chapter 10.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The fortnight is not spent. (7 to 12)**
"On Tuesday the river had reached the line, and Pau had sent a yellow. Larrère had told her that the gate stayed shut and that the Port knew it had a river. He had been told the week before, through his own car window, that he would say exactly that. Sarthou had believed the river sooner." (12)
Every step of the escalation is in that paragraph, and all of it is reported after the fact: the river at the line, the yellow, the prediction coming true word for word, the first villager to believe. Between the café on 30 November and the orange on 11 December nobody makes a second attempt on the flood. Saturday the 5th to Thursday the 10th has no scene on Maylis's side that is about the river.
*Do:* give Tuesday the 8th a scene in Maylis's village. The river reaches the line, Larrère says the predicted sentence in front of the café that wrote "Madame le maire" on a score pad, and the list gets an entry nobody laughs at. Then give Maylis one real attempt on Wednesday or Thursday that fails and costs her something. Put a gauge reading in every Maylis chapter from 7 on. Chapter 10 has none.

**2. The daylight door is left open. (5, 12)**
"That gate is opened on the Friday afternoon, on the ebb, in daylight, by order, with the meadows cleared and nobody on them." (5) Then, on the Friday afternoon: "Maylis was on the dyke by five … It was the white Trafic. It stopped level with her, door to door" (12).
The fix was stated in chapter 5. At five on Friday she has the key on her belt, the handle in its box, daylight, and Jeannot above ground at her window. All that stops her is a letter. She then defies the same order twice, to knock on a hatch and to drive the track, so the letter cannot be what stops her. A thriller reader asks why she does not open it, and the book does not answer.
*Do:* make her try, or make her refusal the scene. Either she fits the handle at five and something physical stops her, or she fits it, looks at the folder on his passenger seat, and takes it off again, and somebody says so to her face in chapter 17. "You had the key" (17) is currently said about 02:57. It should be said about five in the afternoon.

**3. Nobody knows the plan before the night. (8, 9, 11)**
"'Then I'll sit,' Maylis said. 'It's where the sash is.'" (8). "'In the same coat. In the one hand she leaves me.'" (9). "Maylis still meant to wake in Lesca's bed on Saturday, as mayor" (11). "Tonight she would stay sitting in the steel chair when Maylis woke" (13).
Both women intend to stay sitting, which cannot happen, and Maylis writes a handover for a night she intends not to attend. A plan has to be legible before it can go wrong. Chapter 15 then refers to "the chair they had fought over", a fight that is not on the page.
*Do:* one exchange in the steel room in chapter 10 where each says plainly what she will do at 02:51 on Friday, and each sees that the two plans collide. Then the race for the chair is a thing the reader is waiting for, and Inguma taking the seat in 13 is a reversal.

**4. Nothing is closing on Lesca. (10, 13)**
"'When you stood on that platform, before you laid a hand on the winch, did you—' He stopped." (10) "She thought, watching him, that he did not altogether want it said."
The one man who could force her has his own reason to keep quiet. So her half of the book has no pursuer, her confession is free, and her chapters before the microphone are logistics: a flag bearer, a tape cross, eight strips for wheelchairs. Chapter 13 spends 1,700 words on these while a telephone rings in a van.
*Do:* put the evidence in motion. The phone is "in a drawer" (15) and the association's ledger has a line "with nothing written under it" (8). Let one of them start to surface before Saturday, through Maïté or through Larrère finishing his question, so that Lesca confesses with something a step behind her. And cut the tape-cross scene by half.

**5. The secret is spent in instalments and the confession is said five times. (8, 10, 11, 13, 15, 16)**
"I rang once. I had his van in my headlamps and his number in my hand, and I let it ring once and put the telephone in my pocket." (15, steel room). Sixty lines later, at the microphone: "I rang his telephone, and I let it ring once."
The reader guesses at "That's not a no" (8), is told by Coralie (10), is shown (11), hears it in the steel room (13 and 15), at the microphone (15), in Maïté's kitchen (16) and on the landing (16). The public sentence is the climax of Lesca's book and it has been said to the reader a page earlier in the same chapter. Chapter 16 also re-reveals Larrère in the kitchen, which "Castagnet, house" already did, and better.
*Do:* in the steel room of 15, let Lesca refuse the houses without restating the call. Open the Maïté scene at "Which were you counting?". Keep the reader's first hearing of "I knew that he was" for the microphone.

**6. Chapter 2 carries too much before the hook lands. (2)**
"Lesca had the chair, and her first deputy, Monsieur Tastet, sat on her right. Madame Ducasse, the mairie's secretary, sat at the end of the table with the register. Maïté Castagnet, the widow of Jean Castagnet, held her handbag…"
Five new names in four sentences, a timer, a motion about a verb, before the reader is sure there are two villages. The two cards and "It had not had it yet" are the hook of the whole book and they sit behind six scenes and 2,700 words. Crouch has his premise legible, not just present, by this point.
*Do:* split it. Night, stone, envelope, chair, *left*: end the chapter on "Somebody was sitting there with her, in the one seat." Committee, roof and file make the next, ending where it ends now.

**7. The rules bend at the climax. (13)**
"Maylis had been awake when Inguma held her, and had taken the rest back." And: "'The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies … and she will not be taking the place.'"
The first is a new rule that exists to produce the fight for one arm. The second is Lesca being called to the table of the dead and declining by formula. The reader was never told what a visitor risks, so there is no fear before it and no price after it. The arm fight itself has to be parsed: "Lesca set the fingers as she had once set them on a handle with the skin off and went back up".
*Do:* plant both. Add a fourth point in chapter 5, or a demonstration in 9, that whoever is awake at 02:51 keeps her own body. Have Arlette or Madame Etcheto say in 4 or 12 what becomes of someone Inguma does not fetch. Then make Lesca leave something at that table. Write the arm fight in short sentences with the owner of each limb named.

**8. Chapter openings announce their own outcome. (4, 6, 8, 9)**
"On Monday it rained, and Lesca woke in Maylis's bed and drove Maylis's body, with Maylis awake in the right hand, for as long as Inguma took to count a lake." (6)
Chapter 5 ends on legs that move by themselves. The first sentence of 6 says who is moving them, that it will end, and how. The formula is used four times. It answers each cliff in the line after it is set and removes the question of how long she has.
*Do:* keep it once, in 6, where it is new. Elsewhere open in the scene and let the reader count with Inguma.

**9. The winch pays off after the clock has stopped. (14)**
"'It's stopped,' he said. 'Your winch. I've three things to say about it.'"
Nineteen turns are planted in chapters 1, 3, 6, 9 and 12. The payoff comes at about 03:35, to Jeannot, offstage, when the bank has been gone twenty minutes and nothing depends on it. "She had not turned it a centimetre" is the right irony. But the most-planted object in the book is absent from its climax.
*Do:* keep her hands off it, and bring the stick inside the clock. Jeannot shouts "nineteen" along the crown while she can still hear him, before she goes down into the water.

**10. The mechanism is dropped. (2, 5)**
"'Then it's the winch,' Lesca said." "'Nobody was to blame for a dry one.'" (5)
Dr Kessler, the lamps and the question of why Lesca's flood is in Maylis's river twelve years late are all raised and none is returned to after chapter 5. The book's answer is that cause is not the point. A reader promised "whether, somewhere, you also see the other" (1) will still want the two women to say so.
*Do:* three lines in the last steel room. One asks why it rained. The other cannot lie and does not know.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 14 from 02:53 to "Forty-one".** The timestamps, "Both", the dip, the key thrown along the crown, "She had not turned it a centimetre", the doors numbered nine, ten, eleven.
2. **Kévin's count at four o'clock**, and its return: "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything as much." (17)
3. **The kitchen clock.** "Behind his voice a clock was ticking, close, of the kind that hangs in a kitchen" (11), then "Under the time it said Castagnet, house" (14).
4. **The call log.** "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." and "I've had that for twelve years." (10)
5. **Madame Etcheto's line of plants**: "I'll have them put me back at ten" (12), the text at ten, "Twelve. One, two. A chair was pulled in to a table." (15), and the living one asking "Where was it?" (15).
