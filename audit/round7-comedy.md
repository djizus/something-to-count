# Critic's report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** Countable from chapter 2 and never inflated: "Behind it are forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. [...] 'And out on the meadows there is one man.'" (6). What Maylis herself stands to lose is not on the page until chapter 9.
- **Tension: 6.** Chapters 11 to 14 run at nine. Chapters 7 to 10 wait for a date the reader was given in chapter 4, and the book says so itself: "The ham was played last, on a full card, and it took a long time." (9)
- **Progression: 6.** The turn the reader was promised happens between chapters: "On Tuesday the river had reached the line, and Pau had sent a yellow. Larrère had told her that the gate stayed shut" (12). That is a summary of a scene chapter 6 set up word for word.
- **Comedy: 6.5.** Six or seven real laughs in seventeen chapters and a steady smile between them, all in one key. The best: "He had been saving it since 2015, and there was less of it than he had thought." (8)
- **Characters: 7.5.** Coralie carries it: "Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you, and that was me. Eggs, then. Eggs is what Either gets in this house." (1)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** A drowned man's reflector goes out, a telephone rings nine times, and: "'Who's dead?' said Jeannot Castagnet." (11)
- **Prose: 7.** Exact and dry: "The table took the answer and had not once asked to see her working." (2). It explains itself at the wrong moments: "She knew that for the word she wrote on the shopping pad. It was Either." (12)
- **The ending: 8.** It costs a death, a job, a marriage and a village's regard, and the last sentence is a joke that is also a burial: "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (17)
- **Clarity: 5.** A rule arrives in the sentence that needs it: "Maylis had been awake when Inguma pinned her, so the body was still hers. Lesca, coming in from the chair, got one arm, the right, from the shoulder to the bound hand." (13)

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes, from chapter 3 on. Chapter 2 nearly lost me.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On the friend who likes dry village comedy. Not on the friend who asked for something like The Good Place. That friend would call it wry and ask where the jokes were.

## 2. Against the comparisons

Crouch is not funny and does not try to be, so the comedy is measured against The Good Place.

**Below, on being funny.**

- **Speed of premise.** The Good Place states its rules in its first scene, through a cheerful man at a desk, and by the end of the first episode Eleanor's presence has filled the sky with flying shrimp. Here the rules are stated in chapter 5, and the first day of one woman loose in the other's village is chapter 6. There are three such days in the book.
- **Range.** The Good Place has a vulgar one, a name-dropper, a man who cannot choose and an idiot, and they do not sound alike. Here everybody is dry. Larrère, Sarthou, Madame Ducasse, the woman at Hontaube, Tastet, the young man from Pau and Lesca herself all get their laugh the same way, by applying a form to something that will not go on a form.
- **Compounding.** A Good Place episode puts every lie in one room and lets them make each other worse. This book has that room, the loto in chapter 9, and uses it to deliver information.

**Level, on making the moral problem funny without making it small.**

- "Either" is Chidi's stomach ache done as well as the series does it. It is cooked as eggs, ticked in both boxes, refused by a gendarme ("I cannot summon Either to the brigade"), minuted as consent, and finally named for what it cost: "That was Either, with the river in it."
- The stone that cannot take a verb ("It reads that she opened him") is the trolley problem as a drafting dispute.
- The ending is a Good Place reversal: the man she left the gate to save was sitting in "the driest hole in the commune", and his letter of thanks "doesn't thank you".

**Above, on the switch.**

- The Good Place usually protects a serious moment by joking straight after it. This book lets the joke be the serious moment. Kévin counts his fingers in the flood, hoping he is asleep: "'Five,' he said. He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." (14)
- The timer that is the book's best running gag turns out, in chapter 16, to be what cut Maïté off before she could say *left*. The joke re-reads as the harm.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The premise is the funniest thing in the book and it is kept out of play. (Chapters 1 to 5; structural.)**
The two women first speak in chapter 5: "They were of a height, and neither of them liked it." That scene is the book finding its double act: "'Those in favour.' 'There's two of us.'" They meet on their feet four more times, each shorter and graver than the last, and chapter 10's sitting is twenty lines. Meanwhile chapters 3 and 4 play the same discovery twice. Each woman sits in the chair, reads the other's card, looks away and reads it again. Cut one of the two card scenes. Have them stand up and face each other by the end of chapter 3. Give the steel room two more full sittings in the middle, with a quarrel in each.

**2. The loto is built as the set piece and does not go off. (Chapter 9; structural.)**
Chapter 6 lays a farce line. The café decides the commune's girl is carrying on with Castagnet: "'That's the pot explained,' said the owner's wife", and "'That's so we'd not think it,' said Lafitte." At the loto Lafitte reads the whole list to a full hall and asks "What was he given for it?" Then the scene goes off to do five other jobs. Larrère, who has the most to fear from that list, is not in the hall. Coralie is, and nobody says it to her. Maïté never hears it. The theory is never spoken again. Put Larrère at a table and Maïté beside her husband, and let the hall's wrong answer be said aloud in front of the three people it is wrong about, while Lesca, in the wrong body, makes it worse by telling the truth.

**3. One voice for the whole cast. (Throughout.)**
Darrigrand: "Then the commune can sweep it." Madame Etcheto: "That's few. It counts quickly." Maïté: "Nor did you." Arlette: "I've set it." Everyone has the same clipped comeback, and nobody talks too much, gets it wrong, or is loud. The two exceptions are the two funniest people in the book. Jeannot runs on in numbered points, and Kévin says exactly what he thinks: "'I'd fly,' Kévin said." Give the cadence to fewer people. Let the owner's wife gabble, let Tastet dither aloud instead of looking at his hat, and give Kévin and Bixente a scene in each third.

**4. The room where nobody can lie never meets the woman who says Either. (Chapters 5, 8, 10.)**
The rule is set up as a gag: "Tell me your name is Guy Larrère." It is then used only for gravity. In the same scene Maylis says "Either" in that room and it comes out, which tells the reader she truly has no preference. The book's two best devices are in one room and never collide. Once, late, she should open her mouth for "Either" in there and get nothing. That is the comic turn and the moral turn in one beat, and it should replace the moment in chapter 12 where the narrator explains it for her.

**5. The prophecy pays offstage. (Between chapters 9 and 10.)**
Chapter 6: "Pau will send you a yellow, and you'll tell me the gate stays shut, and that the Port knows it has a river." The reader waits to hear him say it. The book jumps from Saturday to Wednesday and reports it twice in summary (10 and 12). Write Tuesday the eighth: the window down a few centimetres, the sentence said word for word by a man who does not notice, and Maylis hearing it as herself. One page.

**6. Rules are delivered as clauses, and the slapstick is written as a by-law. (Chapters 8 and 13.)**
The bargain on which the plot turns is four conditionals: "If I am the one left sitting at nine minutes to three on the eleventh, I wake in your body, in your van, and I open that gate. If I am not, [...]" The fight for the ignition key, two women with one arm, should be the physical high point. The reader spends it working out who has which limb, because the rule for that came one paragraph earlier. A reader who is working something out is not laughing. State the trade in one line each ("Have Friday. I'll have yours." already does it), and establish who gets which limb in a scene with nothing at stake, before the van.

**7. The same sketch four times. (Chapters 6, 7, 9, 12, 13.)**
An office cannot do the human thing because it has no form for it. Sarthou: "I cannot file a forecast." Madame Ducasse: "There's no column." Hontaube: "I've got a tray for each, and a book." Pau: "I have Monsieur le maire." Each is good. The fourth costs the first its surprise, and Hontaube runs thirty-five lines at the head of the chapter with the clock in it. Keep Ducasse whole. Cut Hontaube to "Then it should have gone last Monday" and "You won't need a form then. You'll be ours."

**8. A runner with two beats, sixteen chapters apart. (Chapters 1 and 17.)**
"'Still in the box, were you?' somebody said inside, and the door shut on the laugh." It is not heard again until Marie-Jo retires it: "That's what late is now. They've retired the other." The payoff is the cruellest line in the book, and the reader has forgotten the set-up. Say it twice more in the middle, once to Lesca when she walks into the café in chapter 6 and once at the loto.

**9. The best invention is announced, not found. (Chapter 10.)**
"It was a loto, and the players were the village's dead." The afterlife as a village hall with strip lights, where nobody is asked anything, is told to the reader in one flat sentence. Chapter 8 seeded the voice calling a number "whole and then in its parts". Let Maylis recognise the cadence, then her father's watch beside a card, and let the reader get there half a line before she does.

**10. One joke costs a dark moment. (Chapter 14.)**
At 03:31, with the water risen from shins to knees and one van left: "I've three things to say about it. First, I counted, as you do, by the click [...]" This is the fourth outing of the numbered speech and the only one given with the water rising. It runs a hundred words in a doorway while people sit in the wet. Keep "It's up a hand. A hand." and "I put it in number six." Drop the scaffolding and let him be short for once, which would itself be the joke.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The winch inspection (3).** Six men, four umbrellas, one platform: "In seventy-one it was a ewe." / "It'll have sunk." / "It wants grease." / "It's had grease since 2013."
2. **Larrère's eleven minutes without a bell (8), and the garden gate that pays it (16):** "'I'll be brief,' he said. [...] Nothing came."
3. **The whole of chapter 11.** The reflector going out, the nine rings, "Who's dead?", and "And six. Bugger off."
4. **Inguma's work.** The care plan ("Staff to wait. Staff not to join in."), the night it "had been told twice and given two lots of stars, and it got through them early and came at one", the signatures ("A mayor, it turned out, signed a great deal"), and Arlette's ponies: "That's soon done."
5. **The oven, both times.** Coralie doing Larrère into it in chapter 1, and in chapter 17 the same joke begun and not finished: "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the neck. 'The—'"
