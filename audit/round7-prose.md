# Critic's report: Prose

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. 45,270 words, 4,471 sentences, 86 sections. Nothing else was opened.

Counts quoted below are whole-manuscript counts unless a chapter is named.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** They are concrete and countable from chapter 2 on: "Behind it were forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." The reader has to wait until chapter 5 to learn that this sum is about to be set a second time.
- **Tension: 6.** Chapters 11 to 15 are an 8 or 9 ("The telephone began to ring in her hand."). Chapters 1 to 3 and 7 to 10 run on procedure, and the prose reports several of its hinges after the fact instead of staging them.
- **Progression: 6.** The same position is restated in the same words: "a plan that wanted an order" closes a section in chapter 5 and again in chapter 7, and the order is still wanted in chapters 8 and 12.
- **Comedy: 7.** One register, done very well: "There's nothing here that lets me un-receive it. There's no column." It never changes register, and nearly every speaker has it.
- **Characters: 7.** Coralie is alive in every line she is given: "I don't cook Either. There's no recipe for it. Soup or duck." The two women are told apart by their formulas (pricing, motions) more than by how they sound.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** The book's best skill. In the flooded yard: "'Here,' said Monsieur Daguerre, to the roof of the van." The joke was planted at a loto and is paid in a drowning, and neither is cheapened.
- **Prose: 7.** As craft it is a 9: thirteen -ly adverbs in the whole book, no semicolons, no exclamation marks, every image taken from what the characters handle ("The tall L came out of the wrong hand like a fence post set by a boy"). As a way of delivering a high-concept story to the reader the brief names it is a 5, for the reasons in section 3.
- **The ending: 8.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." It costs a death, a marriage, a job and an office, and nobody is forgiven in words.
- **Clarity: 5.** The mechanics are given in riddles: "'Then three's by the hour,' Maylis said." A rule the climax depends on is first stated in the sentence that uses it (chapter 13).

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes. I would have considered stopping in chapter 3 and could not have stopped after chapter 10.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who reads Penelope Fitzgerald or J. L. Carr, today. On the Crouch and Good Place reader the bar describes, not yet: that reader re-reads a sentence perhaps forty times in the first five chapters to find out what happened, and Crouch never asks that once.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below the bar overall for the reader named; above Crouch sentence for sentence; level with The Good Place only in the last third.**

1. **Crouch states a rule in a line a tired reader can parse, at the moment it is needed. This book compresses the rule and expands the feeling.** That is the wrong way round. His prose is plainer and worse, and it never makes the reader work out what the box does. Here the reader works out the chair, the sitting, the fetching, the lying and the counting from Maylis's asides, and is then told in plain words what she feels about a shopping pad.
2. **Crouch makes the reader afraid for a marriage by saying so. This book does it with two fingers on a wrist and a pillow carried across a landing, and that is better than he does it.** "Her hand stayed on it a little longer than a bandage needs" (17) is beyond anything in Dark Matter. This is the one place the book is clearly above.
3. **The Good Place gives every person a different way of being wrong and a different way of talking. Here everyone is dry.** Arlette, Maïté, Marie-Jo, Madame Etcheto, Monsieur Darrigrand and a clerk at Hontaube who has one scene all deliver the same capped retort. The procedural comedy (the timer, the register with no column, the form that "should have gone last Monday") is level with the show's best bureaucracy jokes. The range is a fraction of it.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. The book explains the wrong things (structural; chapters 2, 5, 8, 12, 13)

Mechanism is compressed until it is a riddle, or invented at the moment of use. Motive, which the scenes have already shown, is then spelled out by the narrator.

Mechanism, chapter 13, at the climax: "Maylis had been awake when Inguma pinned her, so the body was still hers. Lesca, coming in from the chair, got one arm, the right, from the shoulder to the bound hand." Twice before, the sitter drove the whole body and the host kept one hand. The reversal that decides the book is a rule the reader meets for the first time here. Chapter 5 has the same trouble in small: "And in here neither of us can lie" is asserted by a woman on her first visit standing, and Maylis's glosses on the three points ("It's free", "Two cost me a page off the shopping pad", "Then three's by the hour") make the reader decode a joke before they have the rule.

Motive, chapter 12: "Maylis did not get out and fit the handle, since nobody had told her to. She knew that for the word she wrote on the shopping pad. It was Either." Chapter 8, two paragraphs after "'I want that,' Maylis said" has done the work: "What she wanted was a room that stood up when she came in, and a thing asked for at a desk and done by evening." Chapter 10: "She had counted on a seal for twelve years, and the second person who knew had been in the next room the whole time, with a pillow." Chapters 12 and 13 both open on a sentence of synopsis ("On Friday Maylis meant to stay awake at the gate, to get Jeannot Castagnet off the meadows before dark, and to open the gate herself if the bank began to go"). "Meant to" appears fifteen times, four of them in chapter 12.

**Do:** state each rule once, plainly, before it is needed. Put the awake rule in chapter 5 as part of point two, and cut the explanation from chapter 13 so the van scene runs on a rule the reader already holds. Then cut the motive glosses listed above. Coralie says "That was Either, with the river in it" in chapter 17, and it lands harder if the narrator has not said it first in chapter 12.

### 2. The premise sits behind riddling prose for four chapters (structural; chapters 1 to 4)

The sentence that tells the reader what kind of book this is closes chapter 2: "That village had not been spared December 2014. It had not had it yet." It is elegant and a first-time reader cannot be sure what it means. The experiment that causes everything gets ninety words dropped into a paralysis scene, ending "counted one of her, paid her, given her a sandwich and gone back to their university", which is a fine joke and gives no signal that it is the engine. The first crossing comes at about 7,900 words and the first conversation between the two women at about 9,500, a fifth of the way in. Crouch's premise is in play inside his first chapter.

**Do:** give the premise in a voice that can say it plainly. That voice exists: Coralie in chapter 4 says "they want to know if there's another of you somewhere who saw the other. I asked you were there two of you now." Give the other Coralie the equivalent in chapter 1's kitchen. Add one flat sentence to the end of chapter 2 after "It had not had it yet", saying that what happened to her in 2014 is three weeks from happening there. The riddle can stay; it needs an answer beside it.

### 3. Hinges are reported in the pluperfect instead of staged (structural; chapters 3, 10, 12, 17)

656 of 4,471 sentences contain "had". "She had" is the commonest sentence opening in the book (80), ahead of "It was" (77). Much of that is the voice, and is fine. The damage is where a turn of the story is one of those sentences.

Chapter 12: "At five that morning a man had sworn at her down a telephone, alive, and she no longer meant to wake in anybody's bed but her own. Before she left the house she had put her pencil through the handover on the back of the shopping pad." That is the moment Maylis gives up the swap she has been planning since chapter 7. It is the turn of her whole line, and it is backfill in the third paragraph of the next chapter.

Tuesday the eighth is the same. In chapter 6 Lesca predicts to Larrère's face what he will say when the river reaches the line. A prediction coming true is the cheapest thrill this premise owns. It comes true off the page and is then reported twice (chapter 10: "You told him he'd say it"; chapter 12: "He had been told the week before, through his own car window, that he would say exactly that").

**Do:** open chapter 12 on the pencil going through the handover, in the simple past, as an act. Stage the eighth as a short scene at the car window (the window can go down a second time) and cut both reports.

### 4. The relay: characters tell each other what the reader has just read (structural; chapters 8, 15, 16, 17)

Two worlds mean each woman must brief the other, and each Coralie must be briefed. The book does these briefings in full direct speech.

Chapter 15 opens, straight after the reader has lived chapter 14: "The cellar was dry. He had a stove lit down there, and I pulled him out by the collar. The bank held sixteen minutes. You gave it ten, and so did I... Forty-one came out of the home. Madame Etcheto didn't." Chapter 16, on the landing: "A fortnight ago I was in her bed for twenty minutes. Her Coralie still slept in it. She took my wrist, and I turned the hand over and held hers," then "Last Saturday I had her body until midnight. I walked it up her stairs to the box room door". Chapter 17, Coralie: "Last Saturday somebody stood on that landing in your coat and asked me back across. She answered whatever I put to her... Then I sent her down the stairs and shut my door".

In each case the new thing is one line long and comes after the recap: "I'd sign your sum." "No." / "I'm asking you." / "I chose you, Maylis, over somebody who asked."

**Do:** put the facts in one sentence of indirect speech ("Maylis told it in order, to the forty-one") and keep direct speech for what the reader has not heard. This recovers perhaps 600 words from the last three chapters, where pace matters most.

### 5. Everyone has the same wit, and every scene ends on it (chapters 3 to 17)

20 of the 86 sections end on a spoken capper, and most of the rest end on a single-sentence image of an object (a pad, a pillow, a door left open; "The window went up" closes three sections). The cappers are good. They are also interchangeable:

- Monsieur Darrigrand (4): "It's four when you're the one counting."
- Madame Etcheto (3): "A rib doesn't laugh."
- Arlette (8): "I know what you're called. I'm the last that uses it."
- Maïté (5): "I'd never hear the end of it. I wouldn't want to hear the beginning."
- Marie-Jo (9): "That's two answers. I waited six years for one."
- The clerk at Hontaube (12): "Then it should have gone last Monday."

The shared idiom makes it worse. "That" plus a plain noun ("that winch", "that gate", "that man", "that mairie") occurs 144 times, in the narrator's mouth as often as in the villagers'. "I'll not / you'll not" occurs 15 times across at least five speakers. By chapter 8 the reader can hear a scene's last line coming.

**Do:** keep the capper for the people who own it (Coralie, Jeannot, Larrère). Let Arlette be blunt without being neat, Maïté trail off, Marie-Jo talk too long. End a third of the sections one beat early, on business unfinished. Take "that" out of the narration where it is not a character's thought.

### 6. Tokens rung past their yield

Some repeated tokens pay: "I'll be brief" (5) ends at a garden gate with nothing after it; "two fingers" (15) ends on a dead woman's wrist; "Either" ends "with the river in it". Others are rung and never cashed:

- Maylis "priced" things 8 times; the tic shows character by its second use and decides nothing.
- "the width of" 10 and "the height of" 12: doors, hands, fingers, houses, barns, knees, elbows, ears, tables. The measuring habit becomes the narrator's, in both women's chapters.
- "hand of water" 10, "the tall L" 5, "took note" 7, "a door in a bank" 5, "wall side" 6.
- "Either" 42 times. Its work is done by perhaps 25. Cut the ones spoken to nobody ("'Either,' Maylis said", to Inguma, chapter 3) and the second and third of the four in chapter 5.

**Do:** for each token ask where it pays. Keep the plant, one reminder and the payment.

### 7. A handful of words and one joint do too much work

- "stood" 171 times, one every 265 words. The river stood, the stone stood, the card stood, the door stood, each figure "stood". It is the book's default verb for existing.
- "turned" 120, "gave" 110 ("gave that sum", "gave it ten minutes", "gave Lesca the mouth", "gave him the time"), "looked at" 66.
- ", and" 949 times, one sentence in five. With no semicolons or colons anywhere, it is the only joint the prose has. "Then" opens 123 sentences.
- ", with" as a trailing clause 186 times ("with her hand on the rail", "with her arm across her side", "with a back to each").
- The comparison "as she would have [verb] a [farm or office object]": "as she would have tried a bank" (8), "watched a man walk on a frozen pond" (9), "read it off a chart" (10), "weighed a bale" (15), "carried a sack of maize to a hide" (14), with a dozen cousins ("as a child is carried", "as a stage is", "as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another", all in chapter 14).
- Chapter 14 opens 19 sentences with "It was": "It was colder than the rain. It was moving"; "It was black. It was still in there".

**Do:** a search-and-vary pass on these six. Halve "stood". Let the flood chapter have two comparisons, not five.

### 8. The last chapter says the theme aloud (chapters 14 and 17)

Chapter 17, Coralie: "Stay is what you say to keep a thing as it is. I didn't ask what you'd keep. I've asked what you want, and that's another question, and you know it is." This is the book describing itself. Three paragraphs later it does the same thing properly: "What do you want, Maylis?" / "She's to be buried here on Tuesday. I want to dig it myself."

Kévin is handled the same way twice. Chapter 14: "'Five,' he said. He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." Chapter 17: "I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything as much." One of these is enough, and it is the second.

**Do:** cut Coralie's gloss to "I didn't ask what you'd keep." Cut the card simile in chapter 14 and leave "Five" bare.

### 9. Places where the narrator stops sounding like the narrator

- Chapter 15: "she watched her sentence go back through six thousand people at a walk". The village has a card table of regulars, forty houses in its lower half and "two hundred chairs in this square". Six thousand breaks the scale the book has kept to the centimetre, in the paragraph that most needs to be believed.
- Chapter 9: "A mayor, it turned out, signed a great deal." The only wink at the reader in the book.
- Chapter 14: six standalone clock lines ("02:59." "03:03."). Elsewhere a figure appears only when a character reads it off a screen, and time is otherwise in words ("nine minutes to three"). The device works for pace; give each figure its screen or accept that this chapter has a different narrator.
- Chapter 6, the winch: a 93-word run-on with an explanation inside it ("since that give had put her into the rail a week before and she was not going there twice"). The run-ons in chapters 12 and 14 are pure action and work. This one buries the event that matters, Lesca giving Maylis her own scar, in the middle of a subordinate clause.

**Do:** "the whole square and the road behind it" for the crowd; cut "it turned out"; move the "since" clause out of the winch sentence.

### 10. Consistency of usage

- **Eleven or twelve.** Maïté, chapter 8: "I've stood here with you for eleven years." Maïté, chapter 16: "I've stood at that stone for twelve years" and "nothing from you for twelve Decembers". Lesca has stood there "eleven times"; Coralie has "eleven Decembers". The flood is twelve years back and this is the twelfth commemoration, so there have been eleven. Fix chapter 16.
- **Four winters.** Jeannot, chapter 7: "Four winters you've held that key and looked the other way." She wrote about the winch in 2011 and has greased it since 2013. He means four winters of nets; the sentence says four winters of the key.
- **-body and -one.** The narration uses nobody / somebody / anybody 71 times and the -one forms 5 times. "No one came to tell him" is a fixed line and should be the only "no one" in the book. Chapter 4 has both forms in adjacent sentences: "until someone asked. Nobody had asked." Chapter 1: "to anyone who came late".
- **Telephone.** 73 times, for an object that in 2026 shows call logs and is held up to film a stage. "Mobile" appears once, "phone" once. With "lamps", "headlamps" and "waterproof" it dates the book to about 1965. That is a choice, and it costs a moment of doubt in chapter 15 ("telephones rose over the heads"). Keep it or change it, but decide.
- **"Paced".** Chapter 8, "She paced it without meaning to. It was four steps", is done on foot. Chapter 17, "Maylis paced it, from the hatch to that door. It was nearer a kilometre and two hundred", is done in her head. Use another verb for the second.

Clean, for the record: British spelling throughout (grey, towards, metres, judgement, manoeuvre); "the Port", "Madame le maire", "mairie", "tonne" and "loto" each in one form; italics only for written words; "Inguma" withheld from Lesca's chapters until her mother names it; dialogue tags almost all "said". The usual marks of formula writing are absent: no semicolons, no exclamation marks, no ellipses, three dashes (all for speech broken off), one "It was not X. It was Y" in the whole book. The faults above are the book's own mannerisms, not borrowed ones.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 11, the night at the gate.** All of it, and above all: "The reflector burned in the lamps at the end of the track for four minutes more. Then the water came up the door to it and put it out." And Jeannot's six numbered points ending "And six. Bugger off."
2. **Chapter 3, the association at the winch.** Six men, four umbrellas, a ewe from 1971, and "'It wants grease,' he said. 'It's had grease since 2013.'"
3. **Chapter 14 from the corridor to "Forty-one".** Daguerre's "Here", the man who thinks it is Thursday ("Then they'll be wanting the bins out"), room twelve, "a hatbox, and nothing else", and the empty chair in the hall that the dead's loto prepared.
4. **The confession and how it travels.** "Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him." / "The loudspeaker on the dyke road said drowned him when the square had finished hearing it." Then Marie-Jo: "Somebody has to stack them."
5. **The oven and the last line.** Coralie doing Larrère into the oven in chapter 1, failing to in chapter 17 ("'The—'"), and "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it."
