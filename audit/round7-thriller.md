# Round 7: Thriller

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

Verdict against the bar: **below it.** Chapters 11 to 15 are at the bar and in one respect over it. Chapters 1 to 10 are a 6. As a thriller the whole is a 6.5 against a bar of 8.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** They are countable and never inflated. "Behind it were forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." (2)
- **Tension: 6.** 12 to 14 are a 9. The run-in drops the clock: the river crossing the red line is reported after the fact. "On Tuesday the river had reached the line, and Pau had sent a yellow." (12)
- **Progression: 6.** The heroine's largest change of intention happens between chapters, in a subordinate clause. "At five that morning a man had sworn at her down a telephone, alive, and she no longer meant to wake in anybody's bed but her own." (12)
- **Comedy: 7.** Where it works it carries plot. "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade.'" (7)
- **Characters: 8.** "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'" (9)
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." (14)
- **Prose: 7.** "Then the water came up the door to it and put it out." (11) It loses a point each time the narrator names what the scene has shown (see fault 3) and for "priced", which is used until it is a tic.
- **The ending: 7.** Maylis's half pays on the page: "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (17) Lesca's half is postponed to a Monday and a Tuesday the book does not contain.
- **Clarity: 4.** "'If I'm the one left sitting, I wake in your bed, and my body lies in the van with its eyes open and nobody at the gate,' Maylis said. 'If I'm not, the telephone wakes me in the van, and it's my gate and my night.'" (8) I read that exchange three times and still could not say who had agreed to what.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes, but I stopped twice: at the winch inspection in 3 and at the ledger in 10. From the nine rings in 11 I could not stop.

**Would I press it on a friend?** Not on the friend who reads Crouch for the ride. That reader is gone by chapter 3.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, on the thriller lens.** Three things put it there, and one pulls the other way.

1. **Speed and plainness of the premise: below.** Dark Matter has its man taken at gunpoint in the first chapter and asked "Are you happy with your life?"; the box's rules are said once, in plain speech, before anyone uses it. Pines has its man unable to leave town before the reader has settled. Here the premise can be deduced by the end of 2, but it is given as a riddle ("Forty letters needed a man alive to post them. Her name was Maylis Lesca too."), the rules come in 5 as a mock council sitting, and two more rules turn up at the climax itself. A Recursion reader will also recognise the chair and the memory that is not hers; what is this book's own is Inguma and the counting, and those are the parts it explains least.
2. **Chapter-end pressure: below.** A Crouch chapter closes on a door opening onto a worse room, nearly every time. Here 2, 4, 5, 6, 12, 13 and 14 do that. 9, 10 and 16 close at rest, and 10 is the eve of the flood.
3. **Fear for the marriage: level.** Dark Matter frightens by putting the wrong man in the house with the wife. This book does the same and then goes one further: the wife prefers the wrong one and says why. "'I'd have this one again.'" (6) That is a worse fear than Crouch's, and the book has it.
4. **The cost of the choice: above.** Crouch lets the family out through the box. The Good Place plays the trolley problem for laughs with a fake trolley. Here the handle is real, the heroine chooses the opposite track to her double, somebody dies anyway, and a neighbour then names the free option she walked past in daylight. The 02:51 on the telephone in 10 re-reads the first line of the book the way the first-season reversal of The Good Place re-reads its premise. It is smaller, but it is the same kind of reversal.

**Where I was afraid, and of what.** In 4, of a hand being taken. In 6, of the fourth stair. In 9, of Coralie saying yes. In 12, of "*Etcheto back in 12*". In 14, of every timestamp. Between 9 and 11 I was afraid of nothing.

**Where a Crouch reader puts it down.** At the winch in 3, at the conditionals in 8, or at the stock sheet in 10.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The rules of crossing are not known when the climax depends on them. (5, 8, 10, 13)**
"Maylis had been awake when Inguma pinned her, so the body was still hers. Lesca, coming in from the chair, got one arm, the right, from the shoulder to the bound hand." (13)
This rule is invented at the moment it decides the book. So is sitting on Inguma, and so is its price: the hall of the dead arrives in 10 with "One chair stood empty, at a place laid for whoever died that night", and nobody says that the chair could be Lesca's. A reader cannot fear an outcome she cannot predict. In 13 I learned that Lesca had risked her life only once she was already standing in the hall.
*Do:* before the eleventh, in a scene with less at stake, establish three things in plain words. What happens to a woman who is awake at 02:51. That Inguma takes the chair when the two of them quarrel. That whoever sits on it is called to the empty place. Then 13 is a known price paid. Replace the four conditionals in 8 with one flat statement: one chair, and on the eleventh one of them gets the gate and the other goes home.

**2. The clock stops for four days at the two-thirds mark. (9 to 10)**
"'The river reached the line yesterday. Larrère said the gate stays shut and the Port knows it has a river. You told him he'd say it.'" (10)
The red line is the book's one gauge of dread, built a reading at a time since 4. The river crosses it offstage, and it is reported twice, in 10 and again in 12. The sixth to the ninth of December are not in the book. The chapter that stands in their place gives its middle to a vote on who speaks at a ceremony and to "*Nil.*"
*Do:* play Tuesday the eighth on Maylis's side. The river at the line, the yellow sheet, Larrère at his few centimetres of window saying the sentence he was told a week before that he would say. A prophecy kept word for word is a scare, and the book has set one up and then spent it in the pluperfect. Cut the Received and Removed columns.

**3. The moment the whole plot turns on is three sentences, and the narrator explains it. (12)**
"Maylis did not get out and fit the handle, since nobody had told her to. She knew that for the word she wrote on the shopping pad. It was Either."
At five it is daylight, Jeannot is above ground at her window, the key is on her belt. This is the option that costs nobody a life, and 17 hangs its verdict on it: "'At five on Friday it was light, and Castagnet was up on the dyke at your van window... You had it on your belt then.'" It should be the scene the reader shouts at. It is a paragraph inside another scene, and its last sentence tells the reader the theme.
*Do:* make it a scene. The box unlocked, the handle out, the telephone on the dashboard, the folder with her name on its tab, Jeannot letting his clutch up while she stands there. Let him drive off because she is still standing. Cut "It was Either"; Coralie says it better in 17.

**4. Chapter 13 stalls on the best cliffhanger in the book. (13)**
"At eleven a young man came from the prefecture at Pau to walk her through her ceremony."
12 ends on a telephone ringing under a pinned woman. 13 then goes back sixteen hours for tape on tarmac, a seating plan and a day book. I skimmed to get back to the van.
*Do:* put Lesca's Friday daytime before Maylis's Friday, so that the ringing telephone cuts straight to "Lesca was standing. The panel was in front of her". Keep Arlette's ponies, which the rescue needs. The eight names can go where the stock sheet is now.

**5. The heroine decides to desert her village and the reader never fears it. (8, 9, 11)**
"Maylis still meant to be the one sitting when Lesca woke on Saturday, and to wake as mayor in Lesca's bed, with nobody at her own gate." (11)
This is the darkest thing either woman intends, and it is stated as a plan, in a sentence, three times. Nothing shows what "nobody at her own gate" would look like, though the book had the chance: on the Wednesday in 8 her body lay thirteen hours with its eyes open and the only cost shown is Coralie's missed shift.
*Do:* on that Wednesday, have the gate wanted and not turned. A call from the car, unanswered, and water where it should not be on Thursday morning. Then her plan for the eleventh has a picture, and striking out the handover is a rescue the reader has been waiting for.

**6. Chapter 3 drops the hook of chapter 2 for the longest chapter so far. (3)**
"'It isn't the winch at all,' said the oldest of them, who had an umbrella to himself. 'It's the gate. There's something in the slot. In seventy-one it was a ewe.'"
2 ends on "It had not had it yet." 3 returns to a woman who knows less than the reader and stays with her through Christmas lights, a care-home corridor and seven men at a handle. Every item is a plant that pays later (the fingers, room twelve, nineteen, Hontaube), but the reader does not know that yet and has only been promised a flood.
*Do:* halve the inspection. Keep nineteen, "The keyholder has no preference", and Hontaube's van. Let Maylis reach the chair and the card before the dyke, not after it, so that she is catching the reader up while the committee talks.

**7. The cause of the second flood is asked, argued and dropped, and nobody tries the obvious. (5, 13)**
"'Then it had rained two days before you did anything.'" (5)
The two women spend twelve lines on why 2014's river is in 2026, settle on "'Then it's the winch'" and never return to it. In 13 Arlette gives the remedy outright: "'On your side, Maylis. Tonight. With your knees up, so it has nowhere to sit.'" A thriller heroine with a fortnight would have tried that on day two.
*Do:* one paragraph. Maylis sleeps a night on her side, rib or no rib, and nothing sits on her. In the morning the gauge reads what the file reads. That closes the question and makes the river worse, since it no longer needs her.

**8. Nothing moves against the heroine. (7, 12)**
"'You'd have your years of pension in a commune that stopped existing at midnight. You could write to it.'" (12)
Larrère is a voice through a gap in a car window, and his weapon is a sheet of paper. That is right for the comedy. For the thriller it means that the opposition is trays and forms, and the one thing that pursues anybody, Inguma, only ever takes them home. The reader is asked to believe a pension would hold her at 02:55, and it does not hold her for a line.
*Do:* give him one act on the Friday. Put his car on the dyke at five, beside her van, for the scene in fault 3, and then let the reader watch which road he takes home.

**9. Chapter 16 tells Lesca a secret the reader has had for two chapters. (16)**
"'He rang you from here. Guy. From that telephone.'"
The reader had it in 14: "Under the time it said Castagnet, house." The kitchen scene carries one new thing, the verb, and it is the best single word in the book. The rest is recital.
*Do:* cut Maïté's account of the affair to a look at the telephone and the clock above it. Keep *left*, keep the garden gate and "'I'll be brief'" with nothing after it.

**10. Lesca's bill is not presented. (15, 16)**
"'The pillow stays where it is. After Tuesday, when they've voted. I'm not carrying it in to the mayor.'"
She hands back a medal, a sash and three keys without a flinch, since she never wanted them. The thing she wanted is neither given nor refused, and the statement at Bayonne and the vote are both after the last page. Maylis loses her post, her name in the café and Coralie, all on the page. The two halves do not cost alike.
*Do:* let Coralie answer on the Saturday night, either way, or end 16 with one line from Tuesday. A smaller matter in the same chapter: "six thousand people" (15) in a village with one café and two hundred chairs broke my belief at the moment it was most needed.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The minute.** "'Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one.'" (10), with "It rang once" (11) and "'Once,' she said. 'I let it go to nine. He answered on the ninth.'" (15).
2. **Chapter 14 as built.** The timestamps, "'Both.'", the van in the dip that he warned her of, the dry cellar, the key thrown back along the crown, five fingers at four o'clock, the mark on the wallpaper a metre above the pillow, "'Forty-one.'"
3. **Coralie preferring the one who answers, and sending her down the stairs all the same.** The soup in 6 and the landing in 9.
4. **The sentence at the microphone and its echo.** "The loudspeaker on the dyke road said drowned him when the square had finished hearing it." Then Marie-Jo and the two hundred chairs.
5. **Jeannot Castagnet from first to last.** "'I'll go down Friday week because you've said it'", "'Jean's for the stone'", "'And six. Bugger off'", and number forty-two: "'It's four sides, and it doesn't thank you.'" With it, the verb *left*.
