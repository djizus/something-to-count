# Critic's report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

Verdict: not at the bar. As comedy it is a 6.5 against The Good Place's 8. The running gags are built as well as that show builds them. The laughs per page, the range of comic voices and the reader's share in the joke are all short of it.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 7.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." (2). Stated once, exactly, and every later scene draws on it. Maylis's own stake, a pension in Hontaube, is small until 12.
- **Tension: 7.** "The telephone began to ring in her hand." (12). Chapters 11 to 14 hold at the level of the comparison. Chapters 2 and 4 have none.
- **Progression: 6.** "First business is how this works, so that it's said once and we both have it the same." (5). The premise is not in the reader's hands until a quarter of the book has gone.
- **Comedy: 6.5.** "Eggs is what Either gets in this house." (1). Dry, exact, well built, and almost all in one register.
- **Characters: 7.5.** "There's a key, and a Trafic, and Guy, and then there's me, as a distance." (9). Coralie is the funniest person in the book and the one I would miss.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'Who's dead?' said Jeannot Castagnet." (11). The dead man answers his telephone with the café's joke.
- **Prose: 7.** "She had the sum and nothing else." (10). Exact and unshowy, at one tempo from the demon to the eggs.
- **The ending: 7.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (17). The last image is the best dark joke in the book. Chapter 16 stands between the climax and it.
- **Clarity: 5.** "'Yours won't price,' she said. 'I've tried it.' 'My what?'" (8). Lesca has to ask, and so did I.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. From 6 onward I wanted to; 2 and 4 I read on duty.
**Pressed on a friend?** On the friend who reads Penelope Fitzgerald, yes. On the friend who was promised The Good Place, no.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below The Good Place. Crouch is not funny, so there is no contest there, but the comedy is bought with his speed.**

1. **One way of being wrong, where the show has five.** Eleanor is selfish, Chidi is paralysed, Tahani is vain, Jason is dim, Michael is gleeful, and the laughs come from the friction. Here Sarthou, Madame Ducasse, Tastet, the woman at Hontaube, the young man from Pau, Larrère and Lesca all make the same joke: procedure applied to a thing procedure cannot hold. It is a good joke told by seven mouths.
2. **The reader is the last to know.** The show tells the audience in five minutes that Eleanor does not belong, and plays a season of farce off those who have not been told. This book has a farce of that kind, the mayor and the drowned man's wife, and hides it from the reader until 14.
3. **Level, and once above, on the gag that becomes the argument.** Maylis is Chidi. "Either" is his stomach ache, and "Both" (14) damns her as his almond milk damns him. The loto of 9 turned into the hall of the dead, with "Eighty-six. Eight, six" coming back as "Twelve. One, two" (15), is better made than the show's afterlife. Kévin counting five fingers in the flood belongs with these.

The reversal: that 02:51 is the minute of the call re-reads the demon as a conscience. It is honest and it lands. It does not re-read the comedy, as the show's first-season turn does.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The farce is kept from the reader. (5, 6, 7; paid in 14 and 16.)**
"Two bowls were drying on it, and two spoons" and a folder "she had seen … held over a head, crossing the square to the mairie, on most wet mornings." Then Larrère, next morning: "He sat at home last night like a dog in a doorway, and nobody could get near the house."
That second line is the funniest in the book once you know he is complaining that the husband stayed in. On a first reading it is noise. The commune's girl has carried funeral flowers into the kitchen of the mayor's mistress with his folder still on the dresser, and the clue is set at the pitch of a crossword. Farce needs the reader ahead of the people in it.
*Do:* say whose head the folder was held over, in 5. Then 6 and 7 play as farce with the reader ahead of Maylis, and "Castagnet, house" on the screen in 14 is a dread fulfilled and not a puzzle solved. This is the change worth most.

**2. The gag is not at the scene of the failure. (12, charged in 17.)**
"She lifted the handle off the shaft, laid it back on its rag and locked the box." Four sentences, in daylight, with Jeannot above ground at her window. Madame Darrigrand names it as the real charge: "At five on Friday it was light, and Castagnet was up on the dyke at your van window." But the book hangs its comic spine on the telephone call nine hours later ("Both"), and Coralie's verdict, "That was Either, with the river in it", lands on that.
The moral problem is made funny in one place and committed in another.
*Do:* make the platform at five a full scene with the book's word in it. Jeannot is watching; let him ask which. Then the joke the reader has laughed at for eleven chapters is in her mouth at the moment it kills.

**3. Seven people, one comic voice. (Throughout.)**
"I cannot file a forecast." "There's no column." "I've got a tray for each, and a book." "It's a line on the sheet." "I have that as consent."
Any of these could be given to any of the others.
*Do:* keep procedure for the three who own it: Lesca, whose tragedy it is; Larrère, who uses it as a weapon; Madame Ducasse, who means it innocently. Sarthou already has his own wrongness, a man who will do anything to retire with no file open; play the cowardice and drop the diction. Make Tastet timid. Cut the Hontaube call to a third. Give more to Lafitte and the owner's wife, who are gossip, a different key, and underused.

**4. The narrator does not laugh, and summarises where the comedy wants a scene. (9 most of all.)**
"At the interval the hall came to the bar, and for twenty minutes Lesca had its faces in front of her, three deep."
That is the whole village in front of two women in one body, in one sentence. The narrator can do it: "The rule applied to all members and had been needed for one" (2); "A mayor, it turned out, signed a great deal" (9). There are perhaps six such sentences in the book.
*Do:* allow the narrator that pitch in every village scene, and play the interval at the bar in full.

**5. The shared body is reported, not played. (6, 9.)**
"Maylis took the mouth from her for one sentence." The contest for a mouth and a right hand is the richest comic machine the premise has, and it runs at full length once, in 8: the knees that will not bend, "Is she ill?" "She's counting something. Leave her", the hand sitting on the other hand while Larrère talks. After that it is a clause.
*Do:* one more public scene at that length, and the loto bar is where it belongs.

**6. Chapter 16 settles five accounts in summary. (16.)**
"Then Lesca told her that she had twice asked the other Coralie back across a landing, with Maylis's mouth, and that the second time that Coralie had said she would have her and had sent her down the stairs."
This is the confession the love story has waited for, given as indirect speech. The chapter ends "That night Coralie carried her pillow back across the landing", one sentence for what Maylis's Coralie gets a whole kitchen and an oven door to refuse. It has one laugh, and that is a silence.
*Do:* stage the landing in dialogue. Give this Coralie one joke of her own before she comes back; at present she has "Madame le maire" and nothing else.

**7. The rules are given three times, and the first time they stop a comic scene. (3, 4, 5.)**
In 3 the giggles outside room twelve are the warmest comedy in the book. Then Madame Etcheto recites the mechanism: "When it sits on two at once it has them in one seat. The one who wakes first takes the other home in her." Lesca gives the same sentences in 5, where they are funny, because it is a sitting with nobody in the chair. Maylis then glosses each: "So whoever sleeps on rides the other."
*Do:* leave Etcheto the name, the counting and the threat. Let 5 carry the mechanism. Make Maylis's glosses hers, priced or wrong, not paraphrase.

**8. The hall of the dead is explained before it is seen. (10, then 13.)**
"It was a loto, and the players were the village's dead. … A visitor Inguma could not fetch would be called to it."
The reader left the living loto one chapter back and would know the waxed boards and the voice at once. The narrator gets there first, and 13 then has to describe it again.
*Do:* in 10 give the strip lights, the voice, her father with his watch beside his card, and stop.

**9. Jokes that need decoding. (5, 6, 8.)**
"'That's the pot explained,' said the owner's wife." "'That's so we'd not think it,' said Lafitte." "Lesca declined that. Maylis saw how it was done. She did the same herself, with a shorter word."
What the café believes about Maylis and Castagnet is never said aloud by anyone. Gossip is funny when one fool states the theory.
*Do:* let the owner's wife say it in plain words, once, in 6.

**10. Two routines run long at the wrong moment. (9, 14.)**
Lafitte reads the whole score pad again at the loto, about ninety words of what the reader already has, for one laugh: "Wednesday. Not seen." In 14, with the water at the knees and seven chairs left, Jeannot gives three numbered points and "I put it in number six." It is the one place a joke slows a dark scene.
*Do:* Lafitte reads the new entries only. Jeannot keeps "It's up a hand" and "They took note", which is the whole book's bitterness in three words, and loses the numbering.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **"Either", from the eggs to the river.** Every use costs more than the last: no preference minuted (3), "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade" (7), "I have that as consent" (8), "Both" (14).
2. **Larrère's window and "I'll be brief".** Coralie doing him into the oven (1); the window going all the way down (6); "At nine minutes he began to slow. He had been saving it since 2015, and there was less of it than he had thought" (8); nothing coming at the gate (16); the oven door shut on "The—" (17).
3. **The home's comedy and where it goes.** "Staff to wait. Staff not to join in" (3). Monsieur Daguerre's "Here", through "I know you are" in the flood, to the nineteenth sandbag where "Nobody called", to "Forty-one." Lesca in the hall of the dead: "The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies."
4. **Jeannot's letters.** "Put it in forty-two"; the seventeenth letter that makes a mayor laugh in a minute's silence (8); nine rings and "And six. Bugger off" (11); number forty-two delivered by hand, and the man who has a stone in the other village getting down into a grave in his waders.
5. **The square after the sentence. (15.)** "Beyond the last loudspeaker a man began to clap, taking her to have finished." Tastet turning the sheet over "to see whether it went on." Marie-Jo and the two hundred chairs. Lesca correcting the summons for her own removal: "I can't sign a summons to nowhere."
