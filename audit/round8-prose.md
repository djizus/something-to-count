# Critic's report: Prose

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full (44,400 words). Nothing else in the directory was opened. Counts below are from the text.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** "It was twenty-five minutes on foot to that hatch. The bank had ten." (11) The sum is as clean as any dilemma in the comparison books, and it is stated in fourteen words.
- **Tension: 7.** "Tonight Lesca meant to be sitting when the telephone woke Maylis, and to wake at the gate inside her. She meant to sit on Inguma itself, so that it could never fetch her home." (13) The book tells its best move a page before it makes it. Chapters 11 to 14 are a 9. The ten before them spend their tension in advance.
- **Progression: 6.** "On Saturday it rained. Lesca woke in Maylis's bed and drove Maylis's body, and Maylis had her right hand." (9) That is the opening sentence of chapter 6 again. The third crossing arrives on the same terms as the first.
- **Comedy: 8.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade.'" (7)
- **Characters: 8.** "'Because you'd have told me.'" (10) Four words carry twelve years of a marriage.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** "'It's Saturday.' / 'Then they'll be wanting the bins out,' he said." (14) Said in a flooded corridor, and the scene loses nothing by it.
- **Prose: 7.** "The loudspeaker on the dyke road said drowned him when the square had finished hearing it." (15) At its best the sentence-making is an 8 or 9. The tics, the recited rule and the synopsis passages listed below pull it to a 7.
- **The ending: 7.** "She heard the passage, and the front door, and the car door shut on a pillow." (17) Chapter 17 is an 8. Chapter 16 is a 6, because its two hardest speeches are summarised.
- **Clarity: 5.** "That village had not been spared December 2014. It had not had it yet." (2) The premise turns on a sentence built from two negatives and a "had not had".

Would I have finished it unpaid? Yes, from the end of chapter 4. Before that I was reading on trust.

Would I press it on a friend? On a friend who reads for sentences, yes. On the reader the bar names, who knows Crouch, no. That reader stops in chapter 2 or between 7 and 9.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Sentence for sentence: above Crouch.** Crouch names the feeling and moves on. This book never names one. It gives a pad with two boxes, a kitchen timer, a pillow, a tall L, and lets them do the work an adjective does in Dark Matter. There is no dash in the narration, no semicolon, almost no adverb. The invented rural English ("and all", "were sat", "to ask is he in") holds for seventeen chapters without one slip into the modern.

**Dialogue: level with The Good Place.** Everyone is wrong in a private idiom. Larrère: "I was in the room, yes. I was in the room as mayor." Sarthou: "I cannot file a forecast." Madame Ducasse: "There's nothing here that lets me un-receive it. There's no column." The reversal is level too. "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." (10) makes the reader go back to "No one came to tell him" (2) and find it was always an evasion.

**Transparency: below both.** A Crouch reader never reads a sentence twice. Here the premise (2), the rule of the chair (5) and the question of who holds which limb (6, 13) each need a second pass. Dark Matter gives its mechanism once, in plain talk. The Good Place gives each rule once, through someone with a reason to say it, and from then on only breaks it. This book gives its rule about thirteen times, in the same folk formula each time, and it is still the thing a reader is least sure of.

Net, for this lens: above on craft, below on legibility, and legibility is what the bar's reader came for. Below the bar as it stands.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The rule is recited, never trusted. (5, 6, 7, 9, 12, 13; structural)**
"'Two. The one who wakes first takes the other home in her.'" (5) Then Maylis paraphrases each of Lesca's three points back to her: "'So we're both in it'", "'So whoever sleeps on rides the other'", "'So it fetches you when it's done counting.'" Then the narrator says it again at 5's close ("Lesca had stayed sitting, and came home with her"), at 6's opening, at 7's close, at 9's opening, in 12 ("so that nobody was sitting when the telephone woke her"), and three times in chapter 13 alone. "had the driving of her" appears three times. The repetition adds no clarity because it is the same compressed formula each time, never a plainer one.
*Do:* keep the sitting in chapter 5, which is funny ("Those in favour."). Keep one of Maylis's three paraphrases, the plain one: "So whoever sleeps on rides the other." After chapter 6, state the rule nowhere in narration. Show a hand shutting, and the reader has it.

**2. The three hardest speeches are given as synopsis. (15, 16; structural)**
"Maylis told her in order what the five words had come to, from the dry cellar and the van sunk in the dip to Madame Etcheto drowned in her bed." (15) "She told her what had not been in the speech, that the night before she had ridden Maylis at the old gate and sent her to the tonne first, and that the bank had gone into the home meanwhile and drowned Madame Etcheto in room twelve." (16) "Then Lesca told her that she had twice asked the other Coralie back across a landing, with Maylis's mouth…" (16) The book writes out in full what nobody means: minutes, forms, a withdrawal that cannot be received. It then summarises a woman telling her counterpart that an old woman is dead of her five words, and telling her wife that she tried another woman's bed. Lesca's formal register is the book's finest instrument, and it is switched off exactly here.
*Do:* write all three as speech. Each is sixty words. Chapter 16 is under two thousand words and can carry them.

**3. The premise lands in the weakest sentences of the opening. (2)**
"He had been dead for twelve years less eighteen days." Then: "She had taken Jeannot Castagnet, alive, to mean a village that the flood had passed by. / It was 2026 in that village as well. But the rain that had taken eighteen days to drown a man had begun there that morning. / That village had not been spared December 2014. It had not had it yet." The reader has to do subtraction and then parse a double negative to learn what the book is about. Chapter 2 also runs at 26 uses of "had" per thousand words, the densest in the book, because most of it is backstory in the pluperfect.
*Do:* give the last line as a positive statement with a date in it. The flood is eighteen days off over there; say so. Chapter 4 shows how: "A thing dreamed would have changed. A thing remembered would have said *right*" is the same order of deduction and nobody reads it twice.

**4. The narrator briefs the reader before each move. (4, 7, 10, 11, 12, 13)**
"Tonight Lesca meant to be sitting when the telephone woke Maylis… She meant to sit on Inguma itself" (13). "Maylis still meant to sleep through Friday night at home, with the telephone off, and to ride Lesca to the stone on Saturday." (11) "She would be at that gate herself. When Inguma sat on her she would be on her feet in the steel room, between Lesca and the chair" (12). "When the telephone rang and the bank began to go, she would open the gate." (12) "She had two motions now, and they could not both stand." (10) "That left Friday. She had two days to settle what she took from it, and what she left there." (10) A Crouch chapter closes on an event or a fact just learned. These sections close on a statement of intent, so the next scene confirms what was promised.
*Do:* cut every one. The 13 briefing matters most. Lesca sitting down on the thing that has sat on her for twelve years is the best physical image in the book, and it has been announced fifty lines earlier.

**5. The choice is the muddiest paragraph in the book. (14)**
"She could not be the one who did not come. She knew who was under the bank, each of them by name." Everything round it is clocked and physical. Here, at the decision the whole book has walked to, the motive arrives as a double negative, and the next sentence pulls the other way with no hinge between them. The paragraph has eight sentences and faces four ways: the home, the telephone, the reflector, Friday's call.
*Do:* keep "If she went back she turned the handle on him, as Lesca had. If she went on, the river had the Port." Keep the telephone ringing with his name on it and the reflector. Cut the two quoted sentences. She gets out and goes on, and the reader who heard nine rings in chapter 11 knows why.

**6. The narrator explains echoes the reader has already heard. (6, 7, 8, 10, 14, 15, 16, 17)**
"He had left the little off. She heard it go." (7), and again at that chapter's end: "a man had left the little off her name because she had frightened him". "It was not her question. It had been put to her across a shopping pad for as long as she had had a kitchen, and she had never had the use of it." (8) "She said it to have the last word." (6) "Nobody at the table laughed. The same mouth had told them what came on the Friday." (10) "It buzzed, twice, against his chest, as it had on the dashboard of a tractor under the plane trees." (14) "He had moved again and again that the stone be given a verb, and they wanted to see him get one." (15) "He had telephoned that order from the Castagnet kitchen, and Maïté Castagnet sat across from him." (16) "It was the first job she had asked for." (17)
*Do:* cut all of them. The callbacks are well laid and need no caption. The one in 10 that reaches back four chapters ("He did not hear himself say it. Eight days before…") can keep a single clause.

**7. Pronoun fog when two women share a body, and loose access to other heads. (4, 6, 8, 9, 13, 14)**
"Lesca snatched at it with the hand that had left it" (6): the hand that let go is the right, which the rule gives to Maylis, so who is snatching? "'It hurts to hold,' she said." (6): which she? "Lesca was still sitting, so she woke inside her, at the wheel, and had the driving of her." (13) The steel-room scene in 10 begins in Maylis ("Maylis waited, with her weight on the left foot") and ends in Lesca ("Then Lesca was in her own bed"). Outside the pair, the narration drops into Coralie for one sentence ("She felt the pencil through the card of its back", 9) and into Jeannot at the home ("He had come out of a dry cellar half an hour before, by the neck, and had been told that the gate was to be opened on him", 14), which explains the "two breaths" that follow it.
*Do:* in a ridden scene, name the woman at every clause where a limb moves; never "she" for a hand. Keep the comic omniscience over Larrère, which is earned. Cut the Coralie and Jeannot sentences.

**8. The one vision is captioned as it is shown. (10)**
"It was a loto, and the players were the village's dead… One chair stood empty, at a place laid for whoever died that night. A visitor Inguma could not fetch would be called to it." Nobody in the scene knows these things. The narrator, who everywhere else knows only what the two women know, becomes a guide. It also disarms chapter 13, where the same hall is done properly: a voice reads "Lesca, Maylis" off a card, and she sends "apologies received".
*Do:* in 10 give what Maylis sees and stop: the tables, the heads down, the father with his watch beside his card, one empty place. The father tells the reader who they are. Cut the two rule sentences.

The clock openers cost most. Chapter 14's bare "03:11." should cut, and it has been blunted by fifty sections that began the same way. "had" as the verb for knowing, hearing and remembering is the keynote of the voice and it blurs what happened: was she told, did she see it, does she remember it?
*Do:* keep the bare clock for chapters 11 to 14 and open earlier sections on an object or a line of speech, as chapter 8 does. Keep "had" where something is held, or where the pun on having is live ("I've had one straight answer out of you, and that was me"). Keep three similes: the frozen pond (9), the card he had hoped was another (14), the bench when the person beside you stands (4). Keep "Yours won't price" (8) and the bag priced "at less than a morning's load for the tip" (17), and cut the other five.

**10. Consistency of usage and fact.**
- **Dyke, bank, old bank, old dyke, crown.** One structure, five names (32, 62, 6, 1, 22 uses). "the dyke was coming through" (11) and "the bank's coming through" (12) are the same event. A new reader takes them for two walls. Fix one noun for the earthwork and keep "crown" for its top.
- **Eighteen or nineteen.** "the rain that had taken eighteen days to drown a man" (2); "the nineteenth day of rain" (5, 11); "it rained for nineteen days" (15).
- **"Four winters you've held that key"** (7; echoed by Kévin in 17). She wrote about the winch "In 2011", was keyholder in the winter of 2014 in the other village, and has had the key "since she had it from her father" (14).
- **The hour of the call.** Lesca promises "your telephone rings at seven minutes to three" (8). In her own night (11) the order reaches her in bed, before the drive, the bank and the 02:51 call. Nothing she lived gives her that minute.
- **"Lesca" spoken aloud.** The surname is the narrator's label for the mayor. In 9 Maylis says "For Lesca" and the mayor introduces herself as "Lesca. The mayor." Nobody has agreed on the name in any scene.
- **"gate"** means the sluice and, in the same paragraph, the locked gate to its platform (12).
- **"wheelie bin"** (14) is the one modern coinage in a narration that otherwise could be 1950.
- **The last sentence.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." Only the turf has been cut. There is nothing yet to get down into, and "it" reaches back past "string" for a grave that has not been named in the sentence.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The telephone on the chest of drawers (10).** From "The morning after" to "Because you'd have told me." Including "'Nine minutes to three,' she said. 'I've had that for twelve years.'" Not a word.
2. **Larrère's eleven minutes (8).** "At four minutes he glanced at the timer, and went on with more confidence… He had been saving it since 2015, and there was less of it than he had thought."
3. **The confession and its journey through the square (15).** "Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him." through "A child asked what the lady had done, and was told." And Marie-Jo's "Somebody has to stack them."
4. **The home, from the yard to the count (14).** The bins, Monsieur Daguerre's "Here", Kévin's "Five", Coralie's two fingers on a wrist, a gesture the book has repeated since chapter 1 for this one use, and "'Forty-one,' Coralie said."
5. **Either, from the shopping pad to the oven door.** "Eggs is what Either gets in this house" (1), "That was Either, with the river in it" (17), and the impression that fails: "'I'll be brief,' she said… 'The—'" (17). With it, the three long run-on sentences at the winch (6), in the van (12) and on the track (14), which are the only places the syntax lets go and are right each time.
