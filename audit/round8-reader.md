# Round 8 — Reader

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `17.md`, once, in order, in full. 44,400 words. Nothing else opened but the brief.

Lens: an ordinary reader with no special knowledge of France, local government, rivers, Basque folklore or the makers' intentions.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes, from the last line of chapter 4. In chapters 1 and 3 I was reading on trust.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On the friend who reads short, dry English comic novels, yes. On the friend who reads Crouch, no. I would have to warn them to read slowly, and nobody presses a book with a warning.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below, overall. About a 7 where the bar is 8.** The last third (11, 12, 14, 15, 17) is at the bar. The first two thirds are not.

Three things put it there.

1. **Crouch tells the rules once, plainly, then breaks them. This book tells them three times and I still stopped at the climax to work them out.** Madame Etcheto gives them in 3, Lesca drafts them into a motion in 4, Lesca recites them as three points in 5. By chapter 13 I had to hold: lie still and you are in the chair; sitting you get her memories, standing you can talk; whoever wakes first takes the other home; the rider drives but the host keeps the right hand and sometimes the mouth; Inguma fetches when the counting is done; if it cannot fetch you die in your bed; sit on Inguma and it cannot fetch; and there is a bingo hall of the dead with one empty chair. Dark Matter has a box and a drug. Recursion has a chair. I could tell a friend either in a sentence. I could not tell a friend this.

2. **Crouch has his man living the other life by a fifth of the way in and keeps him there. Here the other life is three days.** The other village arrives fast, on page one of chapter 2, faster than Crouch. The first full day in the other woman's body starts at 24% (chapter 6). After that there are two more such days (8, 9), about 8,000 words in all, 18% of the book. I paid to learn nine rules and got three days' use of them.

3. **The Good Place puts its people in a room and lets them be wrong at each other. The two leads here are face to face for about 2,500 words, under 6% of the book.** Their first meeting ("'Those in favour.' / 'There's two of us.'") is the funniest thing in it, and the book never gives me that again at length.

Where it is **level or above**: the reversal. "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." (10) re-reads the first sentence of the book and every 02:51 after it, as a Good Place reversal does. And the ending costs more than Dark Matter's: a named old woman I liked is dead because the better half tried to atone, and the book does not let anyone off.

One more thing a Crouch reader will say, and the book cannot answer it: a steel box built by a university, one of you in each world, and the other one coming over for your wife. That is Dark Matter's box moved to a French village. What is new here is that both women want to swap and that the village's paperwork is funnier than the physics. The first belongs to the half of the book that is missing (fault 1).

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Half the swap is missing. (8, and the empty days between 9 and 10)**

> 'You didn't argue,' Coralie said. She waited for the argument, and it did not come. 'Then go.'

That is the whole of Maylis's time with the other Coralie. Lesca gets a breakfast, a soup, a yellow post box and a landing with Maylis's Coralie. Maylis gets one doorway. She spends one day in the mayor's life and spends most of it failing to speak. Then the book asks me to believe she wants that life enough to sleep through a flood with Coralie "thirty metres from the patch" in order to have it for a day. I did not believe it. I was told ("'I want that,' Maylis said", over a register) and then told it was off (11).

What I would do: give Maylis a second crossing. The calendar has room, since chapter 9 ends on Saturday the 5th and chapter 10 opens on Tuesday the 8th. Put her in the house where Coralie sleeps across the landing. The woman who never asks, facing a Coralie who has stopped being asked, is the scene the book has been building since "Either gets eggs", and it is not there. It would make her plan for the flood night frightening and not just stated, and it would spend the rules I had to learn. It costs five or six thousand words in a book that is short by that much.

**2. The two women's story ends before either ending has happened, and Inguma leaves before the confession. (15)**

> They both knew whose it was. Neither of them looked at the chair Lesca had taken, and neither sat. The door stayed shut, and Inguma did not come back.

This is their last scene. It is three hundred words, at six on Saturday morning. Five hours later Lesca says "I drowned him" into a microphone. A day later Maylis is told she "had the key and gone for a walk". Neither ever learns what the other did. The relationship the book is about stops short of both climaxes.

It also muddies why the thing goes. At the microphone Lesca "waited for the weight on her breastbone" and nothing came, but I already knew it had gone out by the door at six. So what lifted it? Not the confession. I do not know.

What I would do: move Inguma's exit to the night after the confession. One last sitting at 02:51 on Sunday, both on their feet, each telling the other what her Saturday came to. Then the door. The thing that came at the minute of the one ring leaves once the ring has been said aloud, and the reader can see why. Chapter 17's opening changes from a bare wardrobe to that meeting.

**3. The book brakes at its fastest moment. (12 into 13)**

> At nine minutes to three the roof of the van took a weight. […] The telephone began to ring in her hand.

Then sixteen hours backwards, to tape on a stage and a seating plan. The eight survivors and Arlette counting her ponies are both needed. Arlette's is the trigger for the whole climax. But I read them with half a mind, waiting for the van, and they deserve better than that. Then the ring is replayed a third time at the top of 14.

What I would do: put Lesca's Friday before Maylis's night. Lesca's day, rehearsal to lying down at 02:49, becomes the chapter before the dyke. Then Maylis's night runs straight from the ring into the steel room into the van, in one line, once.

**4. Too many rules, told too often, and still no account of what the thing is. (3, 4, 5, 10, 13)**

> One chair stood empty, at a place laid for whoever died that night. A visitor Inguma could not fetch would be called to it.

Nobody in that room knows this. The narrator hands it to me. It is the third system in the book: a university's steel box, a Basque thing that sits on sleepers and counts, and now a bingo hall of the dead. Nobody ever says how they join. The thing on the wardrobe is made of tobacco leaf and riveted plate, so it is made of the barn. A ninety-four-year-old woman who was never in the barn has it too. Which is it? And chapter 5 asks out loud why the rain came to Maylis's village ("'Then it had rained two days before you did anything'") and then leaves it.

What I would do: tell the rules once, in the steel room in 5, and cut the retellings in 4 down to what Lesca needs for her motion. Put the hall of the dead in Madame Etcheto's mouth in 3, where "they find her dead in her bed" already is. Have one person say in one line why the thing wears the barn.

**5. The plans for the flood night are set in riddles. (8, last scene; 9)**

> 'Then I'll sit,' Maylis said. 'It's where the sash is.'

This scene decides the plot: each woman says what she will do on the night of the eleventh. I read it three times. Lesca's plan needs Maylis to wake first. Maylis's plan needs Lesca to wake first. That is the whole contest and the book never says it in a sentence. Then chapter 9 says "On Saturday it rained. Lesca woke in Maylis's bed and drove Maylis's body", two nights after Maylis said she would be the one to sit, and I do not know whether Maylis allowed it, lost, or forgot.

The same habit elsewhere: "'That's the pot explained,'" (6) — explained how? I still cannot say whether the café thinks Maylis is after Jeannot, after his wife, or mad. "the coat you gave him for his six months" (15) — six months of what?

What I would do: one plain sentence each. "If she sat and I woke, she had me. If I sat and she woke, I had her." Let the café say what it thinks.

**6. Two women, three names, and the names swap hands. (7, 16, 17)**

> 'That's not like you, Lesca,' he said.

The narrator calls the keyholder Maylis and the mayor Lesca. I learned that. But Jeannot calls the keyholder "Lesca", and Arlette, Coralie and Marie-Jo call the mayor "Maylis" ("Go to bed, Maylis", 16). When one is riding the other, the people in the room use a third pattern. The last spoken line of the book is "You'll not get that down two metres with one arm, Lesca", said to Maylis. I know it is the payoff of "He had left the little off." I still read it, for a second, as the wrong woman.

What I would do: keep the payoff. Thin the cross-use elsewhere. In the mayor's village let people say "Madame le maire" or nothing, except the two or three places where "Maylis" is the point (Arlette's "I'm the last that uses it", Marie-Jo on the steps). In the keyholder's village keep "little Lesca" until chapter 7 drops it, and mark the drop harder so that I am carrying it in chapter 17.

**7. Chapter 1 asks for more than a first chapter can ask. (1)**

> 'Still in the box, were you?' somebody said inside, and the door shut on the laugh. / Peyrebarthe said it to anyone who came late

In under 2,000 words: a paralysis, a creature, an experiment in 2014, a cracked rib, a partner, the Port, a care home, a shopping pad, a mayor who is also a president, a sluice, a winch, a key, a registered letter numbered 41, a dead father, Saint Catherine's, a blower, minutes of 2011, a duck hide, a hand of water, four turns short, and a gravestone in a parking space. Every one of them is used later. I did not know that on page three. I was sorting, not reading, until Coralie spoke into the oven.

What I would do: name the village before it is used as a subject. Move the winch-in-the-minutes exchange and the father's four turns to chapter 3, where the winch is on stage. Let chapter 1 be the thing on the wardrobe, the man in the car who will not be the mayor, and the eggs.

**8. Two lines break the premise. (8 against 5; 7 against 1 and 3)**

> 'My daughter has never said that word,' she said. 'Not at four years old, when it was an apple or a pear.'

The two women were one woman until June 2014, aged thirty-one. Lesca says so: "I gave that word up at thirty-one." Her mother cannot then say she never used it at four. Either the flood made the mayor decisive or she was born so, and the book's whole argument depends on which.

Smaller: "'Four winters you've held that key'" (7), from Jeannot, to a woman who wrote to the council about that winch in 2011 and has greased it since 2013.

What I would do: Arlette says "not since the flood". Jeannot says four winters of nets, not of the key.

**9. The clock is told in an idiom half the readers do not have. (6, 7, 8, 9)**

> 'That river will be at the red line on Tuesday week. […] On the Friday night the bank comes through at the last house.'

"Tuesday week", "Friday week", "Saturday week". A reader who knows Crouch is probably American and does not know that "Friday week" means the Friday after next. The book's whole countdown is carried in that phrase and in gauge readings (a metre fifty under, ninety under, seventy under, at the line, sixty over, eighty-five over) that I was asked to hold across chapters. I lost the count twice.

What I would do: dates. "On Tuesday the eighth". "On the night of Friday the eleventh". The book already does this when it matters most (10 opens "On Tuesday the eighth of December") and it is instantly clearer.

**10. Nobody says which way the gate works. (1 against 3)**

> Duck come down at night only to standing water, and the gate had drained his off.

In chapter 1 raising the gate takes water off the meadows. From chapter 3 raising the gate puts the river onto the meadows. Both are true of a tidal sluice and I worked it out, but the book's central act is "open the gate" and for two chapters I was not sure what opening it did. The same goes for the lie of the land: river, bank, Port, gate, meadows, track. I had it by chapter 9. I needed it by chapter 3.

What I would do: one sentence on the dyke in chapter 3, in Larrère's mouth since he likes explaining gates. Low river, it lets the meadows out. High river, it lets the river in.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Kévin's hand.** "It was four o'clock. […] 'Five,' he said. He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." (14) and "'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything as much.'" (17). The best use of a planted device in the book.
2. **The landing.** All of it, from "'Which of you is that?'" to "'Then she can say so in the morning.'" (9). It is the scene I would read to someone.
3. **02:51.** The call log in Coralie's mouth (10), and the sentence at the microphone it leads to (15), through to Marie-Jo's "'Somebody has to stack them.'"
4. **Madame Etcheto, entire.** Two lots of stars and it came at one (3); the nineteen bags (12); room twelve; "'Forty-one,' Coralie said." (14); "'Twelve. One, two.' A chair was pulled in to a table." (15).
5. **Jeannot on the telephone and Jeannot at the grave.** "'And six. Bugger off.' / She listened to all of it." (11) and the last page of 17, letter 42 and the second shovel.

Also leave alone the weather. Rain on the tiles is her village, frost and dry tiles is the other. It was the one piece of navigation that never failed me.
