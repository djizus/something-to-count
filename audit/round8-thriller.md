# Round 8 — critic, Thriller lens

Read: `manuscript/01.md` to `17.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else opened but the brief.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes — 8.** "The bank had six of its ten minutes left, and on foot she could reach one of the two." (14) By then gate, man and wife under the bank are one object. Crouch level.
- **Tension — 7.** "02:49. She was awake. She laid her left hand on the wheel and touched off the fingers, and made five." (12) It is at this pitch from 11 to 14 and slack from 8 to 10.
- **Progression — 6.** "Wednesday. Not seen." (9) Lafitte reads the plot back to the bar because for ten days of the countdown the gauge moves and the two women do not.
- **Comedy — 8.** "'Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade.'" (7)
- **Characters — 8.** "'I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak. I've lifted too many of them.'" (9)
- **The switch between dark and comic — 9.** "He said it as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another." (14) A running joke turned into the worst moment of the flood without a seam.
- **Prose — 8.** "Then the water came up the door to it and put it out." (11) Better sentences than Crouch writes. They give up their meaning more slowly than his, and in a thriller that is a cost.
- **The ending — 9.** "'Forty-one,' Coralie said." (14)
- **Clarity — 6.** "Maylis's right hand moved first. It went across to the belt, where the key of the handle box hung, and shut on that." (13) The hinge of the book, that she is about to open the gate on him as Lesca did, is carried by a hand going to a belt.

**Finished unpaid:** yes. From chapter 11 I could not have stopped.
**Press it on a friend:** yes, with the instruction to reach the end of chapter 4. Without that instruction a Crouch reader stops in chapter 1, and one who survives it stops in chapter 9.

## 2. Against the comparisons

On this lens: **below Crouch from chapter 5 to chapter 10, level in 1–4 and 11–13, above in 14–17.** Net, just under the bar.

**Below.**

- Crouch never lets the plan that would solve the book be dropped unfought. Ethan Burke tries the road, the telephone, the sheriff, the fence; each try fails and narrows the next. Here the daylight plan ("That gate is opened on the Friday afternoon, on the ebb, in daylight, by order", ch. 5) dies after one telephone call made in the wrong village (ch. 8), and the midnight dilemma is what is left over, not what the characters were driven into.
- Crouch keeps the usurper acting. Jason2 is in the house, doing things, while the reader watches. Lesca states her intention four times (ch. 4, 8, 10, 13) and acts on it for eight rings of a telephone.
- Crouch states his mechanism once, flat, in a mouth the reader trusts. Here the rules of the chair are given three times (Etcheto in 3, Lesca's three points in 5, the narrator in 10) and the one question a thriller reader asks, what makes the rain cross and would closing the door stop it, is raised and left.

**Level.**

- Chapter ends in the first third: "That village had not been spared December 2014. It had not had it yet." (2); "'Come back across,' her mouth said, and she had not said it." (3); the motion (4). Each is a Crouch last line.
- The clock. A gauge read in centimetres, set against a file ("1 m 50 under", "at the line", "ninety over"), is as readable a countdown as anything in Recursion.

**Above.**

- Fear for the marriage. Dark Matter makes the reader afraid the wife will not notice. This book makes the reader afraid she will notice and prefer the other one: "'I'd have this one again.'" (6), "'I'd have you tomorrow.'" (9). Then it has her refuse on a principle from her own trade. Crouch has nothing as good.
- Cost. Crouch restores the family. This ends with a drowned woman the reader liked, a wife gone on a floor of her own, a job dated out of existence, and a village joke changed for good ("They've retired the other", 17).
- The reversal. "'So you were right, that night. Turned first, it keeps them all.'" (15) The sin the book has held over Lesca for fourteen chapters was the right sum, and the attempt to do better is what kills. That is a Good Place turn. It is thrown away in three lines; see fault 7.

**Where the reader is afraid, and of what.** End of 4, of Lesca. The soup and the fourth stair in 6, that Coralie will like her better. The landing in 9, that she will say yes. Dusk in 12, when the reflector is seen a second time ("Nobody had given her this one"). All of 14. Nowhere in 8, in 10 before Coralie speaks, in the first half of 13, or in 16.

**The central choices.** Maylis at the dip lands. Lesca at the microphone lands. Lesca on the chair half lands; see fault 4.

## 3. The ten faults, worst first

**1. The daylight plan is dropped without a fight, and the middle stalls for it. (ch. 5, 8, 9, 10)**
"'Then I'll pay it. I sign things.'" (5) She never tries to sign anything. In 8: "'Then ask Larrère.' 'I can't ask him.'", and one speech later: "'If nobody will order that gate opened by day, then I'll open it at night myself.'" Between the struck pawl order on 1 December and the call to Hontaube on the 11th, neither woman makes one attempt on the gate, the order or the Port. Lesca spends a whole ridden Saturday like this: "A woman who stands about a square in the commune's waterproof is asked what she is doing, so Lesca had brought a board." And when her prophecy comes true to the word in front of the café ("Nobody at the table laughed. The same mouth had told them what came on the Friday.", 10), nothing follows from a village that now believes her but one man's sandbags.
*Do:* give 8–10 two attempts that fail forward. Lesca, riding on the Saturday, goes after the order she promised, at the sub-prefecture or with a pen, and it costs Maylis something. On Tuesday the 8th the café's belief has a consequence the same day: the Port wants the gate, and Larrère answers by tightening his hold. Friday must be the last door left, not the only one tried.

**2. A loaded gun is never fired. (ch. 7 → 12)**
"It was the mayor's. It stood in the lane behind the Castagnet house with its lights off, and nobody got out of it while she was passing." (7) Six pages earlier she has blackmailed Jeannot and the narration says: "She would have done it again that afternoon, to anybody." On Friday Larrère lays his hand on her undated letter, and she, holding his secret, says "'Is that advice?'" (12). The reader has been shown the weapon and shown that she will use one, and the text gives no reason she does not.
*Do:* have her use it at the car window on Friday and lose. The book has already built the reason she would lose: after the chrysanthemums the village believes she is the one with business in that kitchen. Or have her refuse it for Maïté's sake, said. Either way the scene exists, and it makes fault 3 harder.

**3. The decisive failure has nothing pushing against it. (ch. 12)**
"She had a buff folder in her head." Then: "She lifted the handle off the shaft, laid it back on its rag and locked the box." It is daylight, the man is above ground an arm's length off, the handle is on the shaft, she has seen the whole night, and she stops for a pension in a commune with twenty days to live. This is the act the Port later indicts her for ("At five on Friday it was light", 17) and it takes six sentences with no one in the frame opposing her. Fear turns to irritation here.
*Do:* put the opposition on the dyke. Jeannot is already there, with his fortnight's hand of water and his engine running; let him be the one who stops her, or Larrère's car on the crown, or the winch at nineteen with that rib. Let her get turns into it. Two pages. She still fails by Either, but the reader must feel what is on the other end of the handle.

**4. The takeover ends in a hundred and twenty words, and its price is not counted. (ch. 13–14)**
"She said them over, as the cost of a motion is read out before the vote. Not one of them would drive a van. / She let go." The threat set in chapter 4 is resolved by the usurper's own decision, quickly, and by logistics. Meanwhile the arithmetic that would make it terrible is already in the text and nobody does it: the telephone starts at 02:51, Maylis has her body back at 02:53, and "She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it went."
*Do:* let Lesca hold longer, and win something first. Then have somebody set her two minutes against those two hundred metres, in the steel room in 15. Her wanting should be in the death, measurably.

**5. An exit stands open and nobody tries the door. (ch. 3, 5, 13)**
"'Where did you sleep before Monday?' 'On my side. At the edge.'" (3) "'Then it had rained two days before you did anything.'" (5) "'On your side, Maylis. Tonight. With your knees up, so it has nowhere to sit.'" (13) The book says three times that the sharing began when Maylis lay on her back, and has the two women argue about what brought the rain. No one asks whether turning over ends it. A Crouch reader asks in chapter 5 and goes on asking.
*Do:* one beat that shuts it. A night she lies on her side, nothing sits, and the gauge is up ten centimetres in the morning. Three sentences, and the flood becomes weather that has to be met, not a link that could be cut.

**6. The best cliffhanger is followed by a rehearsal. (ch. 13)**
Chapter 12 ends "The telephone began to ring in her hand." Chapter 13 begins "On Friday at eleven a young man came from the prefecture at Pau to walk Lesca through her ceremony", and gives tape, a seating plan and a day book for some thirteen hundred words before the steel room.
*Do:* move the man from Pau and the front-row taping to Thursday (ch. 10), where the eight survivors will also load the council vote. Keep Arlette and her ponies, which load the gun, and the clock at 02:49. Reach the chair inside a page.

**7. The scenes the double plot was built to produce are reported, not played. (ch. 15, 16)**
"Maylis told her in order what the five words had come to, from the dry cellar and the van sunk in the dip to Madame Etcheto drowned in her bed." (15) "Then Lesca told her that she had twice asked the other Coralie back across a landing, with Maylis's mouth, and that the second time that Coralie had said she would have her and had sent her down the stairs." (16) One woman telling the other what her advice cost, and a wife hearing that her other self said yes: both in indirect speech. The marriage the reader was made to fear for is then closed in a subordinate position: "That night Coralie carried her pillow back across the landing."
*Do:* play the first exchange of each in speech. What are Maylis's first words? What does Coralie's face do at "she would have her"? And give "you were right" room; it is the reversal of the book.

**8. The secret is told a chapter before it is shown. (ch. 10 → 11)**
"'Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one.'" (10) Then chapter 11 plays the night, and its single ring arrives already known. The reader has suspected since "That isn't what I asked" (2); the how was the last thing held back.
*Do:* in 10 let Coralie give the minute and that she picked up the telephone, and stop. "Nine minutes to three. I've had that for twelve years" is enough to re-read the wardrobe. Let the screen's line be seen for the first time on the dyke.

**9. A threat made on Lesca's side is never paid. (ch. 13)**
"'If I come in and you're lying there with your eyes open, I'm ringing Bayonne, and I'll tell them whatever name gets you an ambulance.'" Lesca then goes. The next we see of her world is the square at half past ten. Her side of the climax has no danger in it at all, which is the general weakness of her chapters: they have a deadline (eleven on Saturday) and no peril.
*Do:* pay it. Coralie comes in. An ambulance takes the body out of the one bed the thing comes to. That puts a clock on Lesca's return while she is in the hall of the dead, and gives her Coralie something to do on the night.

**10. The first chapter spends its opening on a winch. (ch. 1; and the doubling in 3–5)**
After a first page that would hold anyone: "'Then it's in the minutes. And it isn't the council's winch, it's the association's, and nobody has written to the association. I was in the room, yes. I was in the room as mayor.'" Commune, association, mairie, gate, key, tonne and forty-one letters arrive before the reader has a reason to keep them. Then the first act runs its beats twice: the envelope in 3 and again in 4, the hand in 3, 4 and 5, where the text itself says "Maylis had heard it the first time."
*Do:* cut the square by a third and bring the stone forward into the first night, so the second presence is in play before the letter. Start chapter 4 with the card already against the clock.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **Chapter 14, whole.** The time stamps, the dip, "'Both.'", the dry floor, the key thrown along the crown, Kévin's five fingers, room twelve, "'Forty-one.'"
2. **One ring against nine.** "It rang once. Nobody answered it, and she did not wait for the second ring." and, in the same chapter, "'Who's dead?' said Jeannot Castagnet." through "'And six. Bugger off.'"
3. **The landing in chapter 9.** Coralie's two fingers on the wrist, "'I'd have you tomorrow'", and "'Then she can say so in the morning.'"
4. **The confession and what follows it in chapter 15.** "'Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him.'", the sentence walking back through the square, "'Madame Castagnet will lay the wreath'", "'Somebody has to stack them'", and Madame Etcheto's "'It never came last night.'"
5. **The gauge and the last lines of 2, 3, 4, 12 and 17.** The centimetres are the book's clock; those five endings are why the next page gets turned. "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it."
