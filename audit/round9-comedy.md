# Critic's report: Comedy

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `manuscript/19.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes, but I would have put it down for a night somewhere in chapters 9 to 11. From chapter 12 I could not have stopped.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On a friend who likes dry comedy, yes. On a friend who loves The Good Place, with a warning that it smiles more than it laughs. On a Crouch reader, no.

## 2. Against the comparisons

Crouch is not funny and does not try to be, so on comedy the book is above him by default. The comparison that counts is The Good Place.

**Overall: below. On one thing level, on one thing above.**

- **Below on range.** The Good Place has six people who are wrong in six ways and funny in six registers: vulgar, panicked, stupid, vain, literal, gleeful. This book has one register, which is deadpan procedure, and nearly everyone works in it. Madame Ducasse ("There's no column"), Sarthou ("I cannot file a forecast"), the woman at Hontaube ("I've got a tray for each, and a book"), the young man from Pau ("I've nothing on the sheet for this"), Tastet ("I have that as consent"), the lieutenant ("It has two"), Lafitte with his pad. That is seven record-keepers telling one joke. It is a good joke. By the fifth telling the reader is ahead of it.
- **Below on heat.** I laughed aloud twice in twenty chapters: at Madame Etcheto's care plan in ch. 3 and at "And six. Bugger off" in ch. 12. I smiled perhaps forty times. The Good Place gets the loud laugh several times an episode, because somebody on screen is always being foolish at full volume. Nobody in Peyrebarthe is ever foolish at full volume except Jeannot on a telephone at five in the morning, and that page is the proof of what the rest is missing.
- **Level on the reversal that re-reads.** "Either" starts as a shopping-pad joke about eggs and ends as the word that leaves a gate shut with the man on the dyke and daylight left. Every earlier "Either" changes when it lands. That is the same mechanism as the end of the first season of The Good Place, at village scale.
- **Above on the switch.** The Good Place almost never lets a joke cost a life. This book does it four times and does it cleanly: Either at the gate, Kévin's five fingers in the flood, "Staff not to join in" ending in room twelve, Coralie unable to finish the oven impression.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. One comic register, shared by the whole cast. (Whole book; structural.)**
"'There's nothing here that lets me un-receive it. There's no column.'" (ch. 9). "'It's received,' she said. 'I've no column for which side.'" (ch. 11). "'Is this a request, or a notification?' the woman said. 'I've got a tray for each, and a book.'" (ch. 14). "'I enter callers.'" (ch. 13). "'It has two.'" (ch. 18).
The joke is that a person will record a thing sooner than act on it. It is the book's theme, and it is right that the village runs on it. But every comic voice is built from it, and they all speak in the same clipped, northern-English syntax ("while you're sat", "I'm stood in it", "I'll not"). Cover the names and Ducasse, Sarthou, Tastet and the Hontaube clerk cannot be told apart.
*What I would do:* keep Ducasse and Sarthou as the two record-keepers and re-engine the rest. The café needs a talker who gets things wrong at length, not three people who annotate. Père Honoré has a vanity ("I have nine on a good Sunday") that is used for two lines and should run all book. Kévin in the loader bucket ("'I'd fly,' Kévin said, from the loader bucket, with rain running off his cap") is a different kind of funny, an innocent, and he gets one scene of it before he is needed for plot. Bixente is set up in ch. 3 as a comic victim and never speaks again except to give a time. Give each of those three a way of being wrong that is not clerical.

**2. The two women stop being a double act after chapter 5. (Ch. 8, 11, 19; structural.)**
Ch. 5: "'Then I'll stand.' She stood. They were of a height, and neither of them liked it." and "'I won't have it said to me by my own face.'" That is the comic heart of the premise: a woman who cannot stand herself, literally. After it, the steel room becomes a place where two accountants settle up: "She had priced what she was giving. It came to a night's work at forty euros the month" (ch. 8); "'It's a life in one hand. You'd have my legs, and my mouth, and my bed'" (ch. 11); and in ch. 19, "'No. You won't sign it, and I'll say why at length'", followed by a 190-word moral summary with no joke in it.
The Good Place keeps its ethics inside a relationship that is funny, and never lets a moral be stated without something puncturing it. Here the central pair has one comic scene and three grim ones.
*What I would do:* keep the ch. 5 parliamentary game alive in every room scene. Lesca chairs; Maylis prices. In ch. 8 let Lesca try to minute Maylis's offer and fail. In ch. 11 let Maylis put a motion and Lesca find herself unable to rule it out of order. In ch. 19, cut Lesca's paragraph to its three facts (the headlamps, the one ring, "you rang nine times") and let "What was the verb?" / "Drowned." do the rest. It already does.

**3. Comic invention stops at chapter 10; after that the book lives on callbacks. (Ch. 11 to 19; structural.)**
The funniest engine in the book is Maylis in the mayor's chair, where passivity is taken for authority: "'I have Either,' Tastet said, gently. 'It's written, Madame le maire. A second answer doesn't strike out a first. You taught me that.'" and "'You have it, Madame le maire,' said Madame Ducasse, with her eyes on the medal. 'It's in the inventory.'" It is used for one chapter in twenty. Lesca in Maylis's village gets two (6 and 10). After the loto there are two new comic situations in nine chapters (the Hontaube call, Jeannot's rant). Everything else that is funny is a payoff.
The payoffs are excellent. But a reader who was told this is a comedy has had most of it by the halfway mark.
*What I would do:* give Maylis a second day on Lesca's side. The Thursday council in ch. 11 ("whether the mayor should open her mouth on Saturday") is played by Lesca alone, as straight procedure. Played with Maylis at the mouth, a council voting on whether its mayor may speak, while she says nothing and is taken to be manoeuvring, is the best scene the book does not have. It would also earn "I want your side" in the same chapter, which at present rests on one Wednesday.

**4. The loto relays recap where it should build. (Ch. 10.)**
"So Lesca drew each order and said something with it, and Maylis took the money and gave the change, and for ten minutes the bar of the loto was better run than anyone in the hall could remember."
That sentence is the scene, and it is told in summary. Two women running one bar with one body is a comic set piece; we get one customer of it (the priest and his roof). Meanwhile Lafitte reads the pad aloud twice in the same chapter, and both readings tell the reader what the reader has just read: "Sunday the twenty-ninth. A pot of chrysanthemums to Castagnet's wife. Monday. Three minutes to the mayor, and called him Guy." The chapter carries Sarthou's statement, the pad, Marie-Jo's 2020 list, Coralie's coffees, the ham, Jeannot's vow and Inguma's arrival. Each is a beat. None is built on the one before.
*What I would do:* play the bar. Three customers, each worse: one where the two halves agree, one where the mouth says what the hand contradicts, one (Jeannot) where it goes wrong in front of the queue. Cut the first pad reading; keep "Can't believe her luck in him," which is a new entry and funny. Keep Daguerre's "Here" into the silence, which is the best laugh in the chapter.

**5. "Either" is overspent before the gate. (Ch. 1 to 8.)**
Before the gate in ch. 14 the word is a punchline nine times: the pad and Coralie's riff (1), to Inguma and to Larrère (3), at breakfast and in the steel room (5), stolen from Lesca's mouth (6), about the duck and to Sarthou (7), to Tastet (8). By ch. 7 the reader says it before she does: "'Is that still what you want?' 'Either.'"
The gate needs the word to arrive when the reader has stopped waiting for it.
*What I would do:* cut three. The bedtime one in ch. 3 ("'Either,' Maylis said. Inguma had nothing to start on") is the weakest. The breakfast one in ch. 5 is redundant, since ticking both boxes is the same joke done better. The duck one in ch. 7 exists for Coralie's reply and she can have that off a shrug. Keep Larrère's pawl, the stolen mouth, Sarthou, and Tastet, which are four different uses.

**6. Jokes that need an audit. (Ch. 6, 8, 10, 15.)**
"Lesca had the urn, the left hand and the mouth. Maylis had the right hand, and Maylis knew what a beer cost." Then: "'Nothing,' Maylis said, before Lesca had the mouth open. 'You're breathing,' Lesca said after her, with the same mouth, a little lower."
The right hand as the passenger's is a fine idea and gives the book its best small physical comedy (the spoon turned over in ch. 6, the timer dial in ch. 8, the low-crossed t in ch. 10). But in the crowd scenes every line has to be assigned to a tenant, and on top of that the reader is holding two Coralies, two Larrères, two Sarthous, Jean and Jeannot, and a calendar ("the thirtieth", "the second", "Tuesday the eighth", "Friday the eleventh"). A laugh needs the reader a half-beat ahead. Here the reader is a half-beat behind, checking.
*What I would do:* one rule per scene, stated once at the top and never broken inside it. In the loto, Lesca talks and Maylis handles money, full stop; drop the three places where Maylis also gets the mouth. Cut dates from dialogue wherever the day of the week will do.

**7. The narrator footnotes its own callbacks. (Ch. 11, 16.)**
"He did not hear himself say it. Eight days before, Lesca had told him through that window, with Maylis's mouth, that the river would be at the line on this Tuesday, that Pau would send a yellow, and that he would say those two sentences." Three lines later Lafitte makes the same point as a joke: "*Tuesday 8th. The line, a yellow, and the mayor word for word.*" The explanation kills what the pad then delivers.
Likewise ch. 16: "It buzzed, twice, against his chest, as it had on the dashboard of a tractor under the plane trees"; "He did it without asking, as he had once got into her bucket"; "The two of them had got the giggles, and been heard."
*What I would do:* cut all four. The reader who remembers is insulted and the reader who does not is not helped by a pointer.

**8. Coralie's wit is turned into a reproach. (Ch. 5, 9, 10, 19.)**
She is the only person in the book who is funny on purpose: "'I'd have taken both. One for the commune, and one for the washing-up.'" and the oven. From ch. 5 on, nearly every scene she has with Maylis ends on the same demand: "'So which was you?'" (5), "'And where are you, while she does your night?'" (9), "'Say which, Maylis. For once.'" (16), "'What do you want, Maylis?'" (19). The funniest person in the marriage becomes its examiner, and the reader starts to hear the question coming as surely as "Either".
"'There's a key, and a Trafic, and Guy, and then there's me, as a distance'" shows she can be hurt and funny in one line. There should be more of that and less of the question.
*What I would do:* in ch. 9 and ch. 10 let her answer the situation with a performance before she asks anything. She does voices. She should do Lesca, to Maylis, badly.

**9. The chorus has no faces. (Ch. 3, 6, 10, 11.)**
"'That's the pot explained,' said the owner's wife." "'Madame le maire,' said the man who was dealing." "said the oldest of them." "A maize grower took the handle out of her hand."
The café is where the village's misreading of events is manufactured, and the best gossip lines in the book ("'She's changing the subject'", "'I said it was him or the wife. It's him.'") belong to a woman with no name. A farce needs its gossips to be people the reader waits for.
*What I would do:* name the owner's wife and give her one fixed wrong theory she defends against all evidence until ch. 19. Fold "the man who was dealing" into Lafitte.

**10. A routine sits on top of the turn. (Ch. 16.)**
"'It's stopped,' he said. 'Your winch. I've three things to say about it. First, I counted, as you do, by the click, and at nineteen it stopped as if it had come to a wall. Second, I got my shoulder under it and my back, and my boots went out from under me on your planks, and I've done something to my knee. Third, it's up a hand. A hand.'"
This is the payoff of a winch set up in ch. 1, and it settles a question the ending depends on: whether the gate could have saved the Port. It arrives as Jeannot's numbered-points act, a hundred words long, with the water at the knees and seven chairs waiting. The reader smiles at the act and misses the fact. Worse, a reader can come away thinking the gate could never have been raised by anyone, which lets Maylis off and contradicts Lesca's "Turned first, it keeps them all" in ch. 19.
*What I would do:* give him the fact first and bare ("It's up a hand and it won't go further"), then let the numbering start and be cut off by Coralie reading the next name. The man is funnier interrupted, and the fact lands.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **"Either" at the gate, "Both" on the telephone, and Coralie's verdict.** Ch. 14: "'Either,' Maylis said." Ch. 16: "'Both.' The line stayed open, and Coralie said nothing down it." Ch. 19: "'I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it.'" The comic tic is the moral failure. This is the book.
2. **The verb for the stone.** Ch. 2: "'Opened what? There's a name over it. It reads that she opened him.'" and the timer ringing on Maïté. Ch. 18: "'It was *left*.'" Ch. 19: "'What was the verb?' 'Drowned.'" The moral problem made into a drafting dispute, without making it small.
3. **Larrère's eleven untimed minutes.** Ch. 8: "At four minutes he glanced at the timer, and went on with more confidence." and "He had been saving it since 2015, and there was less of it than he had thought." The best comic set piece in the book. It builds, it turns, and it ends on a man who does not know what comes next.
4. **Jeannot's rant straight after the 2014 night, and letter forty-two.** Ch. 12: "It rang nine times. 'Who's dead?' said Jeannot Castagnet." through to "'And six. Bugger off.'" The cleanest switch in the book: the reader has just watched the reflector go under, and the same man is alive and furious about teal. Ch. 19: "'It's four sides, and it doesn't thank you.'"
5. **Madame Etcheto's care plan, and what it comes to.** Ch. 3: "'It's in the plan now. Staff to wait. Staff not to join in.'" Ch. 14, counting sandbags: "'Nineteen,' Madame Etcheto said. 'That's few. It counts quickly.'" Ch. 19, the oven door: "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the neck. 'The—'". With it, Kévin's hand: "'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything as much.'" The funniest things in the first half are the saddest in the second, and nothing was changed about them but the water.
