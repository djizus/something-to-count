## 2. Against the comparisons

**Level with the bar overall for this lens. Above it on the sentence, below it on trust and on speed.**

- **Above Crouch on the sentence.** Crouch writes fragments to go fast. This book gets its speed from exact nouns and withheld comment: "Under it the torch found the sole of a wader." (12) There is no sentence in the Crouch novels that does as much with as little. The manuscript is also clean of formula. It has no semicolons, three dashes (all cut-off speech), almost no intensifying adverbs, and almost no "it was not X, it was Y" in narration outside chapter 16.
- **Below Crouch on speed where speed is wanted.** When Crouch accelerates he strips the prose to the event. In chapter 16 this book does the opposite. It adds ten comparisons built on "as", three callbacks to earlier chapters, and a statement of the dilemma as a formula, at the moment the reader wants only the next metre of track.
- **Level with The Good Place on comic construction, below it on trust.** The show plants a rule early and collects on it late (the timer, "Either", "Here", "in the minutes", "There's no column"). This book does the same and does it well. But the show never explains a reversal after it lands. This book does, about twenty-five times, and the two best reveals (11 and 16) are each followed by a sentence that tells the reader what they have just seen.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

### 1. The narrator explains what the scene has just shown (6, 7, 8, 10, 11, 12, 14, 16, 18, 19)

The worst case is the best reveal in the book. In 16 the screen reads "Castagnet, house", and a kitchen clock ticks behind Larrère's voice, a clock the reader heard in 12. That is complete. The next sentence is:

> "He was in Jeannot Castagnet's house, at Jeannot Castagnet's telephone, and Jeannot was a kilometre out on the meadows under a hatch."

In 11 Larrère repeats, word for word, the two sentences he was told he would say. Lafitte's pad then delivers the joke in nine words: "*Tuesday 8th. The line, a yellow, and the mayor word for word.*" Between the two the narrator has already spent it:

> "He did not hear himself say it. Eight days before, Lesca had told him through that window, with Maylis's mouth, that the river would be at the line on this Tuesday, that Pau would send a yellow, and that he would say those two sentences."

The same habit, chapter by chapter:

- 6: "The word was Maylis's own, and she had got to her mouth before Lesca did." (after "'Either,' Maylis said.")
- 7: "It was the mayor's. It stood in the lane behind the Castagnet house with its lights off, and nobody got out of it while she was passing." (after "She knew that car.")
- 8: "Over here the president and the mayor were two people. At home they were one man in a car, and he kept a letter of hers in a folder." The same explanation returns a page later: "At home that man ordered the gate as president and again as mayor, and he kept her resignation in a folder."
- 10: "It was a letter over the mayor's head, from an employee whose resignation he kept in a folder with no date on it."
- 11: "Jeannot's hand was the last one down, and he was the one man who would be out on those meadows on Friday night."
- 12: "That was the woman she had written a handover for. Maylis had meant to wake in that woman's bed, where the sash was, and leave a van on a dyke with nobody in it."
- 14: "Lesca's plan had wanted this hour, with the gate raised by day, by order, and nobody on the meadows."
- 16: "He had come out of a dry cellar half an hour before, by the neck, and had been told that the gate was to be opened on him." This sits directly before "'I was at school with his son,'" which needs no help.
- 16: "If she went back she turned the handle on him, as Lesca had. If she went on, the river had the Port."
- 18: "She owed her sentence to two people in a room, and Maïté was the first of them." Also "They had said what there was indoors, and out here they kept to the gutter." Also "who had kept a gate shut on him for twelve years and had now spoken to him across it."
- 19: "She went back to the landing of the Saturday before, and the woman in Maylis's coat who had asked her across and been sent down the stairs."

**What I would do.** Cut every one of these. Keep the sentences that tell a reader what a thing is (the tonne in 1, the pawl in 3, glass eels in 3, the red line in 3). Those are needed and they are well done. Cut the sentences that tell a reader what an event means. The test is simple: if an object, a screen, a pad or a line of speech has just delivered it, the narrator stays out. This is about twenty-five sentences and six hundred words, and it is the largest gain available for the least work.

### 2. In 18 Lesca tells Coralie things she does not learn until 19

On Saturday at eleven at night, on her landing, Lesca says:

> "While she was on the track the dyke came down into the Port, and into the home at the end of it. They carried forty-one out. Madame Etcheto drowned in her bed, in room twelve."

Lesca let go of Maylis at seven minutes to three, with the van stalled at the head of the track. After that she has the hall of the dead, a voice calling "Twelve. One, two," and a chair pulled in. She has not sat in the chair with Maylis since. The dyke, the track and the count of forty-one are given to her in 19, on Sunday at nine minutes to three, and they are given as news: "'Forty-one,' Maylis said. 'There were forty-two in the beds.'" Lesca's reply there ("I heard her chair pulled in") confirms that the chair was all she had.

**What I would do.** Keep the order of scenes and cut Lesca's Saturday confession down to what she holds: eight rings, five words, a number called behind her, a chair pulled in, and the old woman's question in the square that morning. "I don't know yet what it cost. I heard twelve called." That is a worse night for Coralie and a better scene, and it leaves 19 its news. The alternative is to move the landing confession to Sunday night, which also works and costs nothing downstream, since the statement, the vote and the pillow all come later.

### 3. From 6 to 14 the warning is recited where it should tighten

Counts: "Friday the eleventh" 20 times (7 of them in chapter 6), "forty-two" 24, "over the line" 19, "forty houses" 18, "the patch" 14, "ninety over" 12, "ten minutes" 12, "a hand of water" 11.

In 2 the sum is a liturgy, and "The table took the answer and had not once asked to see her working" earns it. In 6 it is said again with the tenses changed, which is the best joke in the chapter. After that the same warning goes to Coralie (6), to Larrère (6, 7, 14), to Sarthou (6, 10), to Jeannot (6, 14 twice) and to the association (11). Each refuses in character and each refusal is funny, but the reader has the figures by heart and is told nothing new about the flood between the end of 5 and the start of 12. A typical case:

> "'And Friday the eleventh? The river's ninety over the line that night, and the dyke goes at the last house. You were told yesterday.'" (7)

The one new and frightening number in the middle of the book is in 10, and it gets three short paragraphs between a post box and a loto: "A kilometre had taken twenty-five minutes. [...] on the Friday the dyke would have ten."

**What I would do.** This needs structure, and it is worth more than any word-level fix here. Give the walk down the track in 10 a full scene. Let the gauge carry the middle: each reading is currently one sentence ("under the line", "ninety under", "at the line") and each could be the thing a scene turns on. Then cut two of the repeated warnings outright (Maylis to Larrère in 7 and Lesca to Sarthou at the bar in 10) and reduce the rest to a clause. Keep Sarthou's "It has not occurred."

### 4. Chapter 16 changes voice at the climax

Three things happen to the prose in 16 that happen nowhere else.

- Sentences opening "It was" or "It had": 26 in this chapter, against 1 to 7 in every other chapter but 14, which has 11. They come in pairs. "It was not loud. It was the sound of a lorry tipping gravel". "It was colder than the rain. It was moving". "It was black. It was still in there". "The floor was dry. It was as dry as a kitchen." "It had been higher. It had left its line on the wallpaper". The first pair is fine. By the fifth the reader hears a device.
- The dyke becomes "the bank". Chapter 14 has "dyke" 17 times and "bank" once. Chapter 16 has "bank" 10 times and "dyke" twice, and chapter 19 then says "two hundred metres short of the dyke" for what 16 called "two hundred metres short of the bank". A reader new to the ground may take the bank for a second structure, at the one moment the geography must be certain.
- Comparison and callback at full speed. "as it had on the dashboard of a tractor under the plane trees", "as he had once got into her bucket", "as he would have carried a sack of maize to a hide", "as a stage is", "as a child is carried". And in the corridor: "The two of them had got the giggles, and been heard." "Got the giggles" is a phrase from another narrator, and the reader arrives at door twelve already remembering.

**What I would do.** Keep the timestamps and the counting of steps and metres, which are the chapter's engine. Break three of the five "It was" pairs into single sentences with a subject. Use "dyke" throughout and leave "bank" to Larrère's maxim. Cut the tractor, the bucket and the giggles. Keep "as a man turns up a card he had hoped was another", which is the best simile in the book.

### 5. In the shared-body chapters the names hammer, and one sentence fails

"She" cannot serve two women in one body, so the names carry everything. "Lesca" appears 40 times in the 2,793 words of chapter 6 and 41 times in 10. In 8 the two names together come once every 43 words.

> "Lesca had handed that waterproof in with her keys in 2015. Lesca put it on, and it knew her elbows." (6)

> "Maylis could have put a stroke through the sheet. Maylis crossed a t that Lesca had left bare, low, and laid the pen down." (10)

And the sentence quoted under Clarity in section 1, where a chapter told from inside Maylis opens a scene on what Lesca wants and then says the mouth is Lesca's while Maylis uses it.

**What I would do.** The book already owns the answer: the host keeps the right hand. Make the part the subject. "The right hand came up onto the table and took the pen" needs no name and is funnier. Reserve the names for the instant control changes hands. In a paragraph where one of them acts alone, name her once and then write "she". Rewrite the chapter 8 sentence from Maylis's side: she is in a room where everyone expects Lesca, and she has Lesca's mouth to do it with.

### 6. Everyone, narrator included, keeps time the same way

"Since" appears 91 times. "Twelve years" 22, "eleven years" 16, "each year" or "each December" 21. In narration it is a backfill sentence that interrupts the scene. Chapter 8 alone has five:

> "That voice had told her to do things since 2010". "Not one of them had got to the end of a request since 2015." "Maylis had made that L since school". "He had been saving it since 2015". "Her column for it had stood empty since 2014."

In speech it is a ledger of years, and every woman in the book keeps one in the same words. Coralie: "I've said to for eleven years." Maïté: "I've stood here with you for eleven years." Larrère: "I've sat here since 2015 and voted against her and lost." Coralie: "I've had that for twelve years." Maïté: "I've stood at that stone for twelve years asking who was meant to go round the tonnes." Maïté: "I've had nothing from you for twelve Decembers". Coralie: "That's eleven years of wanting a thing that wasn't you."

In 13, 17 and 18 the three biggest speeches in the book are therefore built on one sentence.

**What I would do.** Coralie keeps the ledger. She reads doses off charts and the habit is hers. Maïté counts in things: she has the waders at the range, the two bowls, the suitcase with no dust on it. Give her speeches in 8 and 18 to those. In narration, allow one since-sentence a scene and make the rest earn their place.

### 7. Forty-five lines of dialogue open with "Then"

At least fourteen speakers answer with the same comeback.

> "'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said." (14) "'Then I'll pay it. I sign things.'" (Lesca, 5) "'Then the commune can sweep it,'" (Darrigrand, 4) "'Then it should have gone last Monday.'" (the clerk at Hontaube, 14) "'Then they will serve.'" (Père Honoré, 19) "'Then it can walk down there after.'" (Madame Etcheto, 14) "'Then you'll dig it.'" (Coralie, 19)

It is the village's logic: take the other person's premise literally and hand it back. It is the engine of the comedy, which is the reason to ration it. A priest who uses no contractions and a ninety-four-year-old Basque speaker should not turn a sentence the way a care assistant does.

**What I would do.** Leave it to the people who argue by procedure: Lesca, Jeannot, Madame Ducasse, the clerk at Hontaube. Take half of Coralie's away and all of Père Honoré's, Madame Etcheto's and Arlette's. Arlette already has her own turn ("I'll not say them for you"), and it is better.

### 8. The moral is argued in a speech (19, and the choice in 15)

> "'No. You won't sign it, and I'll say why at length. I was right about the houses. I can show you that on a file, to the centimetre. The file stops at the platform. [...] A woman is dead of that, and it still isn't what I did. Don't hand me your sum to sit behind. I've had my own for twelve years.'"

It runs to about 190 words, the longest unbroken speech in the book, and it states the distinction the previous five chapters have shown: one ring against nine, a telephone put in a pocket against a track run twice. "At length" is in character, and the reader still receives the theme as a paragraph. Chapter 15 has a smaller case, where the decision is reasoned out as a proposition: "For as long as she did, nobody drove, since a van does not go down a track with one woman at the wheel and another at the gear lever."

**What I would do.** Cut the speech to four sentences and let Maylis break it once. Keep "The file stops at the platform." Keep "You rang nine times." Keep "A woman is dead of that, and it still isn't what I did." Keep "Don't hand me your sum to sit behind." In 15, keep "Not one of them would drive a van" and cut the sentence about the wheel and the gear lever. The stall and the horn have already said it.

### 9. Reflexes of the plain style

The plainness is the book's strength, and a few of its tools are worn.

- **Placement verbs.** "Stood" 159 times, "put" 141, "turned" 127, "lay" or "laid" 112, "looked" 99. "Stood" does for people, timers, cards, vans, water and figures ("each of them stood"). Do not swap in synonyms. Cut the stage directions between lines of dialogue that place an object and do nothing else.
- **The recognition beat.** "She knew that car." "She knew that folder." "She knew that line." "Lesca knew that reach." "She knew the car." "She knew the smell." "She knew the voice". Eight times or more. Keep the folder (5) and the reach (15).
- **Measuring by the nearest object.** "The width of" 11 times, "the height of" 13, "a hand" as a measure most of 27. Add the durations: "for as long as it takes to read a number plate" (10), "for the space of two breaths" (16), "the time she would have given a reading on the gauge" (11), "a few blows of the hammer" (11). Those that belong to a trade are good. The narrator's general-purpose ones are a tic by chapter 10.
- **"Priced".** Seven times, two of them in the last scene of 19. The bag is the one to keep: "It came to less than a morning's load for the tip." Cut "She priced it at a morning for two arms".
- **Smaller repeats.** "A long way off" or "a long way" 8 times. "Took her time" 4. "Let her breath go" twice within a page across 6 and 7. "At walking pace" twice (6, 7).

### 10. Consistency of usage

Spelling is British throughout and I found no slips. "Telephone" holds everywhere in narration. The hunter's plural "duck" holds. The French terms (mairie, tonne, loto, commune) are unitalicised and consistent. Dialect "sat" and "stood" for "sitting" and "standing" stays inside speech. What does not hold:

- **The date of the flood.** The opening is headed "The night of the twelfth of December 2014". Every other reference, about a dozen, says "the night of the eleventh" (2, 5, 12, 17). Chapter 12 has it right: "the night of the eleventh of December 2014, going on for the twelfth". Head the opening to match.
- **Dyke and bank.** See fault 4. Chapter 12 also mixes them (bank 6, dyke 3).
- **Lamps and lights.** 7: "Its lamps were off." Four lines on: "with its lights off". Sixteen of each in the book. Vehicles have lamps in this book, so keep to that.
- **"Four winters".** Jeannot in 7: "Four winters you've held that key and looked the other way". Kévin in 3: "All these winters". Larrère in 7: "every winter of nets". The woman in the opening has the handle box open in 2014, and 16 says the key "had not been off it since she had it from her father". If four winters is how long the netting has run, Jeannot's line should say so. As written it dates the key.
- **Friday for Saturday.** Kévin in 19: "On Friday in that yard, in the water, with the chair in my hands, I counted." It was four on Saturday morning, and chapter 16 says so.
- **The gnomic present.** The narrator steps out of the past tense for a general truth about once a chapter: "Duck come down at night only to standing water." (1) "Counting does not keep it off a bed." (3) "A woman who waits about a square in the commune's waterproof is asked what she is doing" (10). The first is information. The other two are a second narrator. Also "A mayor, it turned out, signed a great deal" (10).

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **The opening, and its two replays.** "She laid her hand flat on the dyke beside the water, and the clay moved under her palm. She had mended that dyke each spring for six years. She gave it ten minutes." It returns in 12 and in 16 with the wording moved a little each time, and the third time the reader's hand is on the clay. The last line of the opening, "It rang once," is the book.
2. **"Either".** Thirty-four uses and none wasted. It goes from a shopping pad to "'Eggs is what Either gets in this house'", to the association's book, to Sarthou's form, to the dyke at dusk, to "'That was Either, with the river in it.'" Do not thin it and do not explain it.
3. **Monsieur Daguerre's "Here".** The loto, the nineteen sandbags ("Nobody called."), the roof of Kévin's van, and the prefect's date in the other village. It is the purest instance of the switch.
4. **Coralie and the call log in 13.** "'Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one.'" Then "'Nine minutes to three,' she said. 'I've had that for twelve years.'" Then "'Why not?' 'Because you'd have told me.'" The whole haunting is explained by a woman reading a screen, with no comment from the narrator.
5. **The places where the book declines to speak.** Room twelve, from "Twelve was shut" to "'Forty-one,' Coralie said." Coralie at the oven door in 19, who gets as far as "'The—'". And the last line. These are the proof that fault 1 is a habit and not a need.
