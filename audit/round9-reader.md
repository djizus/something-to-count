# Critic report: Reader

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `19.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else opened but the brief.

Lens: an ordinary reader with no knowledge of the Adour, French communes, Basque folklore, sluice gates or duck hides.

Overall: **7.5**. A good book that is harder to read than either comparison, and for a Crouch reader that difficulty falls in the wrong place: at the start.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 8.** "Behind it were forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the meadows there was one man." (2) I could say the stakes to a stranger in one sentence by chapter 2, and they never moved.
- **Tension: 7.** "The bank had six minutes, and on foot she could reach one of the two." (16) Chapters 12 to 16 are a 9. Chapters 9 to 11 are a 5.
- **Progression: 6.** "On Wednesday the same six men gathered on the dyke at the same winch" (11). The book says it itself. The date counts down, but the middle visits the same rooms twice.
- **Comedy: 8.** "'Opened what? There's a name over it. It reads that she opened him.'" (2) I laughed aloud there, at Bixente's two lots of stars, at "There's no column", and at every "Here".
- **Characters: 8.** "I've held hands for a living since I was nineteen. I know when one's holding and I know when one wants to be let go." (5) Coralie is the person I read for.
- **The switch between dark and comic: 9.** "'Here,' he said. / 'I know you are.'" (16) The running joke is wheeled out through floodwater and is still the joke, and is now also a man answering to his name.
- **Prose: 7.** "The reflector burned in the lamps at the end of the track for four minutes more. Then the water came up the door to it and put it out." (12) At its best nothing is wasted. The same economy made me re-read about thirty sentences to learn who was doing what.
- **The ending: 9.** "Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it." (19) Both choices are paid for, in both villages, and nobody is let off.
- **Clarity: 5.** "Under the second heading she wrote what the accounts say in a year with no disposals. *Nil.*" (11) I read that scene three times and still cannot tell you what she concluded.

**Would I have finished it unpaid?** Yes, but I would have put it down in chapter 2 if chapter 00 had not been there, and I drifted from 9 to 11.

**Would I press it on a friend?** On the friend who reads slowly, yes, with the words "get past chapter 2". On the friend who reads Crouch on a plane, no, not yet.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch on ease and speed. Level with The Good Place on making the problem funny. Above both on what the ending costs.**

- **Below: I never re-read a sentence in Dark Matter.** Crouch has a character say the premise in plain words (the box, the corridor, the doors) and then runs. Here the premise arrives in chapter 2 as a sum about registered letters, and the rules of the chair are not said plainly until Lesca's "three points" in chapter 5. For chapters 2 to 4, fifteen per cent of the book, I was working things out when a Crouch reader would be turning pages.
- **Below: Crouch's chapters end on a door closing behind you.** Twelve of these do ("Come back across", the fourth stair, the telephone ringing in her hand). Chapters 9, 11 and 18 end on a filing note.
- **Level: this is the trolley problem with a village in it,** which The Good Place staged as a joke with a real tram. The book's own turn is better than a restaging: the third option, "Both", is the one that kills somebody. Each person is wrong in their own way, as on the show: Larrère by procedure, Jeannot by grievance, Sarthou by file, Maylis by "Either", Lesca by sum.
- **Level: the reversal re-reads what came before.** Coralie's call log in chapter 13 changes every "Madame le maire" and every mention of the landing, in the way the end of the show's first season changes every earlier scene. The kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice does the same on a smaller scale.
- **Above: Crouch saves the marriage. The show saves everyone.** Here the woman who confesses loses her office and gets the pillow back; the woman who tried to save everybody loses her job, the old woman and the pillow. I believed both.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. Chapter 2 delivers the premise as arithmetic. (Structural.)**

> "Jean Castagnet had sent the mairie twelve. He had been dead for twelve years less eighteen days. For a while she had both figures, and each of them stood."

This is the paragraph in which I am meant to learn that there are two villages, that in one a man drowned and in the other he is alive, and that this woman is the same woman. I learned it by subtraction. I also had to carry, in that one chapter: a second mayor after being told who the mayor was, forty letters against twelve, Jean against Jeannot, a stone, a timer, a lake, and a file. The last two lines of the chapter are perfectly plain, but they come after the hard part.

What I would do: let Lesca see the thing before she counts it. Put the stone first, with his name on it, then the memory of him alive at a counter, then one sentence in her own flat voice that says it: there is another Peyrebarthe, where the other lamp came on, and he is alive in it. Then the letters, as proof. Move one of the "three points" up as well: the reader should know by the end of chapter 4, in a sentence, that whoever stays sitting wakes in the other's body.

**2. The hinge of the whole book passes in two sentences, and I missed it. (15)**

> "Maylis's right hand moved first. It went across to the belt, where the key of the handle box hung, and shut on that. Lesca knew that reach."

At this moment the two women have changed places. Maylis is about to open the gate on him; Lesca is the one who wants the track. Everything after depends on it: "Go to the tonne first", the dip, the two hundred metres, room twelve, and Lesca's speech in 19 ("you'd have drowned him, and you'd be me"). I read the reach for the key as Maylis keeping it from Lesca. I only understood on the second pass, from chapter 16's "to tell her not to. Not first."

What I would do: give Maylis's intention one plain sentence here, in her head or out of her mouth, before Lesca answers it with the clutch. She has the sum, she has the woman in the home, she means to turn the handle. It costs nothing and it is the choice the brief says is at the centre.

**3. Chapters 9 to 11 scatter and repeat. (Structural.)**

> "On Wednesday the same six men gathered on the dyke at the same winch, and the president presided through the top of his car window." (11)

Chapter 8 ends with the best turn so far: "I'm coming to stay." Then come a half-length chapter about withdrawing a letter (9), a loto that is funny but long (10), and a chapter of seven scenes in two villages (11): a petition, a second viewing of the winch, a flag bearer, a stock sheet, a second council about procedure. I counted five sittings with the white timer by the end of 11. These were the chapters I skimmed.

What I would do: make two chapters of the three. Keep the handover and the box room; the yellow box with both envelopes; the landing ("I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak"); Jeannot's hand going up for "shut"; Larrère's unfinished question; the tied vote. Cut the flag bearer to a clause in 17, cut the stock sheet or make it say its conclusion, and fold the petition into the association's meeting.

**4. I did not know Lesca could die until she nearly had. (3, 5, 8, 11, 15)**

> "The one it cannot fetch is found in her bed, and plays loto with the rest." (3)

That is the rule that gives chapter 15 its danger, and it is said once as a riddle and once as a joke ("Then I'm found dead in my bed. Those in favour."). The voice calling numbers behind the steel wall in 8 and the hall in 11 meant nothing to me when I met them. So when Lesca sits down on Inguma I took it for a clever move and felt no fear for her, and "She was not in her bed" confused me before it frightened me.

What I would do: have somebody who believes it say it straight before the night. Arlette is already in the mayor's office in 13 saying "She said them so as to stay in her bed. You say them so as to stay out of yours." Give her the next sentence: what was found in the bed of the one who did not come back. And in 11, let Maylis know whose the empty chair is.

**5. Who is doing what inside one body. (6, 8, 10, 15)**

> "It gave all at once, and the pawl missed its tooth, and Maylis let go with the right hand, since that give had put her into the rail a week before and she was not going there twice, and the gate dropped on its chain and the handle ran back, and Lesca took the right hand and snatched at the handle and felt it go round through the fist before it stopped." (6)

The surname-and-first-name device is the best piece of help in the book and I never mistook one woman for the other in dialogue. In action I did, repeatedly. Two names, one body, three limbs changing hands in a sentence. The van in 15 is the same.

What I would do: fix the rule on first use and never vary it (the visitor has everything but the right hand; the mouth is the one thing fought over), then in action give each woman her own sentence. Where the mouth changes hands, the pitch of the voice already marks it well; use that every time.

**6. Chapter 11 changes village mid-chapter with no signal. (11)**

> "On Wednesday morning Lesca telephoned Sarthou, who had been the gendarme at the Port until the State took his house, and who lived now in a flat at Bayonne."

A page earlier Sarthou was sandbagging his own step. I went back to check whether he had moved. Every other crossing in the book is marked by the weather, which is the cleverest orientation trick in it: rain is Maylis's village, frost and dry tiles are Lesca's. This crossing has none. With two of everybody (two Coralies, two Larrères, two Sarthous, two Darrigrands, two Madame Ducasses) I need it every time.

What I would do: open every section that crosses with the sky. One clause.

**7. Maylis's plan was explained to me after she dropped it. (8, 9, 11, 12)**

> "Maylis had meant to wake in that woman's bed, where the sash was, and leave a van on a dyke with nobody in it." (12)

That is the first time I understood what the trade in chapter 8 was: that on the night itself she would be gone, in Lesca's Saturday, and Lesca would do the gate. "Your Saturday. The square, and the sash. That's what I want for the night" did not tell me. So for four chapters I did not know what the quieter of the two women was planning, and her reversal at five in the morning, which should be the making of her, landed as information.

What I would do: say the plan in the steel room in 8, as plainly as Lesca says hers. "You take my night. I take your morning. I'm not there when it rings."

**8. The book raises the question of the rain three times and then shrugs. (2, 5, 19)**

> "'I don't know either.' / They left it there." (19)

I can live without the cause. I could not follow Lesca's wrong answer: "Inguma kept it on my side of this room, and I came in past it on purpose and let it across. That flood is mine." (5) I do not know what she is claiming there, so I could not enjoy Maylis disproving it. And a reader who came from Crouch has been trained to think the mechanism is the plot; three mentions is a promise.

What I would do: make Lesca's claim in 5 one clear sentence ("I went looking for you on the Thursday, and I brought my weather with me"), keep the disproof, and end the exchange in 19 on the line the book already owns, from chapter 5: nobody was to blame for a dry one. That is an answer. "They left it there" is the author leaving it there.

**9. Too many forties. (throughout)**

> "'Forty-one,' Coralie said." (16)

Forty houses. Forty-two people in their beds. Forty-one registered letters, with forty-two "spoken for". Forty euros a month. Forty letters in Lesca's head against twelve. Then nineteen days of rain, nineteen turns of the winch and nineteen sandbags. At the last line of chapter 16, the hardest line in the book, part of my head went to Jeannot's letters. If that echo is meant, it is not worth what it costs; the count of the living should belong to nothing else.

What I would do: change the letters to a number nowhere near forty, and the monthly pay likewise. Leave the houses, the beds and the hatch.

**10. Local things named before they are shown, and two stories promised and not told. (2, 8, 11, 17)**

> "the firm at Anglet about the firework bull for the twelfth" (8)

I did not know what a firework bull was until one ran at a crowd in chapter 18, where it is wonderful. Before that it was noise, twice. The same goes for "Pau has sent a yellow", the sub-prefecture, the brigade, the biodiversity police, "the code", Saint Catherine's, and the Adour, which is named for the first time in chapter 5 as if I knew it. The book explains the tonne, the pawl and the glass eels in half a line each, so it knows how.

And: "the night she handed her sash back across the council table in a carrier bag" (2, again in 17). I waited the whole book to be told what that night was about. Kévin's prison in Lesca's village is given in one clause in 17 and is enough. The carrier bag is not given at all.

What I would do: six words at first use for each term. One sentence for the carrier bag, in Marie-Jo's mouth, in 17, before "Somebody has to stack them".

*Smaller, same family:* "She gave it ten minutes" (00, 16) reads as "she waited ten minutes". Chapter 12 has it right: "She put what was left in it at ten minutes."

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **"Either", from the eggs to the river.** The shopping pad, the boxes, "I can't summon Either to the brigade", Tastet minuting it as consent, Jeannot on the platform at five, and Coralie's "That was Either, with the river in it." It is one word and it is the whole of Maylis.
2. **The chrysanthemums.** The pot bought before she knows she has bought it, "Who's dead?", Marie-Jo on the church steps, the pot in the sink among the peelings, and the café pad headed *Madame le maire* that grows out of it.
3. **Coralie and the call log.** "Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one." And "Because you'd have told me." Best scene in the book.
4. **Chapter 16 from the dip to "Forty-one".** The times on the screen, the key thrown back along the crown, "Here" / "I know you are", Jeannot carrying Castéra, Kévin counting five, the mark on the door frame she does not look at, room twelve, the hatbox.
5. **The last page.** Letter No. 42, brought by hand, "It's four sides, and it doesn't thank you", the second shovel, and him getting down into it.

## 5. Chapter by chapter

- **00.** A woman, a dyke, a winch and one ring of a telephone. I had to know who she rang. **9**
- **1.** A thing on the wardrobe and a village in which the mayor will not admit to being the mayor. The eggs made me like them both. **7**
- **2.** The other woman. The hardest chapter in the book; I understood it at its last two lines. The stone's missing verb is the first big laugh. **6**
- **3.** Fingers, stars, leaves, and the envelope that says *left*. Ends with her mouth saying a thing she did not say. **9**
- **4.** The envelope that says *right*, and a hand held for the first time in eleven years by somebody else's body. The motion "and Coralie Mounet in it". **9**
- **5.** The two of them in one room with one chair, and the rules at last. Then the pot of flowers. **8**
- **6.** Three minutes for Guy, a café that decides she thinks she is the mayor, soup with a tick. Ends on the fourth stair. **8**
- **7.** The undated resignation frightened me more than the flood had yet. Maylis learns to threaten. The car in the lane with its lamps off. **8**
- **8.** Maylis in the other house: a laugh in the minute's silence, eleven minutes with no bell, a telephone answered at the second ring. "I'm coming to stay." **8**
- **9.** A handover and a letter that cannot be un-received. Half a chapter. Coralie takes the box room. **5**
- **10.** The loto. Funny, crowded, long before it turns. The landing is the scene; the two envelopes in the yellow box is the image. **7**
- **11.** Seven scenes, two villages, one unmarked crossing. Jeannot's hand going up is the only thing I kept. **5**
- **12.** Her night, entire, with the reflector. Then nine rings and "And six. Bugger off." Could not stop. **10**
- **13.** Tape on the tarmac for eight who are left, a mother's short curse, and the call log. **9**
- **14.** Hontaube's form, the handle on the shaft in daylight, "Either", the walk to the hatch, the weight on the roof of the van. **10**
- **15.** Short and the most confusing where it matters most. The hall of the dead and "sends her apologies" I will remember. **9**
- **16.** The best chapter. I read it without looking up. **10**
- **17.** She says it. Nothing comes down on her. Then chairs to stack. Ends on a question I wanted answered. **8**
- **18.** The widow's verb, the gate at the bottom of the garden, a form with two boxes. The vote and the pillow go by fast. **6**
- **19.** Silt, a dated letter, "gone for a walk", a pillow in a car, two shovels. The last chapter; I closed it and sat for a while.

**What I will remember in a month:** Either. The pot of chrysanthemums. A car window going all the way down. An old man calling "Here". "Two fifty-one." A boy in floodwater counting five fingers and wishing for six. A man in waders getting into a grave.

**What I will have forgotten:** how the chair works, why it rained, and most of chapters 9 to 11.
