# Critic report: Thriller lens

Read: `manuscript/00.md` to `19.md`, in order, in full. Nothing else.

## 1. Scores

- **Stakes: 6.** For seven chapters the thing the heroine stands to lose is a job: "You'd have your years of pension in a commune that stopped existing at midnight." Lives are on the page only from 14.
- **Tension: 7.** 14 to 16 are a 9 ("The bank had six minutes, and on foot she could reach one of the two."). 7 to 11 are a 5, and they are a quarter of the book.
- **Progression: 6.** At the two-thirds mark the premise is switched off by the heroine herself: "On Monday night Maylis slept on her side, with her knees up, and nothing sat on her."
- **Comedy: 8.** "Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the brigade."
- **Characters: 8.** "I've held hands for a living since I was nineteen. I know when one's holding and I know when one wants to be let go."
- **The switch between dark and comic: 8.** The oven joke of chapter 1 returned in 19 and broken off: "'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the neck. 'The—'"
- **Prose: 8.** "The reflector burned in the lamps at the end of the track for four minutes more. Then the water came up the door to it and put it out."
- **The ending: 8.** Both halves cost: "Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him", and on the other side "the car door shut on a pillow."
- **Clarity: 6.** A book run on a date gives its first page the wrong one: the heading of 00 is "The night of the twelfth of December 2014", and from chapter 2 to the end it is "the night of the eleventh". The rules of the crossing are not stated until chapter 5 ("The one who wakes first takes the other home in her") and three new ones arrive in 14 and 15.

**Finished unpaid?** Yes. 00, the last line of 4 and the date carried me through 9 and 11, where I would otherwise have stopped.

**Press it on a friend?** Not on one who reads Crouch. I would have to say "push through the middle", and a book that needs that sentence is not level with him.

## 2. Against the comparisons

**Below Crouch as a thriller up to chapter 11. Level with him from 12, and above him in 16 and in what the ending costs.**

- **The double has no teeth.** In Dark Matter the other Jason takes the wife on about page thirty and keeps her for half the book. Here the other woman writes down that she will ("and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it"), asks once on a landing, is told to go downstairs, and goes. The fear for the marriage is the best new idea in the book: that Coralie will prefer the one who answers ("I'd have this one again"). It peaks and is disarmed inside one scene in chapter 10.
- **The rules are loose where Crouch's are tight.** He states a rule, tests it small, then stakes the climax on it. Here the thing can be avoided by lying on one's side, until the night it comes through a van roof to a woman sitting up awake.
- **The climax is better than his.** 16 is clocked to the minute, every distance was walked beforehand in 10 and 14, and the bill is paid by a named person the reader liked. Crouch's endings give most of it back. This one does not.

**Where the reader is afraid:** of the thing on the chest in 1 and 4; of a hand closing from inside in 4; of Coralie's answer in 6 and 10; for Jeannot and the Port from the reflector in 14 to "Forty-one". Nowhere in 7, 8, 9 or 11.

**Where a Crouch reader puts it down:** at the postman in chapter 1 if 00 had not bought patience; at the end of 9; at the second committee vote in 11.

**Does the central choice land?** Yes, at 02:59 in the dip: "She got out, and went on." It is weakened beforehand by being steered ("Go to the tonne first" comes out of the other woman) and afterwards by being explained in dialogue in 19.

## 3. The ten faults that most hold it back

**1. The double never takes anything. (6, 10, 11, 15)**
"I'm not coming over for a night. I'm coming to stay." Against that: "I don't get into a bed over somebody who can't speak", and then "Eight. If she let go, the van was Maylis's."
Lesca's whole attempt to keep the body lasts eight rings of a telephone. Both of her ridden days end because the counting ran out, not because anyone beat her. The reader never believes she can stay, so her letting go in 15 gives up nothing she has held.
*Do:* let her succeed. From the loto she sits on the thing and keeps the body from Saturday night to Tuesday. Maylis watches from the right hand as her own prediction comes true at the car window. Coralie lives three days with the one who answers, so her refusal is tried and not just stated. Maylis has to take the body back with what the rules give her: an alarm set earlier, a longer count. Then the eight rings are the surrender of something real. It also gives Lesca's own village a body lying open-eyed for three days in commemoration week, which that half of the book needs (fault 10).

**2. The menace is optional, and its rules change at the climax. (3, 11, 13, 14, 15)**
"On Monday night Maylis slept on her side, with her knees up, and nothing sat on her." Then, in the van: "It doesn't need you asleep." Then: "sat down on what had taken it." Then: "She was not in her bed."
The first is an off-switch the heroine uses for three of the last seven days. The other three are rules met for the first time in the act that depends on them: it comes to someone awake and upright; sitting on it keeps you over; getting off it unfetched puts you among the dead.
*Do:* take the side away from her after the first crossing (once it has had two in one seat, it comes however she lies). Test "sitting on it" once, small, before 15. Have the old woman or the mother say in advance where a visitor goes who is not fetched.

**3. The middle's stakes are paperwork, and the blackmail never touches the choice. (7, 9, 11, 14)**
"It has no date, so it isn't a letter. It's a sheet of paper that I keep."
The undated resignation is set up in 7, raised in 8, tightened in 14, and at 02:55 on the night it does not cross her mind. It is cashed only as aftermath. Chapter 9 is a handover note and a man failing to un-send a letter.
*Do:* make Larrère's hold physical and about the gate. After the letter to Pau he takes the handle out of its box, or puts the association's padlock on it, or posts a man. Her options on the night then narrow in iron and not in a folder. Fold what matters of 9 (the handover, the pillow going to the box room) into 8 and 10 and lose the chapter.

**4. The price is found, not fought for. (16)**
"Twelve wouldn't open. The water was on the door, on that side, and I couldn't shift it, and I had the corridor full."
This is reported at ten past four about something that happened at twenty past three, while the heroine was in the yard with a wheelchair. The one death in the book, of the woman who taught her the words, takes place behind a door nobody on the page is trying to open. And Coralie, planted four times as "thirty metres from the patch", is never in water above the knee until it is over.
*Do:* put Maylis at door twelve at 03:20, one-armed, with the river's weight on the other side, and have Coralie call her off it for the corridor. That is the mayor's sum made again by the other household (one behind a door, a corridor full), and the hour of chairs is then worked with the reader knowing. Keep "Forty-one" exactly where it is.

**5. The climax turns on the wrong plant. (1, 3, 6, 7, 9, then 16)**
"The handle was in its box. The key was on her belt. She had not turned it a centimetre."
The winch that stops at nineteen is planted five times and never stops her. It stops Jeannot, after the bank has gone, in a comic speech: "at nineteen it stopped as if it had come to a wall." What does stop her is the dip, which she had been warned of twice and had waded herself: "He had told her about the dip, and at dusk she had waded it." The first reads as a gun not fired, the second as the author's hand.
*Do:* at five o'clock on the platform let her turn it herself to nineteen and stick, with the one man who could put a shoulder under it standing on the planks. Then "Shut, or open?" and "Either" cost the turn that mattered. In 16 make the dip a known gamble, taken because the alternative is twenty-five minutes on foot, and say so in one line.

**6. The proven prophecy is thrown away. (11)**
"*Tuesday 8th. The line, a yellow, and the mayor word for word.*" Next morning: "'On what information?'" "'None I can give.'"
Her prediction has come true to the sentence, in front of the café, and is written on a pad. Twelve hours later she is asked for her information before six landowners and does not mention it, and neither does anybody else. This is the one moment the Cassandra plot could turn, and it does not even fail. It is simply not tried.
*Do:* have her, or Lafitte with the pad, put it to the association and be beaten on the merits: a guess, a forecast, the woman who thinks she is the mayor. A defeat is progression. An omission is not.

**7. The first crossing is told three times and the engine starts at chapter 6. (3, 4, 5)**
"'Come back across,' her mouth said, and she had not said it." Then the same line in full in 4, then again, read back by Coralie, at the top of 5.
The two envelopes are opened twice and the two cards read twice. The book reaches the scene where one woman drives the other in daylight only in 6.
*Do:* end 3 before the line is spoken, on the hand turning over. Let 4 own the line. Open 5 on the two boxes, without the quotation.

**8. The box and the rain are raised and dropped. (1, 5, 19)**
"'I don't know either.' They left it there."
The steel room, Dr Kessler and the envelope "which nobody had asked her for since" open the book and never come back. The one attempt at a cause ("That flood is mine") is refuted on the dates in 5 and the question is then shrugged off on the last night. A Crouch reader waits all book for someone to come back for that envelope.
*Do:* either give the question to a character who presses it and pays for not getting an answer, or do not ask it in 19. As written, the last steel room draws attention to its own gap.

**9. Chapter ends that let go. (6 to 7, 9, 10, 11)**
"'I'll see to the range first,' Maylis said." "'The winch,' Maylis said. 'It was about the winch.'" "She did not lie down."
The fourth-stair cliff of 6 is answered in the first lines of 7 by nothing happening. 9 ends on a fib to a secretary. 11 ends on a woman sitting at a table. Four of the five chapters before the night give no reason to turn the page.
*Do:* end 7 on the car with its lamps off behind the Castagnet house. End 10 on the letter falling onto Coralie's envelope, with the reader told what it will cost. End 11 on the hall of the dead and the empty chair, which is at present buried in its middle.

**10. The mayor's village has nothing at risk until 13. (2, 8, 11)**
"a single item, which was whether the mayor should open her mouth on Saturday."
From 2 to 11 the question on that side is whether she will be allowed to give a speech. The eleven unclocked minutes are the funniest thing in the book and nothing can go wrong in them.
*Do:* bring Coralie's call log ("Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one.") forward as a threat before it is a confession: let the reader know by 8 that someone on that side holds the minute, and not who.

## 4. Five things that must not be touched

1. **00 and its replay in 14.** The headlamps down the track, the reflector "screwed on crooked", "She read it, looked away at the gauge and read it again."
2. **The motion at the end of 4.** "*That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*"
3. **"Either" on the platform at five o'clock** with the pawl in its first tooth, and "Both" down the telephone at 02:55.
4. **The clock of 16**, from 02:53 to "Forty-one", including Kévin counting his fingers in the headlamps: "'Five,' he said."
5. **The kitchen clock behind Larrère's voice in 12**, paid by the screen in 16: "Castagnet, house."
