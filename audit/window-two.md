# Window two: chapters 5 to 8

Checked sentence by sentence against `state.md` parts 1 to 4, against the rest of the window, and against chapters 1 to 4 and 9. Single passages of 00, 12, 14, 16 and 17 were opened to settle one fact each.

Part 5 of the state was not read. Its three headings were seen when the file's headings were listed, so it was known beforehand that chapter 5 is said to clash with chapters 1 and 4. The text under them was not opened.

Counts: People 2, Facts 3, Time 1, The world 2, The telling none, Owed none. One state entry is corrected at the end.

## 1. People

### 1.1 Chapter 5, the café at noon

> Maylis had never smiled at Jeannot Castagnet in her life, and the whole café was watching her do it.

Disagrees with chapter 1, six days earlier, at his van window:

> 'Put it in forty-two.'
>
> He laughed. She started to laugh too, until the rib stopped her. He saw it happen.

The two of them share that joke twice (chapter 17: "You told me twice to put it in forty-two"). She has laughed with him to his face.

Mend, in chapter 5: narrow the claim to what is new, which is the smile held on him. For example, "Maylis had never stood and smiled at Jeannot Castagnet in her life". Chapter 1 stays.

### 1.2 Chapter 7, on the platform

> Lesca had taken the watch off Maylis's wrist and was holding it in the left hand, out of the rain.

Disagrees with `ride`: in Maylis's body Lesca has the left hand and no other. A left hand cannot unbuckle a watch from its own wrist. The sentence only works if the watch is on the right wrist, which is Maylis's, and the text does not say which.

Mend, in chapter 7: "off Maylis's right wrist".

## 2. Facts

### 2.1 Chapter 5, the kitchen

> Maylis had stood over that pad every morning for sixteen years and had never got as far as one tick.

Disagrees with chapter 4, two days earlier, on the same pad:

> Maylis picked up the pencil in her left hand. Both answers were true. There was no third box.
>
> She ticked both.

She has made two ticks on that pad since Saturday.

Mend, in chapter 5: say what she has never done, which is choose. For example, "and had never ticked one box and left the other". Chapter 4 stays.

### 2.2 Chapter 6, the café on Tuesday at 4

> Friday, when the town hall's open. You'll have your complaint back.

Disagrees with chapter 1, also a Tuesday, and with `town-hall-hours`:

> He spent his mornings there, until the town hall opened at 2.

> 'The town hall opens at 2.'

Jeannot is speaking at 4 on a Tuesday afternoon, when by chapter 1 the town hall has been open for two hours. Nothing in the book makes Friday its day. The complaint is not at the town hall either: Sarthou "took the form out to his car", and Jeannot says "I go to the gendarmes".

Mend, in chapter 6: "Friday. You'll have your complaint back." Chapter 8's "You've had your complaint back" still holds.

### 2.3 Chapter 5, Jeannot at the café door

> 'Forty-two's started,' Jeannot said. 'You're on its second page.'

Disagrees with chapter 17 and `letters`:

> 'It's not a complaint,' he said. 'It's the first one that isn't.

On 30 November No. 42 is already begun, as a complaint, with her on its second page. The No. 42 he hands over is four pages about the night of 12 December and is "the first one that isn't". Both hold only if he threw away what he had started, and nobody says so.

Mend, in chapter 17 (outside this window): one clause from Jeannot, for example "I started it as one." Chapter 5 stays.

## 3. Time

### 3.1 Chapter 6, the council

> By then he had been on his feet for eleven minutes.

Disagrees with three clock times in the same scene:

> 'Council is at 6:30,' she said.

> At home, in Maylis's bedroom, the clock radio said 18:40.

> The church clock began to strike 7.

The 18:40 cut comes after "At three minutes he stopped for the bell", so he was on his feet by 6:37 at the latest and his eleven minutes end by 6:48. All that is left before the clock strikes is one paragraph from him, the bell and a show of hands. About ten minutes are unaccounted for. The 7 cannot move: "It was five hours late", "It's 7. Thirteen hours" (chapter 7) and the state's count all rest on it.

Mend, in chapter 6: "for twenty minutes".

## 4. The world

### 4.1 Chapter 7, looking back from the hut

> The bank was a dark line across the end of the fields, with the roofs of the Port behind it.

Disagrees with the prologue:

> On the other side of the road was the bank, a wall of clay as high as a house, and behind it the river.

> From up there the river was on one side of her and the fields were on the other, far below, dark and empty.

and with chapter 14, running along the top of the bank from the gate towards the Port:

> The river was on her left, close and loud. On her right, down below, there were no street lamps any more.

> She went down the land side of the bank on her heels, through somebody's rows of beans, into the water.

The Port and the fields are on the same side of the bank, the land side. Only the river is behind it. From the hut, the Port's roofs are on Maylis's side of the bank, along its foot, two hundred metres down from the gate. "Behind the bank" is the book's phrase for the Port as seen from the platform (chapter 3: "Behind it were forty houses"); here it has been carried over to a place where it points the other way.

Mend, in chapter 7: "with the roofs of the Port at one end of it" or "along the foot of it".

### 4.2 Chapter 7, on the platform

> Maylis didn't, but she had nobody to say it to and no mouth to say it with.

Disagrees with `ride` ("the host keeps the right hand and can seize the jaw") and with what she does on either side of this sentence. Chapter 5:

> Maylis got to the mouth first.
>
> 'Either,' she said.

> Maylis felt the mouth hang open, and she took it.

Chapter 8, the same evening:

> Maylis had her own teeth shut.

> The teeth came apart. Maylis got to the mouth first.

She has the mouth whenever she takes it, and holding it is the whole of the bingo scene a few hours later.

Mend, in chapter 7: end the sentence at "nobody to say it to".
