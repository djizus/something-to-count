## 2. Facts

**1. The hour the river was read (chapter 3 against chapter 15).**

Chapter 3: "She had read the river against that line and written down what she saw, one line a day." Then: "On 8 December 2014 the river had reached the line. Three lines later she came to the last one in her own handwriting." That last line is "*11 December 2014, 22:00. Over the line. Rising.*"

Chapter 15: "the red line on the pillar of the old gate, which she had read at 8 every morning".

Three lines after the 8th is one line each for the 9th, 10th and 11th. So the 11th has a single line, and it was written at 22:00. A reading at 8 every morning would have left a morning line for the 11th above it, and the last line would be four lines on, not three.

Mend, in chapter 15: "which she had read every day". Chapter 3 stays as it is.

## 3. Time

**1. Maïté's gutter in a dry month (chapter 3 against chapter 16).**

Chapter 3, Saturday 28 November, stone village: "Lesca set the tile back in its row and cleared the leaves out of the gutter along the roof." The same chapter: "Peyrebarthe had been dry for two weeks."

Chapter 16, Saturday 12 December, same house: "'That back gutter's blocked again. Have a look at it before you go.'" and "The gutter was packed solid. Lesca cleared it by hand, dropping the rotten leaves past Maïté's shoulder in black handfuls."

The calendar (part 3, stone village) has frost on 2 December and dry on the 11th and 12th, with wind on the 12th. A gutter emptied fourteen days earlier, in a village with no rain recorded for a month, cannot be packed solid with rotten black leaves.

Mend, in chapter 16: let the wind have filled it with dry leaves ("The gutter was full again. Lesca cleared it by hand, dropping the dry leaves past Maïté's shoulder in handfuls."). Chapter 3 should not change, because Maylis's question in chapter 4, "Your back gutter. Over the bedroom. Is water coming in?", rests on that afternoon.

**2. Ten minutes that do not run down (chapter 0 against chapter 10).**

Chapter 0, at the foot of the bank where the road ends: "Ten more minutes, she thought. Then it would break." The typed file puts that moment at 02:45 (chapter 6: "*02:45. Water through the bank at the repair beside the last house, across the width of a door. The keyholder gave the bank ten minutes.*"), and the gate goes up at 02:55, which is the ten.

After that thought, chapter 0 has her drive up onto the bank, climb the platform, take out the handle, hold it for three breaths, climb down, turn the van and look down the track. Chapter 10 reaches the same point and says: "The trapdoor was twenty-five minutes away, on foot, in the dark, with the dip under water. The bank had ten." Two lines later: "The screen said 02:51."

By the book's own clock the bank had four minutes left at 02:51, not ten (`night-2014`).

Mend, in chapter 10: "The bank had four." Chapter 0 stays.

## 4. The world

**1. How high the river stands against the bank (chapters 0, 3 and 4 against each other).**

Three things are fixed and cannot all be true.

- The bank is as high as a house, and the houses stand at its foot. Chapter 0: "She drove along the foot of the bank with the wipers on full, past forty houses" and "On the other side of the road was the bank, a wall of clay as high as a house, and behind it the river." Chapter 0 again, from the top: "the fields were on the other, far below". Chapter 5 and chapter 12 agree ("in the bank above his roof"; "From the top of the bank she could see Coralie's back door, across Sarthou's roof").
- The red line is at doorstep height and the river is ninety centimetres over it. Chapter 3: "her father had painted a red line on the pillar at the height of the doorsteps in the Port." Chapter 4: "your river will be ninety centimetres over your father's red line".
- The river is at the top of the bank. Chapter 0: "When she climbed onto the platform and pointed her torch at the water, it was going past just under the boards, close enough to touch." The platform stands on top of the bank ("She drove up onto the bank, to the floodgate"; "she climbed back down to the van and turned it around on top of the bank"). Chapter 3: "That night the river over there would be at the top of the bank".

Ninety centimetres over the doorsteps is about a metre up a bank as high as a house. The water is then several metres below a platform that stands on the top.

The number and the high bank carry the plot: the Port is lost through a breach at the patch, not over the top, and the flood depths later on agree with ninety (a table's height, the windowsills). So the sentences that put the river at the boards are the ones to change. In this window: chapter 0, "it was going past just under the boards, close enough to touch", and chapter 3, "would be at the top of the bank" (for instance "would be ninety over the line"). Outside it, chapter 12 says the same thing four times ("just under the boards of the platform", "The river went by under the boards", "at the height of the boards", "close under the top of the bank"). That is six sentences, and it is the smallest mend I can find: lowering the bank or moving the line each breaks more.

**2. The river at the boards ten hours early (chapter 0 against chapter 12).**

Chapter 0, in the last minutes before the gate is raised, with the river at ninety over: "it was going past just under the boards, close enough to touch."

Chapter 12, Friday 11 December 2026 at 5 pm: "The river was over the red line. It had gone the colour of the sky, and it was very wide. It went past just under the boards of the platform." Later that evening, at the hut, Maylis says: "'It'll be ninety over the line by 3, Jeannot.'" and Jeannot answers that Pau gives less than that.

The river cannot be at the same height against the same platform at 5 pm, short of ninety, and again at ninety over ten hours later.

Mend, in chapter 12: the 5 pm sentence. If World 1 is mended as suggested, this goes with it and needs nothing of its own.

**3. The gate raised on Monday 23 November is never lowered (chapter 1 against chapters 4 and 5).**

Chapter 1: "On Monday the mayor, Guy Larrère, had rung and told her to raise the floodgate down in the river bank." Jeannot: "You put the gate up, and my water ran out through it, down into the river."

Chapter 4, five days on, Lesca: "That floodgate is opened on Friday afternoon." Maylis does not say it is open already. Chapter 5, Monday 30: "At eighteen, the bottom of the gate was ten centimetres clear of the stone it rested on." So the gate is back on its stone, and no sentence puts it there. The calendar has "gate raised, hut drained" on the 23rd and nothing after.

This is a missing step more than a clash, and the lowest item here. It is listed because the whole book turns on whether that gate is up or down. Mend, in chapter 1, a clause in Jeannot's mouth: "You put the gate up, my water ran out through it, down into the river, and you put it down again."
