# Bible

## 1. The woman

Maylis Lesca was 31 on the day of the experiment and is 43 now.

She was the only outdoor employee of the commune of Peyrebarthe, as her father had been until 2008, when the council took her on "for the time being". She cut the verges, dug in the cemetery and set out the trestles for the fête. For an extra €40 a month she held the key to the sluice gate on the flood meadows and turned its winch when the landowners told her to.

She wanted to be asked. For three years she had told the council the winch would seize, and each year the council minuted that it took note. She wanted one decision in the village to be hers.

**Household.** She lives with Coralie Mounet, her partner since 2010, in the small house behind her mother's. The partner is a woman on purpose. A husband would pull the book toward a marriage strained by her office, and Coralie gives her a tie with a woman that is not about a man. The village calls them "the girls at Arlette's". They have no children in either world, because neither wanted one. The village has views. The old women hold that the box did it. Marie-Jo says it is why she has the time. Arlette has stopped asking, and says so often. In the other village they say she has the commune instead.

**How the village treats her.** Writers show these in passing.

- With the outdoor job and the key. Men stop their cars to give her instructions, and the older ones add how her father did it. The delivery driver asks for the man from the commune, then has her sign. A landowner takes the winch handle to show her, and hands it back when it will not turn. At 43 she is "little Lesca".
- As mayor. To her face she is Madame le maire, and behind her back "la Lesca". The prefecture's letters open "Monsieur le Maire". After each decision the café says she was always hard. Larrère explains the gate to her at each commemoration. People with a complaint take it to Coralie at the care home or to Arlette's kitchen.

**Telling the two apart.** Where nothing happened she is Maylis to everyone. In the other village she became the mayor, and her mother is the last person who uses her first name. The narration follows the village and calls one woman Maylis and the other Lesca. Chapters alternate. In the dream each is "she" and the other is "the other one".

## 2. The village

Peyrebarthe had 640 people on the day of the experiment. It lies on the left bank of the Adour between Urt and Urcuit, twenty minutes upriver from Bayonne, in the Pyrénées-Atlantiques, with the Landes on the far bank. On that stretch the families and the place names are as often Gascon as Basque. The prefect sits at Pau and the sub-prefect at Bayonne, where the village goes for a doctor, a roofer or a flat.

The bourg sits on the terrace among kiwi orchards and maize. Below it the Port quarter, forty houses and a care home, stands behind the embankment, the raised bank of clay between the river and the houses. Past the bank lie the barthes, 300 hectares of flood meadow drained to the river through gates. The high barthe grows maize. The low is common grazing, where duck hunters keep tonnes, half-buried cabins with a concrete cellar, used at night from August to January. The landowners' association (the ASA) decides when the sluice opens. Hunters want water and maize growers want none. Glass eels run in winter at €400 a kilo, so men net them at the gates and the biodiversity police (the OFB) raid. The Adour floods in December. The fête is in late July, with a banda, a pelota final at the fronton and a firework bull. The prefecture wants small communes merged, and Peyrebarthe and the next commune, Hontaube, are on its map.

**True on this bank.** The meadows, gates, glass eels, maize, kiwis, night hides, firework bull and tobacco barn are all found here. The course landaise and the duck sheds were the far bank's and are gone.

**The one Basque word.** The creature is Inguma and has no other name. A village of Gascon names has the word because it is a border village, and the old of both kinds are in the same home. Arlette's mother came from the Basque side.

The same eight people live in both villages. The mairie's secretary is Madame Ducasse and the first deputy is Monsieur Tastet. The owner's wife at the café is Josiane. Madame Etcheto has had room twelve for fourteen years. A ninth is minor. Madame Etcheto, ninety-four, a resident of the home, says the words against Inguma each night, and in Maylis's village she drowns in her bed on the last night.

**The joke.** The council rented the old tobacco-drying barn to a university team for a fortnight at €600 and "placed Mlle Lesca at the team's disposal for one afternoon". In both villages anyone who is late is "still in the box". No character knows the afternoon did anything.

## 3. The experiment

**From where she sat.** They built a steel room the size of a garden shed inside the barn, with a chair bolted to the floor and a panel with two lamps. One lamp would come on, once. She was to write "left" or "right" on a card, seal it in an envelope, and press a buzzer once to say she had seen it without saying which. Then she sat until they opened the door, three hours later. She got €180 and a sandwich. The woman in charge was Dr Kessler. They left by the weekend without asking for the envelope. She still has it, unopened.

**For writers.** The panel measured a single particle held in two states at once. Quantum theory applied to the whole sealed room says that, seen from outside, the room then holds both results, with a woman who saw left and a woman who saw right. The team then tried the one measurement that could show both, which means bringing the two results back together. For anything as large and warm as a person it fails. They got noise and published nothing.

The split happened anyway. Maylis saw left and Lesca saw right. An ordinary split is left alone, but this one the team half undid, so the two were never cleanly parted. No character knows this. After the door opened, the two worlds kept the same land, people and grudges, and the weather differed within weeks.

**Calendar.**

- Thursday 12 June 2014: the afternoon in the box.
- Night of Thursday 11 to Friday 12 December 2014, Lesca's world: the flood. The one ring is at 02:51 on the twelfth, so both nights fall on the same date and the nineteenth day of rain.
- April 2015, Lesca's world: seven of fifteen councillors resign over Larrère's order, the village votes again, and the new council elects Lesca mayor. Lesca is re-elected in March 2020 and March 2026.
- Maylis's world: Larrère, mayor since 2008, is re-elected in March 2020 and March 2026, his fourth term.
- Monday 23 November to Sunday 13 December 2026: the book. Maylis's Adour passes the line on Tuesday 8 and comes through at the patch on the night of Friday 11. Lesca's twelfth commemoration is on Saturday 12.
- Saturday 5 December 2026, Maylis's world: the loto.
- 1 January 2027, Maylis's world: the merger with Hontaube takes effect.

## 4. The event, the choices after it, and Maylis's years

**The frame.** Lesca achieved a great deal at a cost and wants Maylis's life so as to forget. Maylis achieved little and wants Lesca's life, with some idea of the price. Merging adds memories, so Lesca would remember more. Lesca has to notice before the end that nothing she receives from Maylis removes anything of her own. From chapter 4 Lesca's wish has an object. She means to have the bed that Coralie still shares, and from chapter 7 she says that she is coming to stay. Maylis wants a day at the mayor's desk and takes one in chapter 8. In chapter 10 she lends Lesca her arms for the one night of the eleventh, against the mayor's chair when she asks for it. Each tells the other to her face.

**The gate.** On the night of 11 to 12 December 2014 the Adour rose against the bank. Larrère phoned the order to keep the sluice shut. Lesca drove to the gate, saw Jeannot's white van at the end of the barthe track, rang his number once, and turned the winch. The river spread into the barthe, and the Port stayed dry. Jeannot drowned in the cellar of his tonne. Walking out to knock would have taken twenty-five minutes, and Lesca gave the bank ten.

**Who was behind the bank.** Coralie was on night duty at the home that night, below the level of the river, with forty-two residents in their beds, most of whom cannot walk. Lesca knew where she was. The sum she has given the council for eleven years is forty houses and a home against one man. The sum she made on the platform had one woman in it. The book never lets her say which decided her. Neither excuses her. She rang once and did not wait, and the private reason makes the public arithmetic a thing she has hidden behind.

**Now.** Lesca got the prefect's medal and a week on national television, and has been mayor since 2015. The village has 900 people, and Hontaube has asked to join it. A stele in the square carries the drowned man's name and, under it, the name of the woman who opened the gate. Maylis has the same job and key, and keeps the winch turning herself. The Port is half shuttered, and on 1 January Peyrebarthe becomes a hamlet of Hontaube.

**The later choices.** The gate was the first of four. As mayor Lesca made three more, and each was right by the numbers and cost one person.

These three are public and minuted, and the café still argues about them. With the gate they are what Maylis envies, since Lesca has had every decision for eleven years. The gate stays the only secret and the only late reveal. No public choice gets a recurring image, so the reader carries the reflector and nothing else.

**Maylis's twelve years.** Three moments in her twelve years are hers.

- January 2017. Jeannot's white van sank to the axles on the barthe track. Maylis towed it out at dawn with the commune's tractor, was docked the diesel, and got letter 17 about the ruts.
- February 2020. Marie-Jo asked her to stand on a list against Larrère. A commune's employee cannot sit on its council, so she would have had to resign her post. She said she would think about it, and the filing date passed.
- December 2022. Kévin offered her €200 a night to leave the gate unlocked in eel season. She refused the money and has looked away for nothing since.

**Who knows about the van.** Père Honoré knows all of it, from her confession in January 2015, and she trusts the seal. Coralie has seen the call log, Jeannot's number at 02:51, unanswered, and she knows where she herself was standing. She does not know Lesca saw the van, and Lesca thinks she knows nothing. She has not asked in twelve years, because she does not want to be told which of the two answers is true. Lesca believes one man knows and cannot speak. Coralie tells her on Friday 11 December, in chapter 12.

**Wants.** Maylis wants a village that waits for her to decide. Lesca wants a village where 12 December is a date. Lesca was right about the houses, and Maylis was never given the ten minutes. When she is, a woman dies behind the bank, so neither is the better woman.

**Glimpses, as Maylis gets them.**

1. Her own name cut in stone in the square. She concludes that she is dead there.
2. Herself in the tricolour sash, and the square full.
3. Grey water lying where the low barthe should be.
4. Kévin Labat on the commune's mower, in her jacket.
5. Maïté in black, taking her kiss on the cheek.
6. A van's rear reflector in headlights at the end of a track, in rain, and her own hand on a winch.

The first three come under the weight, in chapters 1 to 3, before either has crossed, and Maylis concludes from the stone that she is dead there. The others she sees with her own eyes from chapter 8. Lesca sees Jeannot at the café counter, a parking space where the stele should stand, and a gauge with rain on it. On Friday 27 November she opens the file of 2014, the readings agree, and she knows the flood has not come there yet, so no world exists in which she chose better. In the first argument, on the night of 28 November, she tells Maylis the date and what the stone is, with the public sum. The reflector and the one ring stay hidden until the whole night comes through on Thursday 10 December, in chapter 11. The reveal is that Lesca knew whose van it was, rang once, and opened.

## 5. The dream

**How they find each other.** Both have dreamed the steel room for twelve years. Since the flood Lesca has woken under the figure and let it push her back under. In the book's first week Maylis cracks a rib on the winch platform and has to sleep on her back, and it is on her too, at the same minute.

**The room.** It is the box, and both women are in its one chair. While both sit, neither can see the other, because they occupy the same place. Each has what the other is carrying that night, as a memory of her own with the wrong furniture. Both lamps are lit, and the envelope lies open with the card showing the other one's word.

**The figure.** It is a creature, there is one of it, and both villages have a name for it. In Basque tradition Inguma, or Mauma, comes into houses at night and presses on sleepers. People said words against it at lights-out, "Inguma, enauk hire beldur" (Inguma, I am not afraid of you), and then set it to count the stars, the grass and the sand on the coast before it might come. Madame Etcheto, a resident of ninety-four who says them each night while the staff wait at her door, gives Maylis the name and the words in chapter 3. Arlette gives Lesca the use of the count in chapter 4.

This book's Inguma is the size of a ewe, and it is a hunched back and nothing else. The back is dry brown leaf, lapped like tobacco hung to cure. Where the leaves part there is grey plate, with a row of rivets down the spine. It has a back on every side, so it has no face and no hands and never turns round. Cold comes off it. It smells of the steel room and the barn. It does not breathe, and its leaves lift when she breathes in.

At 02:51 it is on top of the wardrobe. It comes down to the floor with the sound of a sack let down, crosses the boards with a dry rustle, and is on the bed in one movement, as a dog gets up. It sits on her breastbone with its back to her and presses as hard as she pushes. If she gives in, it puts her under and she gets faint glimpses. If she stays awake under it without fighting, she reaches the room.

The two bedrooms are one room, as the chair is one chair, and it sits in the place the two women share with a back to each. It has come to that bed every night since the flood, when the two lives first held something to keep apart. Lesca sleeps alone in the middle of the bed, and it turns her onto her back. Maylis slept on her side at the edge for twelve years with it beside her, and the rib put her in its place. Coralie has shaken brown leaf out of the blanket all that time and blamed the plane trees. It comes at 02:51, the minute of the one ring. The cold open shows the ring, chapter 11 shows the night, and Coralie's call log gives the minute in chapter 12.

It does not speak and nobody explains it.

**The three rules.** Lesca states them in chapter 5, at the first meeting, as a mayor states a motion. The narrator says the second flat in chapter 4, straight after it first happens. Nothing else in the book is a rule.

1. One chair. When Inguma sits on them at nine minutes to three and they lie still, both women are in the one chair in the steel room. If they stand up, they can see each other and talk, and neither can lie.
2. Whoever stays goes with her. When one of them wakes, she goes home. If the other is still sitting, she wakes up inside the first one's body, in the first one's village, and drives it. The owner keeps her right hand and can snatch a word or two. The visitor's own body lies in her own bed with its eyes open.
3. Inguma fetches. It always comes and takes the visitor home, and it stays away only while it is counting what it was given. Anybody who says the words can set the count. The one it cannot fetch is found in her bed, and sits at the loto of the dead.

The forum, its don'ts and the man in Limoges are gone. Kévin keeps the finger count, five for awake, which is kept for the flood. A talk ends when Inguma comes in and sits down in the empty chair, and that is shown and not explained. A woman who lies on her side at the edge of the bed is not sat on, and one who pushes under Inguma is not in the chair. The rib allows Maylis neither for long.

**Who is driving.** A ride is told from the passenger, Maylis in 6 and 9 and Lesca in 8, and the driver is known by what the body says and does. Each sentence with an act names who acts, and "the right hand" is always the owner's. Each count is given a size on a clock, aloud. Each change of day opens on the weekday and the date. In narration the two are Maylis and Lesca, always.

**What talking costs.** What is said standing is kept and used. Maylis has the date and the nets from Lesca's mouth, and Lesca has Maylis's wish for the desk from hers.

**What a writer may repeat.** "Thirty-five kilos" is Maylis's guess in chapter 1 and may return twice. The smell is named only when it turns up somewhere new, such as the car, and at most once a chapter. Its full coming, from wardrobe to floor to bed, is told once for each woman. A night that brings nothing new gets one clause. Each described appearance shows it doing one thing it has not done before, or in a new place. The leaf on the blanket appears three times in the book.

**Why it is right.** She can answer for a life only if the acts in it are hers. Each crossing leaves one of them with something the other did.

**The middle, chapters 6 to 10.** See `middle.md`. It is one fight in three rounds. Lesca takes Maylis's body for a day, is answered in kind, and comes back to keep it. Beaten in daylight by the man she is trying to keep alive, Maylis hires the woman she threw out. The plan to open the gate by day changes at each step: told, found to need Larrère twice over, written over his head, asked for aloud and voted down.

The one Inguma cannot fetch is found in her bed and sits there, which is Madame Etcheto's saying from chapter 3. In chapter 10 Maylis walks to the place and reads the card, LESCA, MAYLIS. In chapter 14 the place is called for Lesca, and she sends her apologies. In chapter 16 the voice calls twelve, and a chair is pulled in.

## 6. The ending

**The two motions.** After the first crossing Lesca writes that the mayor remain seated and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it. Maylis writes a handover on the back of the shopping pad in chapter 10, for one night. The night of 2014, which she is given whole in chapter 11, ends it. She lets his telephone ring nine times, and in chapter 13 she puts her pencil through each line.

**The fight.** In chapter 13 Maylis has the handle on the shaft at five, in daylight, and says "Either" on the platform. Larrère has told her that if the gate moves before he rings, the letter is dated and the sheet with Kévin's name goes to the prosecutor. At dusk she knocks at the tonne and the hatch is bolted on her. She stays awake, and at nine minutes to three Inguma comes down on the roof of the van and holds her, with the telephone starting to ring. In the room, in chapter 14, she puts herself between Lesca and the chair and says, "You rang once." Inguma comes in by the door and takes the chair. Lesca sits down on its back and holds, and goes with her.

**The cab.** It is told in Lesca's chapter. Lesca has the body and the mouth, and Maylis has her right hand. Maylis's hand goes to the key of the gate, as she planned. Lesca goes for the ignition, to drive the track herself. With one woman at the wheel and the other at the gear lever, the van stalls. Lesca's choice is written out: hold, and keep this bed, and nobody drives, or let go, and go home to the stone. She lets go on the eighth ring, and her last use of the mouth is the five words. They are said nowhere before, and they are what sends Maylis to the man.

**The hall refused.** She lands on the waxed boards and not in her bed. Her name is called to the empty place. She sends her apologies, since she has a commemoration at eleven and a third sheet to put right. Her mother's count of ponies is done, and Inguma fetches her. Coralie has found her with her eyes open and rung Bayonne, so it sets her down on a stretcher there, before six.

**The last night.** It is chapter 15. At 02:53 Maylis takes Larrère's order. At the patch Coralie asks which she wants, the gate or Castagnet, and she says "Both". At the dip the van sinks. On foot it is one or the other, and she goes to the man. The bank holds sixteen minutes, and her own van stands across the track in front of his. Every timestamp stands to 03:11, when the bank goes. Coralie kept to her plan and took those who could walk first. Madame Etcheto has drowned in her bed in room twelve. The last word is "Forty-one".

**The last meeting.** It is on Sunday at nine minutes to three, in chapter 18, after both Saturdays. Inguma sits on them once more and they stand. Maylis gives the count: forty-one, of forty-two. She offers Lesca the acquittal, and Lesca will not sign it. She was right about the houses, and she rang once. "What was the verb?" "Drowned." Neither knows why it rained. Then Inguma goes.

**What it costs Lesca.** The commemoration is played in full, and at the third sheet she says at the microphone that she saw his van, rang once and opened the gate. Then the prefect leaves, his office asks for the medal back and suspends her, the gendarmerie wants her on Monday, and seven councillors have signed for Tuesday. Marie-Jo uses her first name. That evening Maïté holds her to "Which were you counting?", and she has only the sum. Maïté puts her own question to Larrère at the garden gate, and he has nothing to say. On the Saturday night Lesca tells Coralie all of it, including the hand she held over there, and asks. Coralie keeps the pillow where it is until they have voted. On Monday Lesca lays the telephone on a desk at Bayonne. On Tuesday the council sits without her in the chair, and that night Coralie carries the pillow across.

**What it costs Maylis.** Larrère dates the letter, so she digs as nobody's employee. Sarthou's file has one name in it, and it is hers. The Port tells her to her face that she had the key, and the café has a joke about a walk. Coralie's leaving takes a scene of its own. She says what "Both" was, that on the landing she chose Maylis over somebody who asked, and that she has wanted the post at Bayonne since March. "Stay" is not an answer to what Maylis wants. She carries her pillow down to the car. Asked what she wants, she wants to dig the grave herself. Jeannot brings letter 42 by hand and takes the second shovel.

**The shape.** Each ends with what the other had. Lesca has no office and a woman in her bed. Maylis has a decision that the village holds against her, and an empty landing.

**For writers.** Each choice costs one person. One woman opened the gate on a man. The other walked away from it, and a woman died behind the bank. The cellar stays dry, because Maylis never opens the gate, so her walk saves a man who was in no danger. That is meant. Nobody says what the death means. Which reason decided Lesca at the gate is never said, because she does not know.

## 7. Shape

The book is a cold open and eighteen chapters, about 44,000 words. `middle.md` governs chapters 6 to 10, and `revision8.md` and `revision9.md` the rest. ## 10. Rules for writers

- The text never names the trolley problem or any philosopher.
- French words appear only where English has none, clear from context the first time. The Basque is Inguma and the words said against it. No glossary, no tourism, no beret, and no politics unless a villager brings it up in passing.
- She is never described by her body, her looks or how men see her. She does not look in a mirror.
- She has no backstory of assault and no longing for a child.
- Her choices keep their arithmetic and their ambition. She rang once and opened. She is written no softer, no sorrier and no more excused than a man would be.
- She is allowed to be funny, petty and wrong.
- The text puts people in the scale: who sleeps behind the shutters, who is in the beds. "Forty houses" is Lesca's phrase, and she uses it in council.
- She has at least two ties with other women that are not about a man. They are Coralie and Arlette. Marie-Jo is a third, through the fête and the list.
- The figure is a creature, a back of leaf and steel that sits on her breastbone turned away from her. It never lies along her, touches her with a limb, comes between her legs or reads as a man in her bed. Nothing about it is sexual, in image or in implication.
- Nothing is staged twice. An event is played once, in the village where it costs most. The other woman gets a line of it and does the wrong thing with it in front of people.
- The wrong thing in public is the engine. Lafitte keeps Maylis's slips on the back of the café's score pad. Larrère has Lesca's minuted as facts of the sitting.
- Every chapter names its woman in its first sentence, makes plain what she is trying to do next, and ends on a threat, an act or a question. Lesca's aim does not change: she is going over to stop it, first by telling, then by taking the night. Maylis's changes once. From chapter 7 she wants the mayor's desk, and from chapter 10 she wants the gate opened by day, and failing that she lends her arms for one night.
- Each crossing is told once, from the side that learns most, and the other woman gets a line. The way in is told in full once, in chapter 4, and is a clause everywhere else. Fingers are counted only where somebody sees it.
- The pluperfect marks the first verb of every step back in time.
- No noun is withheld. Back-history is argued by two people in a room or it is cut. The numbers a reader is asked to carry are 02:51, nineteen, forty houses, forty-two, forty-one and ten against twenty-five. A gloss comes once, at rest.
- No decision of the mayor's is summarised. Somebody she cost argues it with her.
- Once named, the creature is Inguma. "It" means Inguma only when no other noun stands between.
- Each habit of speech has one owner. Marie-Jo counts and says so. Arlette is "not asking". Jeannot talks at length and numbers his grievances. Kévin shrugs. Sarthou speaks reports and makes no remarks. Coralie refuses the measurement in Maylis's village and asks questions. In Lesca's she gives instructions and keeps the title to the end. Larrère has "They tell me" and "I'll be brief". Père Honoré uses no contractions. Lesca is long and certain, speaks first and over-answers, and her minute on the forty houses is heard in full in chapter 2, and once more in chapter 6, in the other village's café. Maylis weighs, paces and prices what she meets, and gives short wrong answers on purpose, and "Either" costs her something in public each time. No villager is one tag, and Jeannot and Larrère each break theirs once.
- The flood defence is the embankment or the bank. The word "dyke" is never used.
- "Never explained" covers the figure's nature, the room and the hall. It does not cover how a gate works or what an experiment was for. Section 11 lists what the text says plainly, and where.

## 11. What the text makes plain

1. **What the box was for.** Chapter 1, in the bedroom, inside the first 600 words, in Dr Kessler's words to her: one of two lamps will come on, she will see one, and they want to know whether somewhere she sees the other. They opened the door, counted one of her and went home. Coralie says it again in chapter 4, in 2014.
2. **What the gate does.** Chapter 3 says it once, at the winch. The red line marks the height of the doorsteps in the Port. With the river over it, the bank is holding the river above the houses. Opening the gate then gives the river 300 hectares to spread into and takes it off the bank, and whatever is out on the meadows goes under.
3. **Why a hide wants water.** Chapter 1, at the first complaint. Duck come down at night only to standing water.
4. **The eels and the padlock.** Chapter 3, at the van. Licensed boats alone may fish glass eels, out on the river. Netting at a gate is poaching and carries prison. The padlock is on the gate platform, from which a net can be let down into the throat of the sluice.
5. **Why it sat on one of them for twelve years.** As set out under the figure. Madame Etcheto says it in chapter 3. It has not come for Maylis. It has come to the bed, and this week she is lying where it sits.

**The rain.** No cause is stated, and nobody sent it. The two skies have not matched since the June of the box. The rain of 2014 fell on one village, and the other had a dry winter. In Maylis's village it has not stopped raining since 24 November 2026, the first night both women lay under the creature at the same minute, and the river has followed the readings of 2014 day for day since. That night came the day after the rib, and Lesca first went in on purpose on the 26th. So her claim that she let the flood across fails against the dates, and Maylis breaks it in the first argument. What Lesca did start is dated and hers: a man who goes down on the Friday because she forbade it, a folder with a resignation in it, and a list at the home in which those who walk go first.

The reader is given the calendar of 2014 in chapter 2, in Lesca's minute, and learns with her, at the end of that chapter, that the flood has not come to the other village yet. Every reading after that is a countdown. Maylis is told on the night of 28 November.

Never paid: which reason decided Lesca at the gate. Marked as hidden in chapter 2, where Maïté asks whether she knew he was out and gets "No one came to tell him", and paid in chapters 11 and 16. The reader has Larrère and Maïté by chapter 7, from the two bowls and his car in the back lane, so chapter 17 keeps only the telephone and the clock. Also withheld and paid: the verb missing from the stone, what she told the priest, the suitcase, why the hunter will not name Larrère, and why 02:51. The hall is shown and not explained.
