Something to Count

Vera Shandles

The night of 11 December 2014

It had rained for eighteen days, until the river stood higher than the doorsteps of the houses beside it.

She drove along the foot of the bank with the wipers on full, past forty houses that had their shutters closed and their people asleep inside. On the other side of the road was the bank, a wall of clay as high as a house, and behind it the river. At the end of the row the care home had one light on, over its back door.

She stopped where the road ended and got out in the rain.

Water was coming through the bank. It ran down the clay in a sheet as wide as a door and crossed the road in her headlights. She put her hand flat on the bank beside the water and felt the clay move under her palm.

She had repaired that bank every spring for six years, so she knew what it could take. Ten more minutes, she thought. Then it would break.

She drove up onto the bank, to the floodgate. From up there the river was on one side of her and the fields were on the other, far below, dark and empty. The gate was there to let the river out onto those fields when it ran too high. It was raised by hand, from a platform of boards beside it. When she climbed onto the platform and pointed her torch at the water, it was going past just under the boards, close enough to touch.

She took the handle out of its box.

She held it for three breaths. Then she climbed back down to the van and turned it around on top of the bank, until its headlights pointed out over the fields, along the one track that crossed them.

She looked down that track while the wipers crossed the windscreen twice.

Then she took out her phone and rang a number that was already in it. She held the phone to her ear while the rain ran off her hood.

It rang once.

Tuesday 24 November 2026, 2:51 a.m.

Maylis Lesca was awake in her own bed and couldn’t move.

She tried her right hand. Nothing. She tried her left, then both feet, then her jaw. Only her eyes still worked. She could see the ceiling, the paper lampshade, the top of the wardrobe and the red numbers of the clock radio. Rain was falling on the roof.

Something was sitting on the wardrobe.

It was about the size of a sheep and darker than the ceiling behind it. As she watched, it came down. It hit the floor like a full sack dropped off a trailer. When it crossed the room she heard dry leaves dragging over the boards. It jumped onto the bed, sat down on her chest and turned its back to her.

It was covered in overlapping brown leaves, and where the leaves parted there was grey steel underneath, with a row of rivets. It was heavy. She dug graves and lifted sacks of cement for a living, but she had never had to lift a sack off her own chest. It smelled of cold steel and old tobacco, in a house where nobody had ever smoked. She knew that smell.

She pushed up against the thing with all the breath she had, which wasn’t much. She had cracked a rib on Monday, and that was why she was sleeping on her back in the first place. The thing pressed down just as hard. Beside her Coralie slept on, turned to the wall.

At 2:54 the little finger of her right hand moved against the blanket. The rest of the hand followed. The thing got down off her and left the way it had come, and by the time Coralie turned over there was nothing on top of the wardrobe.

Coralie found Maylis’s wrist, put two fingers on the inside of it and watched the clock.

‘Your pulse is 112,’ she said. ‘What’s wrong?’

‘It’s the rib.’

‘A rib doesn’t give you 112. Have you been out of bed?’

‘I’ve been lying here,’ Maylis said.

‘Then you’re bad at it. I’ve got people of ninety who can lie on their backs all night without waking anybody.’

‘They’ve had the practice. This is my first night.’

Coralie shut her eyes again, but she kept her fingers where they were. ‘Do you need the toilet? At work that’s what it is, nine times out of ten.’

‘And the tenth?’

‘The tenth, we ring the priest.’

‘Don’t,’ Maylis said. ‘He’d only ring me to dig the hole.’

Coralie laughed into the pillow. ‘It’s slowing down,’ she said. ‘You’ll live. I can get you water, a pill or my pillow. What do you want, Maylis?’

‘Nothing. Go back to sleep.’

What she wanted was for Coralie to stay awake and keep her fingers on that wrist. But Coralie had to be up at 6, so Maylis didn’t ask.

She stayed awake and thought about the smell instead. It was the smell of the old tobacco-drying barn at the end of the village, where she had once sat for an afternoon and been paid for it.

Twelve years earlier, in June 2014, the village council had rented the barn to a team from a university. Inside it the team had built a steel room the size of a garden shed. The room had one chair in it and a panel with two lamps. A lot of steel for one chair.

‘One of these two lamps will come on,’ the woman from the university had told her at the door. ‘You’ll see which one. What we want to know is whether there is another you, somewhere, who sees the other one.’

Maylis asked where somewhere was. The woman said that was the question.

One lamp came on. Maylis wrote down which one on a card, put the card in an envelope and closed it. They opened the door, paid her, gave her a sandwich and went back to their university. The envelope went home with her, into a drawer.

She slept a little towards morning. When she woke there was a picture in her head that hadn’t been there before: a stone, standing in front of the town hall where the mayor parked his car. Grey granite, as high as her shoulder. Somebody had put it up straight. A name was cut into it in capitals: MAYLIS LESCA. Above hers was another name, which she couldn’t read. Under hers was a date. That she could read: 12 DECEMBER. A year came after it, but that part of the picture wouldn’t stay in her head.

Maylis dug the graves in Peyrebarthe, so she knew what a name and a date on a stone meant. It was the 24th of November. She counted the days. Eighteen.

She treated the stone as one more job and told nobody.

Coralie had left before it was light. She worked at the care home down in the Port, at the end of the forty houses that stood on the low ground behind the river bank.

Coralie’s tomato plant on the kitchen windowsill still had one green tomato on it. Maylis would have pulled it up in October. The shopping pad was on the kitchen table. It said Tonight, then soup, then the rest of the duck, with a box drawn beside each choice. Maylis held the pencil over the duck. Then she wrote Either across the bottom and went out to the van. She was going to the square first, to look at the place where the mayor parked.

* * *

It rained all day Tuesday, straight down. Maylis was the village’s only outdoor worker, so she spent the day in the square, raking leaves off the drain covers under the plane trees. Two trailers of leaves a week came off that square. Eighteen days made five trailers.

She raked left-handed, because of the rib. On Monday the mayor, Guy Larrère, had rung and told her to raise the floodgate down in the river bank. The handle had stuck and thrown her against the rail.

His car was parked in front of the town hall, in the space where the stone stood in her picture. Wherever she started, she raked her way back to it. The roof came up to her shoulder. So had the stone. Larrère was at the wheel with the engine running. He spent his mornings there, until the town hall opened at 2. By her count that was a tank of diesel a week, to go nowhere.

The post van came around the church. The postman was new, so he went to Larrère’s window. Larrère lowered it a few centimetres, which was as far as it went for anybody.

‘I’ve got a registered letter for the mayor,’ the postman said into the gap. ‘Who is also the president of the landowners’ association, it says here. The town hall’s shut. Where can I find him?’

‘The town hall opens at 2.’

The postman turned to Maylis. Her yellow raincoat had COMMUNE DE PEYREBARTHE across its back.

‘Is the man who works for the village around?’

‘That’s me,’ Maylis said.

He looked at the rake. He looked at Larrère, who was looking at the church. Then he held out the receipt card and a pen.

She signed in the box, M. Lesca, with her father’s tall L. Rain fell on the card and the ink of the L ran. The L cut in the stone wouldn’t run.

‘If you see the mayor,’ the postman said through the gap, ‘can you tell him it’s come?’

‘I’ll tell him,’ said Larrère.

The postman gave Maylis the envelope and drove away.

‘You didn’t have to sign so quickly,’ Larrère said. ‘Put it in my tray. I’ll be brief. The handle.’

He closed his right hand around nothing and turned it behind the glass. ‘Your father turned it with one hand. Like that.’

‘It stuck on Monday,’ she said. ‘At nineteen turns.’

‘So there’s the question of the handle,’ he said. ‘It isn’t settled, and you’re not to worry about it.’

The window went up.

She had written to him three times about that handle. She went back to the leaves.

Jeannot Castagnet had watched from his white van, outside the café. Now he drove over and rolled his window all the way down.

‘I watched him not sign it,’ he said. ‘I’ve paid for forty-one registered letters, just to get that man’s name on a card, and it’s little Lesca who signs for them.’ He nodded towards the flood fields, where he shot ducks from his hut. It was a cellar dug into the field, under a trapdoor in the grass. ‘One. The water down there is mine. A duck comes down onto standing water. On Sunday night I had a hand’s width of water on those fields.’ He laid his hand flat on the van door. ‘That deep. Two. That gate lets water through both ways. On Monday the river was lower than my fields. You put the gate up, and my water ran out through it, down into the river. Then you put it down again. Last night I sat over mud.’

‘I was told to raise it.’

‘Three,’ Jeannot said. He was looking at Larrère’s car. ‘He had that gate raised to let my water out. That’s his answer to letter forty.’

‘Put it in forty-two.’

He laughed. She started to laugh too, until the rib stopped her. He saw it happen.

‘What have you done to yourself?’

‘A rib. The handle stuck.’

‘Then that goes in forty-two as well,’ he said. He wasn’t laughing any more.

He drove off. Maylis stood the rake upright beside the mayor’s car. Larrère looked at the church. She opened her pocket knife and cut eighteen marks down the rake, starting level with her shoulder.

* * *

Maylis came home at 7 that evening and found Coralie standing in the kitchen with the shopping pad in her hand.

‘I don’t cook Either,’ Coralie said. ‘There’s no recipe for it. Soup or duck.’

‘Whichever’s less trouble.’

‘That’s Either again, with more words.’

She dropped the pad on the table and pulled the dish towel off the oven door. Maylis was still standing there in her raincoat with her hair wet. Coralie rubbed it dry with the towel, hard.

‘The hill was clear of leaves when I drove down this morning, every bend of it. You do my road before the square, every autumn, and you’ve never once said so. Sixteen years, and you’ve told me once what you wanted. It was me. Eggs, then. Eggs are what Either gets in this house.’

Sixteen years earlier, in August 2010, Coralie had found her behind the tent on the last night of the village festival. Maylis was stacking chairs. Coralie took the top one out of her hands.

‘You’ve carried my shopping up from the Port twice now, and never said why. What do you want, Maylis?’

‘You,’ Maylis said.

Coralie put the chair back on the stack herself.

Now Coralie broke eggs into the pan. Maylis sat down a little at a time, the way the rib wanted it done, and told her about the registered letter, the postman and the man in the car who was looking at the church. She left out the stone.

‘He said he’d tell him?’ Coralie said.

‘He said, “I’ll tell him.” That’s forty-one Jeannot’s paid for. He’s spent more on stamps than the association has spent on its gate.’

Coralie laughed, with her head back.

Then she put the fork down, opened the oven door a few centimetres and bent down to speak through the gap.

‘I’ll be brief,’ she said, low, from the back of the throat. ‘The eggs. There’s the question of the eggs. It isn’t settled, and you’re not to worry about it.’ She looked at the oven shelf. ‘In my tray,’ she said, and shut the oven door.

Maylis laughed. The laugh got as far as the rib and the rib hurt. She pressed her forearm against her side, shut her eyes and went on laughing as much as she could.

‘Don’t,’ Coralie said. ‘No. Wait.’

She took the pan off the heat and went to get the first-aid box she had brought home from work. Maylis stayed in her chair while Coralie wrapped the wide bandage around her three times, over the T-shirt, and pulled it tight.

‘Now you can.’

‘It’s gone.’

‘Then I’ll do the handle,’ Coralie said. She faced the oven door, closed her hand around nothing and turned it. The laugh came back.

They ate the eggs out of the pan. Afterwards Coralie ticked both boxes herself, wrote eggs under them, drew a third box and ticked that one too.

At 10 Coralie pulled the blanket off the bed and shook it out. A few crumbs of brown leaf fell on the floor, as they had most nights for twelve years.

‘Those plane trees,’ she said. ‘You bring the square home in your hair.’

She got into bed on the side by the wall and was asleep before the light was off. Maylis turned it off at 10:10. Then she was lying on her back, looking at the top of the wardrobe.

At 2:51, the same minute as the night before, the thing came down off it again and sat on her chest.

This time somebody was under it with her. Not Coralie. Coralie was asleep by the wall.

2

On Wednesday it was still raining. Maylis woke up with another picture in her head.

In this one she was standing at the town hall door with the mayor’s sash across her chest, blue, white and red. The square in front of her was full of people, all waiting for her to begin. In eighteen years the only thing that square had ever waited for her to do was move the tractor. The stone was still in her head too. Seventeen days.

Her father had always had the Christmas lights up in the plane trees by Saint Catherine’s Day. That was today. With the rib she needed help to hang them, which gave her a reason to stop at the Labats’ door on the way. Something had sat on Kévin Labat’s chest as well. He had told the whole village about it. She wanted to know how he had got out from under it. She told his mother about the lights. By the time she had the shed unlocked, Kévin was standing at its door.

‘My mum says you’ve cracked a rib, and you’ve got nine strings of lights to hang. Does it hurt? Can I go up in the bucket?’

‘The village won’t pay you,’ Maylis said.

‘It never has. Is that a yes?’

His van was parked by the first plane tree. He opened the back doors to get his gloves, and she saw two blue cool boxes tied along one side. Boxes like that were for glass eels, the baby eels that came up the river on the winter tides. A kilo of them sold for more than she earned in a week. A man could go to prison for catching them at a floodgate. Maylis kept the key to the floodgate, and to the padlock on its platform, so she turned her back on the open doors.

‘You could look,’ he said. ‘All these winters, and you never have. Don’t you want to know?’

‘I’ve got lights to hang.’

He left his phone on the dashboard of the village tractor, out of the rain. Then he climbed into the bucket on the front of the tractor with a string of lights over his shoulder, and she sent him up into the tree, working the levers left-handed. He pulled the string tighter than she would have.

At 9 the church clock struck and the phone buzzed on the dashboard. Up in the bucket Kévin let go of the string and held his left hand in front of his face. He touched each finger in turn with his right hand, moving his lips. Then he picked up the string again.

‘What are you counting?’ she called up.

‘Fingers.’ He leaned on the edge of the bucket. ‘It’s a thing you can learn, waking up inside your dreams. The phone goes off on the hour and you count. Five, and you’re awake. In a dream the hand comes out wrong. Six, or seven, or they won’t keep still. Then you know, and you can do what you like. I want to fly. As high as the church. Is that stupid?’

‘And if you lie down and you can’t move?’

‘With something sitting on you? I’ve had that twice, wide awake. I pushed it off both times. I was so scared. Have you?’

‘I pushed,’ she said. ‘With a cracked rib.’

‘The thing on your chest is the way in,’ Kévin said. ‘Don’t push it off. Lie still under it and you’re in the dream, wide awake.’

* * *

Coralie was on the late shift. She had said once that the old woman in room twelve talked every night to something on top of her wardrobe. Maylis wanted to hear what was said to it. So at 9:45 that night she drove down to the Port to pick Coralie up.

The care home had its night lights on and the lamp lit over the back door. Coralie was in the corridor, at the end nearest the bank. She was standing outside the last door in her work uniform, with her arms folded. The door had the number twelve on it. It wasn’t quite shut.

‘Two minutes,’ Coralie said quietly. ‘I can’t go till she’s done. It’s in the care plan.’

‘What is?’

‘Listen.’

Inside, the old woman was speaking slowly to somebody.

‘Inguma, enauk hire beldur.’

‘That’s Madame Etcheto. She’s ninety-four. She says that every night to a thing that sits on her chest. The words mean she’s not afraid of it.’ Coralie put her mouth to Maylis’s ear. ‘Now she gives it its work.’

‘You’ll count the stars,’ the voice said. ‘The ones behind the cloud as well. You’re not to come till you’ve finished.’

‘Does it work?’ Maylis whispered.

‘Till 3,’ Coralie said. ‘Then it comes anyway, and Madame Etcheto rings her bell for us.’

A boy with a mop was standing at the far end of the corridor. He stayed where he was.

‘That’s Bixente. He drives the minibus. He got his Basque from his grandmother, and in his first week he said the words along with Madame Etcheto, through the door, to be friendly. She reported him. She said he had made it lose its count, and it came at 1 instead of 3.’ Coralie’s shoulders had started to shake. ‘It’s in the plan now. Staff to wait. Staff not to join in.’

Maylis laughed. The rib caught the laugh and turned it into a bark.

‘Who’s that at my door?’

Coralie pushed the door open.

‘It’s Coralie, Madame Etcheto. And Maylis, who’s come to take me home. She’s the one who repaired the lamp over the back door, and she’ll tell you she didn’t.’

Madame Etcheto was lying against two pillows with her hands on top of the blanket, facing her wardrobe. Her eyes went to the arm Maylis held across her side and stayed there.

‘The village’s girl,’ Madame Etcheto said. ‘Your father dug my husband’s grave.’ Her voice was strong. ‘You were laughing.’

‘It was the rib.’

‘A rib doesn’t laugh. What’s wrong with it?’

‘She cracked it on Monday,’ Coralie said. ‘She has to sleep on her back. And on Tuesday at 3 in the morning she was awake with a pulse of 112, and she tells me that was the rib as well.’

‘Where did you sleep before Monday?’

‘On my side. At the edge.’

Madame Etcheto nodded.

‘That’s Inguma. It has a name, so use it. What do you have a great many of?’

‘Leaves,’ Maylis said. ‘In the square. Two trailers a week.’

‘Give it those. Go on. I’ve said mine.’

Maylis looked at Coralie. Coralie had stopped laughing.

‘It has to count the leaves in the square,’ Maylis said.

‘Before it comes,’ said Madame Etcheto.

‘Before it comes.’

‘That will do for one night.’ Madame Etcheto shut her eyes. ‘It hasn’t come for you, girl. It comes to your bed. You’ve been lying where it sits since Monday, and you’ve given it nothing to count. My mother told me the rest. Some nights it takes the sleeper away with it and leaves her body in the bed. Before morning it takes her home. The one it cannot take home dies in that bed.’

She opened her eyes.

‘After that she plays bingo with the rest of the dead.’

‘With cards?’ Coralie said.

‘A card of numbers each, and grains of corn to mark it. They lay a place for her at their table.’

‘You said it comes to Maylis’s bed. I’m in that bed too,’ Coralie said. ‘When has it ever sat on me?’

* * *

That night, on her back in bed, Maylis said the words quietly to the top of the wardrobe.

‘Inguma, enauk hire beldur.’

It sounded like something she was reading off a label.

In room twelve she had given Inguma the leaves in the square. But she had raked the square that afternoon, right down to the drain covers.

That left the stars, which Madame Etcheto gave it herself. Maylis could give it the ones she could see, or the ones behind the cloud as well.

‘Either,’ Maylis said.

At its usual minute Inguma came down off the wardrobe and landed on the floor.

* * *

On Friday evening Maylis opened the top drawer of the chest of drawers and took out the envelope. It had been in there since the June of the steel room. The glue had held.

That morning she had woken with a third picture in her head. This one was grey water. It didn’t move. It was where the flood fields should have been, from the foot of the bank to the far trees. None of the three pictures was hers. But the first had her name in it, over a date that was fifteen days away now. And that steel box had been built to find out whether there were two of her.

She remembered which lamp had come on. She cut the envelope open with her pocket knife and pulled out the card.

left

That was the lamp she had seen. She put the card in the chest pocket of her raincoat.

At 2:50 that night she counted her fingers. Five. When Inguma came she didn’t push. Somebody had been under there with her since Tuesday night, and she wanted to know who.

Then she was in the chair in the steel room. The arms of the chair were warm, though she hadn’t been sitting there long enough to warm them. She counted her fingers again. Five.

The panel was in front of her, with both its lamps lit. On a shelf under the panel was a brown envelope, with the card pulled half out of it.

right

It was in her own handwriting.

A long way off, Coralie’s alarm rang. Maylis got up out of the seat. Somebody was still sitting in it.

Then she saw the lampshade and felt two fingers on the inside of her wrist. Her left hand came across and closed over Coralie’s fingers. Maylis hadn’t told it to.

‘Come back across. I’ve left you on that landing eleven years,’ her mouth said.

Lesca, the mayor, was lying on her back in a bed that wasn’t hers, listening to the rain on the roof. The room could have been her own. The paper lampshade: hers. The top of the wardrobe: hers. The clock radio, 06:00 in red: hers. The rain: not hers. No rain had fallen on her own roof for two weeks. Pillows: two. At home she had one. On the back of the door hung a yellow raincoat with COMMUNE DE PEYREBARTHE across its shoulders. She had handed that coat in with her keys when she became mayor.

She breathed in, and a rib caught on the right side. There was nothing wrong with her own ribs.

A minute earlier she had been asleep in a steel chair, with somebody warm sitting in it beside her. Then an alarm had rung a long way off. The warmth had got up to go. Lesca had stayed sitting, and it had taken her with it.

Coralie was lying between her and the wall, warm from the bed, with her hair flat on one side. She reached across Lesca to stop the alarm. At home that alarm rang on the other side of the landing.

Coralie sat up, lifted the right wrist off the blanket, put two fingers on the inside of it and watched the clock radio.

‘Your pulse is 130,’ she said. ‘You were lying there with your eyes open again. What is it this time?’

An answer went past Lesca on its way to the mouth. It was about the rib, and it wasn’t hers. Maylis was in here too, and she was about to say it.

Lesca got in first. She told the left hand to move and it moved. She brought it across and closed it over Coralie’s fingers.

They were warm, and rough at the tips from the soap at work. Under Coralie’s fingers the right wrist began to pull away. Lesca wasn’t the one pulling. Maylis was. Lesca held on with the left hand while the pull went up the right arm and into the cracked rib.

‘Maylis?’ Coralie said.

Lesca told the mouth to open. It was stiff, but it was hers to use.

‘Come back across. I’ve left you on that landing eleven years.’

The voice came out low.

Coralie kept her fingers where they were, on the pulse.

‘Across what?’ she said. ‘I’m here. I’m in the bed.’ She was looking at the mouth. ‘And you don’t ask. You’ve never once asked me for anything. I’d have come for half that sentence, from the far end of the Port. Where did you get eleven?’

She bent down and looked into one eye, then into the other. Then she took her fingers back. She had to pull to get them.

‘Who is that?’

She got out of the bed and stood beside it, holding her fingers in her other hand.

Lesca opened the mouth to tell her, but the jaw was pulled out of her hold.

‘The rib,’ the mouth said, in a voice higher than Lesca’s.

Two words were all Maylis could get out before Lesca had the jaw back. By then the jaw was no use to Lesca. The room had gone cold. The thing was on the chest with its back to her, just as it had been on her own chest every night for twelve years. It took her home.

She was in her own bed, under a dry roof. Clock radio: 06:20. Pillows: one. Coralie was coming around the bed in her dressing gown with the phone in her hand. The thing got down.

‘Twenty minutes,’ this Coralie said. ‘Lie still. Your eyes were open and I couldn’t wake you, so I’ve rung your mother. Give me that wrist, Madame le maire.’

She took it between a finger and thumb and watched the clock. Lesca let her have it.

‘It’s 90, and coming down.’ She laid the wrist back on the blanket. In this village she ran the care home in the Port.

She was at the door when Lesca said her name.

‘Madame le maire?’

Lesca had the sentence ready. This was the woman it was for.

‘Nothing. Go back to bed.’

The spare room was four steps away, across the landing. Coralie had carried her pillow over there in the February after the flood, and the pillow had stayed. The door shut.

Lesca was lying in the middle of the bed with her left hand closed.

In June 2014 the woman from the university had asked her whether, somewhere, she also saw the other lamp. Somewhere was another Peyrebarthe.

So she could cross.

* * *

There was a white frost on the square that morning. Lesca left her car by the church and crossed on foot. She had no parking space, because the one in front of the town hall door had been given to the stone.

The stone faced the square, so most days she walked past the back of it. That Saturday she went around to the front. The top line was JEAN CASTAGNET, who had been Jean in the town hall’s files and Jeannot at the café. Under him, in the same capitals, was MAYLIS LESCA. Under that was the date. There was frost in the bottom of each letter.

He had drowned on the night of the flood. In his whole life he had sent the town hall twelve letters. But something had come home with her from that bed, the way mud comes home on a boot. She remembered signing for a registered letter from him on Tuesday, in the rain, with a rake in her other hand. It was his forty-first. Lesca hadn’t held a rake since she handed in the coat.

Over there he was alive and still writing.

She didn’t trust it. She had wanted him alive for twelve years, and she had nothing to check it against.

She stood in front of the top line with her keys in her hand and read his name through to the last letter before she went in.

In the council room the committee for the twelfth commemoration of the flood had got as far as Larrère, who had asked to speak. Marie-Jo Labat turned a white kitchen timer to three and stood it in front of him. Any member could speak for three minutes on any item. Lesca had made that rule in her first month as mayor. It was for Larrère, who had been mayor before her.

Larrère used his three minutes to explain floodgates to Lesca across the table.

‘A gate is a door in a bank, Madame le maire. It is not a tap. You don’t turn it on because the sink’s full.’

The timer rang while he was in the middle of the word hydraulic. He finished the word and laid a hand flat on the table.

Lesca answered him, as she did at that table every year.

‘A door in a bank is opened from the platform, Guy, and I was the one on the platform. The bank had ten minutes left. Behind it were forty houses, and the care home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the fields there was one man, at his duck hut, and no one came to tell him. I opened it. Behind the bank, nobody even woke up.’

It took her half a minute. Nobody at that table had ever asked her a question about it. Maïté Castagnet, Jean’s widow, held her handbag in front of her with both hands and looked at the radiator.

The last item was the stone. Marie-Jo stood the timer in front of Larrère again.

‘I propose that the stone be given a verb,’ he said. ‘It has a man’s name and a woman’s name and nothing between them. A stranger reads it as a married couple. I propose Saved.’

‘Saved who?’ Marie-Jo said. She hadn’t spoken to Lesca directly since 2018, so she said it to the wall above Lesca’s head. ‘Castagnet, Saved, Lesca. It reads as if he was saved, by her.’

‘Opened.’

‘Opened what? Castagnet, Opened. It reads as if he was opened.’

‘I have a verb,’ Maïté said.

Every head turned. At that table she had never said anything except that she abstained. She opened her mouth to say the verb.

The timer rang.

‘That’s the member’s time,’ Marie-Jo said, to the wall. ‘It goes to the vote.’

Larrère’s verb lost the vote, as it had every time before. Lesca voted against it. Her left hand went up still closed. She had to open it to be counted.

Maïté waited until the others had their coats on.

‘Did you know he was out?’ she said.

‘No one came to tell him,’ Lesca said.

‘Yes. You said— At the table, you said that.’ Maïté did up the top button of her coat. ‘There’s a tile gone over the back bedroom. Right over the wardrobe.’

‘I’ll come at 2, while there’s light.’

* * *

The Castagnet house stood on the edge of the high ground above the Port. Lesca had looked after its roof since the January after the flood, though no council vote had ever asked her to.

That afternoon Maïté stood in her black coat and held the bottom of the ladder while Lesca set the tile back in its row and cleared the leaves out of the gutter along the roof. She was nearly down again when Maïté spoke.

‘It was raining by now, that year. It started in the night, and I had his rubber boots drying by the stove every evening after that.’

‘On the 24th. It’s in the file.’

Lesca stopped on the ladder. It was white with frost under her hands. Peyrebarthe had been dry for two weeks. But that morning she had been in a bed with rain falling on the roof. And on Tuesday, over there, the ink had run on the card she signed.

Tuesday had been the 24th.

She came down the last steps, laid the ladder along the wall, drove to the town hall and let herself in. She took the flood file for 2014 down from the cupboard and opened it on her desk at the pages where the height of the river was written down.

She had been the keyholder that winter. Every day she had gone down to the old gate, where her father had painted a red line on the pillar at the height of the doorsteps in the Port. She had read the river against that line and written down what she saw, one line a day. Now she read those lines with a finger under each.

24 November 2014. Under the line. Rain since the night.

25 November 2014. Under the line. Rain.

26 November 2014. Under the line. Rain.

27 November 2014. Under the line. Rain.

28 November 2014. Under the line. Rain.

Today was the 28th.

Ever since she stood at the stone that morning, she had taken the other village for the lucky one. Its river had stayed behind its bank in 2014, so its Castagnet had lived. But the rain over there had started in the night before the 24th, exactly as it had in her file. Four lines later it was still falling.

That village hadn’t had its flood yet.

She turned the page and went on down the dates. On 8 December 2014 the river had reached the line. Three lines later she came to the last one in her own handwriting.

11 December 2014, 22:00. Over the line. Rising.

Under that the file was typed, by the gendarmes.

First light. CASTAGNET Jean recovered from the cellar of his duck hut. Trapdoor locked on the inside. Watch stopped at 03:11.

This year the 11th fell on a Friday, thirteen days away. That night the river over there would be at the top of the bank, with Castagnet out at his hut. Larrère would ring Maylis, as he had rung Lesca, and tell her to keep the gate shut. Maylis had the key, but only Lesca had the date.

She couldn’t get Jean Castagnet out of his cellar. But over there he was still above ground, and she wanted to keep him there.

She took a sheet of the town hall’s paper from the drawer. When something had to be decided, the only form she had for it was a motion. So she wrote the heading for one. That morning she had stayed sitting, and she had woken up over there with a mouth. A mouth could tell that village what was coming, during office hours.

She wrote the motion under the heading.

That the mayor remain seated.

It didn’t say for how long. That morning the thing had come for her after twenty minutes. She wanted a day.

* * *

When it got dark Lesca crossed the yard to her mother’s kitchen and asked her question before she sat down.

‘The thing that sits on a sleeper. What did your mother give it to count, and how long did that keep it off her?’

Arlette was at the stove. ‘My mother had words for everything. She had some for the stove, and a whole set for the priest that she only used after he’d gone.’ She turned the potatoes. ‘For Inguma she had the grass on the village field. She gave it that, blade by blade, and it was counting till daylight. Madame Etcheto, down in the Port, gives it the stars. Coralie tells me the old woman rings for the night staff at 3. Stars, in winter, under cloud. It needs more than that. A great many things, and small ones.’

‘I want a day. A whole one, and a Monday. On a Sunday the town hall is shut, and there’d be nobody to tell.’

Arlette put the pan down.

‘Nobody gives it that much just to sleep, Maylis.’

Her mother had never called her anything else.

* * *

When Lesca came in, Coralie had the soup on and two places laid at the kitchen table.

‘Madame le maire will sit down,’ she said, ‘and eat it while it’s hot.’

‘I’ll have it here. I’ve got the minutes of this morning’s meeting to read.’

‘It’s soup. It doesn’t need a vote.’

Lesca ate it standing at the stove, with the minutes open beside the pan.

At 10 they went up. At the top of the stairs there were two bedroom doors, with the landing between them.

‘Good night, Madame le maire,’ Coralie said, and shut hers.

Lesca left her own door open and got into the middle of the bed. From there she could see the light under Coralie’s door. That morning she had held Coralie’s fingers, in Maylis’s hand. Before that she hadn’t held them since a February eleven years earlier.

When the light went out she got up, went down to the kitchen and unfolded the sheet of paper on the table.

That the mayor remain seated.

A motion is supposed to say what it is for. This one kept that back. She had sent Larrère’s motions back down the table for less.

She changed the full stop to a comma and went on in the same ink to the end of the line.

That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one’s bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.

4

It was breakfast that same Saturday, in Maylis’s kitchen, with rain on the window. Coralie stood in her work uniform with the coffee pot in her hand. She gave Maylis back the whole sentence from 6 that morning, in the voice she used for reading a dose off a chart.

‘That’s what I heard,’ she said. ‘I said it over to myself on the stairs, so I wouldn’t lose it. Was that you?’

Maylis opened her mouth and had nothing to put in it.

Coralie put the pot down.

‘Then I’ll tell you what it is at work,’ she said. ‘You haven’t slept a night through since Monday, and you’re on your back for the rib. That’s how it starts. The head wakes up before the body does. The eyes are open, nothing will move, and there’s a weight on the chest. Madame Etcheto calls it Inguma. The doctor calls it sleep paralysis, and half the night staff have had it.’ She laid her hand on Maylis’s. ‘And people who don’t sleep say things. We had a man in room six who didn’t sleep for a week after his wife died. On the last morning he told me to bring the horses around, in a voice I’d never heard out of him. He’d never owned a horse. By lunch he was asking who had been shouting in his room.’

Maylis wanted that to be all it was: a rib, five bad nights and a word the doctor had for it. Then the stone was a dream, and the date on it was only a date. For as long as Coralie’s hand was on hers, she believed it.

‘He asked for horses, though,’ Coralie said. ‘You asked for me.’

She took her hand away, pulled the shopping pad across the table and wrote the sentence out on it, pressing hard. Under it she drew two boxes. She wrote You beside one and Somebody else beside the other. Then she turned the pad around and put the pencil on top of it. ‘Tick one.’

Maylis picked up the pencil in her left hand. Both answers were true. There was no third box.

She ticked both.

‘It’s what I’ve got,’ she said.

‘Then I’ll tell you what I’ve got. You held my hand. You put yours over it and held on, and I can’t remember the last time you did that. And the whole time, your wrist was trying to get out from under my fingers. I felt both. I’ve held hands for a living since I was twenty, so I know when a hand is holding on, and I know when it wants to be let go.’ Coralie put a finger on each box. ‘So which one was you? The holding, or the getting away?’

The getting away was hers. She could hear how that would sound, said across the table to Coralie. The holding on belonged to somebody else. Maylis wished it had been hers.

‘I was slow waking up,’ she said.

‘You were wide awake, with a pulse of 130.’ Coralie took her finger off You and left the other on Somebody else. ‘I’ve waited a long time to be asked for one thing in this house. This morning somebody asks me, out loud, holding my hand. And it’s about a landing I’ve never slept across, with the wrong number of years in it, and you can’t tell me who did the asking.’

‘No.’

Coralie waited. She had the early shift to get to, but she took her time over her coat.

Then she tore the page off the pad, folded it and put it in her coat pocket. On her way to the door she reached behind the fruit bowl and took out an envelope that had been standing there since March. She put it in her bag.

* * *

That night Maylis kept still under Inguma again. She wanted to see who had held Coralie’s hand. Then she was in the chair in the steel room. She stood up.

The warmth along her back stayed where it was. She took a step towards the panel and turned around.

Lesca was sitting in the chair, in Maylis’s T-shirt. She had Maylis’s hands too, on the steel arms of the chair, the left one open now and the right one closed in a fist. She sat much straighter than Maylis did. There was nothing wrong with her ribs.

‘Good,’ Lesca said. ‘You can get up. That saves a week. I have a number of things to tell you, and I was starting to think I’d have to say them through your mouth, which is no way to do business. I found that out this morning. Sit down.’

‘There’s only one chair.’

‘Then I’ll stand.’ Lesca stood up. ‘A meeting needs somebody in the chair. Since neither of us is in it, I’ll chair from here. The first item is who I am. Maylis Lesca, mayor of Peyrebarthe.’

‘We’ve got a mayor. He sits in his car.’

‘Not in my village. In mine he sits at my table, and I time him. In June 2014 I sat in this chair and the right lamp came on.’

‘The left,’ Maylis said. Both were lit now, on the panel behind her. ‘They asked whether I also saw the other one.’

‘You’re looking at her.’

‘I had a stone,’ Maylis said. ‘With my name on it. And a square full of people.’

‘Both mine. I got a rake, and a postman. The second item is Monday. This morning I had twenty minutes in your bed before Inguma fetched me home. On Monday I’m having the day. All those in favour.’

‘There are two of us,’ Maylis said.

‘Then it’s carried, or it’s a tie, and in a tie the chair decides.’

‘There’s nobody in the chair.’

‘Next item,’ Lesca said. ‘Which do you want first? What I’ve come about, or what happened at 6 this morning?’

Maylis opened her mouth to say Either.

‘Don’t.’ Lesca’s fist closed tighter. ‘Not in here. I gave that word up when I was thirty-one, and I won’t have my own face say it to me.’

Maylis had written Either on the shopping pad on Tuesday, when she wanted the duck.

‘This morning at 6,’ she said. ‘I want that first. You held Coralie’s hand.’

‘With your hand,’ Lesca said. ‘She put two fingers on your wrist, and I took them.’

‘With my mouth,’ Maylis said. ‘Eleven years. I don’t know anything about eleven years.’

‘No. That’s an item for another night. Listen to me now, because this is what I came over for. The stone you saw stands in front of my town hall. It isn’t a grave. It’s for a night.’

Maylis had been counting the days to the 12th since Tuesday. For one breath she didn’t have to.

Then Lesca told her about the night, in order. Jean Castagnet’s name was cut above theirs. He had drowned in the cellar of his duck hut on the night of 11 December 2014, on the eighteenth day of rain. The 12th was the morning they got him out. Afterwards his eel nets were found under the floor of the hut, green and folded wet.

‘It’s rained here since Tuesday,’ Maylis said.

‘Since the 24th. I know it has. Your river is where my file says it was, day for day. On Friday the 11th, at night, your river will be ninety centimetres over your father’s red line, and water will be coming through the bank beside the last house in the Port.’

‘We had a dry winter here in 2014,’ Maylis said. ‘I was cutting roadsides in December. Why would it come now?’

‘Your mayor is Larrère, and he sits on the association’s money,’ Lesca said. ‘There’s a handle that sticks at nineteen turns, and three letters about it in the minutes. It comes because nobody has looked after your bank for twelve years.’

‘I have,’ Maylis said. ‘I patched it beside that last house in April, one wheelbarrow at a time. And I wrote those three letters.’

Lesca’s fist opened and closed.

‘Then why does it come?’ Maylis said.

‘I don’t know why it comes.’

‘Then stop coming,’ Maylis said. ‘You’ve got a date and no reason for it, and this morning you had my mouth. Stay in your own bed.’

‘I can’t. Inguma has sat on me every night for twelve years and put me in this chair, and I’ve never kept it off. I stopped trying in the first month.’ Lesca looked at the arm Maylis held across her side. ‘You pushed it off, the first nights. I felt you do it. That rib will tell you how many more nights you can push.’

Maylis took a breath to answer and the rib caught on it.

‘Last item, then,’ Lesca said, ‘and it’s the one I want you to remember. That floodgate is opened on Friday afternoon. In daylight, while the tide is going out, by order, with the fields cleared and nobody on them. And nobody stands on a platform in the dark and chooses.’

‘Whose order?’

‘Larrère’s. He’s the president of the association that owns that gate, and nobody else can give it. On Monday I’m coming over to ask him, in office hours.’

‘An order is a signature,’ Maylis said. ‘It took that town hall three letters to take note of a handle. This order lets a river onto every man’s corn in broad daylight. That costs more than I can pay.’

‘Then I’ll pay it. I sign things.’

‘There’s no word on your stone?’ Maylis said. ‘Between Jeannot and us?’

‘The council debated it three times.’

‘What would the word be?’

Lesca didn’t answer.

The door of the box opened. It had rubber around its edge and no handle. Inguma came in with the sound of a sack being dragged, crossed the steel floor between them and turned its back on each of them as it passed. Then it climbed into the empty chair.

Maylis was lying on her back in her own bed, with rain on the roof.

She believed the date. She wanted that gate opened on Friday afternoon as much as Lesca did. What scared her was Monday, when her own mouth would say so to Larrère, who paid her.

* * *

On Sunday morning Maylis found she couldn’t look at Jeannot.

She went to the café at 10 for bread, which the café sold from a crate on the counter. Jeannot was on his stool in his jacket, with his glass in front of him. She crossed the room looking at the crate, stopped beside him and put her money down.

‘Little Lesca,’ he said.

‘Jeannot.’

Out of the corner of her eye she could see his hand on the counter. She tried to lift her eyes up his sleeve to his face, but they wouldn’t go past his wrist.

‘On Tuesday you signed for my letter,’ he said. ‘And now you won’t look at a man. What have I done?’

‘Nothing.’

‘Then look at me when you say it.’

She looked at his boots.

‘One,’ Jeannot said. ‘Those are my boots.’

Lesca had told her how he drowned. None of that had happened in this café, but her neck didn’t know that. She wanted to tell him to stay off the fields on the 11th. The only reason she could give him was a dream.

She had passed the flowers on her way in. Josiane, the owner’s wife, kept the pots left over from All Saints’ Day outside the door, at a reduced price. Maylis took her bread, went out and picked up a pot of bronze chrysanthemums by its edge. They had a week left in them. She had gone back in and paid for them before she knew what she was doing.

‘Who’s dead?’ said old Bordenave, from the card table.

She had no answer. Jeannot turned on his stool to look.

‘Two,’ Jeannot said.

She went out with the bread under her arm and the pot in her left hand. She crossed the square in front of the café window and took the road along the edge of the high ground. That road led to four houses and no graves. The café watched her go. Jeannot lived in the fourth house.

Marie-Jo was coming down the church steps with a bucket of dead flowers. There was no Mass in Peyrebarthe that Sunday. On Sundays like that she did the vases. She stopped on the bottom step.

‘Those are chrysanthemums.’

‘Yes.’

‘You’re four weeks late for All Saints, or you’re early for somebody,’ Marie-Jo said. ‘Where are you going with them?’

‘To Maïté Castagnet.’

‘With him sitting in the café? Somebody should go in and tell him he’s dead.’

Marie-Jo stayed on the bottom step with her bucket. Maylis felt Marie-Jo watching the pot all the way to the fourth house.

* * *

Maïté opened her door in a blue dressing gown, with a potato in one hand and a knife in the other. She looked at the pot before she looked at Maylis.

‘Has something happened to him?’

‘No,’ Maylis said. ‘He’s at the café.’

‘Then who are those for?’

‘For you.’

Maïté looked up from the flowers at Maylis’s face. Maylis could feel the look on her own face, the one people give a woman beside a grave. She couldn’t get it off. Maïté took a step back from it.

‘You’d better come in,’ Maïté said. ‘Before the street sees.’

She took the pot and walked once around the kitchen with it. In the end she put it in the sink, on top of the potato peelings.

From where Maylis stood she could see the rack beside the sink. Two bowls were drying on it, with two spoons. On the cupboard, next to the bread, was a cardboard folder with one darker patch where rain had got at it. On wet days Larrère held that folder over his head between his car and the town hall door.

Maïté moved the bread on top of it.

‘Jeannot comes up from the fields at first light, and he’s at that counter by 8,’ she said, with her back turned. ‘On a Sunday I don’t see him until his lunch is on the table. It’s been like that since I got married.’ She turned around. ‘And now the village is on my doorstep with a pot of flowers. What does it want?’

That was when Maylis found out what she had come to say. The night before, in the chair, before she stood up, she had been given one of Lesca’s afternoons. In it she was on a ladder at the back of this house with a roof tile in her hand. Maïté was holding the bottom of the ladder, dressed in black.

‘Your back gutter. Over the bedroom. Is water coming in?’

Maïté went still.

‘Since October. It runs down the inside wall and onto the wardrobe, so there’s a basin up there. He says he’ll see to it when the duck season’s over.’ Her eyes had narrowed. ‘Who told you about my gutter? No. It’s Josiane. She’s never been past my gate, and she— It’s Josiane.’

‘Nobody. I could fix it. There’s a ladder at the shed.’ It was half an hour’s work.

‘With that rib? On a Sunday?’ Maïté laughed once. ‘The village’s girl up my wall, and those in my sink, and him at the— No. I’d never hear the end of it.’

‘I’m sorry,’ Maylis said.

‘What for?’

She had no reason she could give for it. Past Maïté, at the end of the passage, the bedroom door was half open. On top of the wardrobe a metal basin stood under a brown stain on the ceiling. Beside it was a light brown suitcase with both its straps done up, the only thing up there with no dust on it.

Maïté saw where Maylis was looking. She went down the passage, pulled the door shut and came back.

‘That’s winter things,’ she said. ‘In the— It’s blankets.’

At the front door Maylis bent down and kissed her on the cheek. Maïté kept her face still for it. Then she stood in her doorway with her hands in the pockets of the dressing gown.

‘You looked at me just now as if I’d buried somebody,’ she said. ‘People looked at me like that at my mother’s funeral. If you see me in the square, don’t— Not there.’

On Sunday night Maylis could have pushed, but she kept still. Keeping Lesca out meant having a better plan, and Maylis didn’t have one. Somebody had to tell the village. She would rather it was Lesca, who signed things. The chair was warm. She didn’t turn around to look, because that meant another meeting.

At 6 on Monday morning she woke up. Her legs swung out of the bed and carried her down to the kitchen.

Maylis let her mouth speak without her.

‘I want Larrère in my office at 9.’

Coralie had just come down in her uniform. The voice was Lesca’s, lower than Maylis’s own.

‘In your what?’

‘At the town hall, then, if he prefers.’

‘You’ve got a shed, Maylis. It has a lawnmower in it.’ Coralie had stopped with the coffee pot in her hand.

The shopping pad was on the table. Under Tonight it said soup, then omelette, with an empty box drawn beside each. Lesca picked up the pencil with the left hand, ticked soup and wrote 7 after it. Two seconds.

Coralie put the pot down and turned the pad around.

‘One,’ she said. ‘With a time on it.’ When she looked up she was close to laughing. ‘So I’ve got soup. What else do you want? Go on. Give me the rest.’

Lesca had the rest ready. Maylis felt the chest fill for it, as deep as the cracked rib would let it. Maylis got to the mouth first.

‘Either,’ she said.

Coralie let her breath out.

‘For a minute there I had somebody,’ she said. She took her keys off the hook. ‘Don’t rub the tick out. I want to find it there at 7.’

The front door had hardly shut behind her when the kitchen went cold. Something heavy came up against the kitchen door from the outside and stopped there.

Lesca turned the head towards the door and spoke to it out loud.

‘Inguma, enauk hire beldur. In my village there’s a lake where the village field was. You’ll count the stones the gravel company took out of it. You’re not to come till you’ve finished.’

The weight moved off the door and the cold went with it.

‘That’s a day,’ Lesca said to the right hand, which was still Maylis’s.

* * *

At 8 Lesca walked her down through the Port in the rain, to the last house. Sarthou, the gendarme, lived in it.

The patch Maylis repaired every spring was in the bank above his roof. It had turned dark across the width of a door. It was supposed to last until spring. The rain had washed some of the clay down over the road, and Sarthou was out there with a shovel.

‘That is the village’s clay,’ he said. ‘I have removed it from my gate twice since 7.’

By Maylis’s count he had half a wheelbarrow there. Lesca walked her up onto the bank, to the platform of the floodgate.

‘On Friday the 11th, if nobody orders this gate up in the afternoon, somebody has to turn it at night,’ Lesca said out loud, to the right hand. ‘I want to know if these arms can do that alone.’

Lesca unlocked the box with the key from the belt, fitted the handle and began to turn it with the left hand. The chain came up wet. Lesca counted the clicks of the catch, the steel tooth that stopped the chain running back. At eighteen, the bottom of the gate was ten centimetres clear of the stone it rested on.

At the nineteenth the handle stopped.

‘Here it needs a shoulder and both arms, and I have one,’ Lesca said. ‘Give me the other.’

Maylis kept her right arm where it was, across her side.

The left shoulder went under the handle and the legs pushed.

The handle gave all at once. The catch slipped, the gate dropped on its chain and the handle came spinning back. It was Maylis’s gate, and it was falling. Her right hand went out before she had decided anything. It closed on the handle, which ran on through her fist until Lesca got the left hand to it and stopped it.

Maylis opened her hand in the rain. The skin was gone in one straight line, from the base of the forefinger to the bottom of the palm.

Lesca kept the eyes on it longer than a cut deserved.

‘I have that,’ Lesca said. ‘The same hand and the same line. I got mine the night I raised this gate.’ The left hand let the gate down one click at a time and locked the handle in its box. ‘So one arm won’t do it. On the Friday I’ll need both of yours, and you’ll have to give me the right one.’

* * *

At 9 Maylis was standing beside the village van, in front of the town hall, where Lesca had stopped her. Larrère pulled up next to the van and lowered his window a few centimetres.

‘The lights,’ he said through the gap. ‘Your father switched them on at 5. At 5 this year, or at 6?’

Maylis had answered that gap with the same word for eighteen years. He was already closing the window on it. She felt the mouth get ready for the word.

Lesca stopped it. The mouth tried again.

‘Ei—’ it said. Lesca cut it off. ‘You have three minutes.’

The window stopped. Maylis still had her right hand, wrapped in a handkerchief. She held it up to the gap with the palm open, to say sorry for the mouth.

‘I have what?’

‘Three minutes on any item, Guy. You’ve spent some of them on my father. I’ll spend the rest on what you should be asking me. On Tuesday the 8th that river is at the red line. The prefecture at Pau sends you a yellow warning. You’ll stop this car at the café door and tell me that the gate stays shut, and that people in the Port know they live by a river. Three nights later the river comes through the bank at the last house. So on that Friday afternoon you’ll order the gate raised, in daylight, while the tide is going out, with the fields cleared.’

The window went down. It went all the way down, into the door. Maylis hadn’t known it could.

He let the rain fall on his sleeve.

‘What did she tell you?’

‘Who?’

‘Madame Castagnet,’ Larrère said. ‘Twenty minutes in her kitchen yesterday, with flowers, they tell me. And this morning the village’s girl knows what I’m going to say on a Tuesday.’ His voice was steady again. ‘Guy, she calls me, in my own square. You’ll stay away from that house. And this morning goes into your file, with the time on it.’

The window went up, all the way.

* * *

At noon Lesca walked her into the café.

Jeannot was on his stool at the counter with a glass in front of him. He had mud drying on his rubber boots. He was scratching his ear.

The legs stopped inside the door. The eyes had gone to the back of his neck, and Lesca left them there. Maylis had stood beside that neck at this counter for eighteen years without once looking at it. It was red from the weather and it needed a barber. She felt the breath stop in the chest and start again.

‘Still in the box, were you?’ the dealer said. He didn’t look up from his cards. It was what the café had said to anybody who came in late, ever since the June of the steel room. ‘The bread went at 11.’

‘For three hours,’ Lesca said. ‘On 12 June 2014, from 2 until 5. I’ve been on time ever since. I came at noon because you’re all here at noon.’

The cards went down.

Jeannot turned around on his stool, glass in hand.

The eyes wouldn’t leave him. Maylis could feel her own face smiling at him.

‘What?’ Jeannot said. ‘Have I got something on me?’

The mouth opened and nothing came. When Lesca did find something to say to him, it was an old bill out of Maylis’s memory.

‘You owe the village a tank of diesel. Its tractor pulled your van out of the track, in January 2017.’

‘And left two wheel tracks in it. That’s my letter seventeen. The village was sent the bill.’

The face went on smiling while he argued.

‘She’s been like this since 9,’ old Bordenave said. He kept the café’s score pad. He turned it over to the clean side. ‘I saw Larrère’s window go down. Right down. What did you tell him? Say it slowly.’

‘What he’ll say to me on Tuesday the 8th, at that door. That the river’s at the line, that the gate stays shut, and that people in the Port know they live by a river.’

Bordenave wrote it down and put the date beside it.

‘The 8th’s a week off,’ the dealer said. ‘Give Bordenave one for this week.’

‘Friday the 4th, in the morning. The river is seventy under the line.’

‘She’s got a mayor in her,’ the dealer said. ‘Put that at the top.’

Bordenave printed Madame le Maire across the top of the page and underlined it. Then he turned the pad around for her to see.

‘It takes a small m,’ Lesca said. Bordenave went over the M and made it a small one.

Then Lesca turned the face to the room. Maylis heard the voice settle.

‘On the night of Friday the 11th that river comes through the bank at the Port. Behind the bank there are forty houses, and the care home. And out on the fields, in his duck hut, there is one man—’

‘One man,’ Josiane said from behind the counter. ‘She’s said it herself. One man, and she can’t take her eyes off him, and she knows by heart the month she towed him, and yesterday she’s up at his wife’s with a pot of flowers off my doorstep. A woman takes flowers to the wife because she’s after the husband or because she’s after the wife. It’s adultery whichever she’s after, and it’s in that house.’

‘Which do I put?’ Bordenave said.

‘The husband,’ Josiane said. ‘She lives with a nurse, and that’s to throw us off.’

‘One man,’ Jeannot said, and got down off his stool. ‘That’s me she’s got out there.’

‘Go down to your hut tonight if you have to,’ Lesca said. ‘On Friday the 11th you stay in your kitchen. I forbid you to go.’

‘Nobody at that town hall has ever answered me, and now it forbids me,’ Jeannot said. ‘I hadn’t given the 11th a thought until you handed it to me.’ He stopped beside her on his way to the door. He smelled of his stove and of tall wet grass. ‘I’m giving it one now.’

Lesca had no answer for that. Maylis felt the mouth hang open, and she took it.

‘Put it in forty-two,’ she said, in her own voice.

He looked at the face.

‘Forty-two’s started,’ Jeannot said. ‘You’re on its second page.’

He went out into the rain.

* * *

At 7 there was soup, because soup had been ticked.

Maylis sat in her own chair with her right hand beside the bowl, wrapped in a clean dish towel. That hand wouldn’t hold a shovel for a week. Lesca held the spoon in the left. Coralie served the soup and sat down across the table.

‘By 10 they knew at work that you’d given Larrère three minutes,’ Coralie said. ‘What did he do?’

‘He lowered his window, to the bottom. He has talked to this village through a gap since 2008. It took his first name to get the glass out of the way.’

Coralie put her head back and laughed. Then she looked across the table for Maylis to laugh with her.

Maylis got that laugh out of her three or four times a year. Lesca didn’t know she was meant to laugh too.

‘By 2 we had the flowers as well,’ Coralie said. She was still smiling. ‘Chrysanthemums, up to Maïté Castagnet’s door on a Sunday, and today you couldn’t take your eyes off her husband. Josiane says it has to be one of them.’ She put her spoon down. ‘So which Castagnet is it?’

‘Him,’ Lesca said. ‘I’ve had that man on my mind every day for twelve years.’

‘Twelve,’ Coralie said, across the two bowls. ‘And it’s his wife who gets the chrysanthemums. I’ve had sixteen and a shopping pad.’ She picked the spoon up. ‘Well, I asked. What else? Or do I have to hear that at work too?’

‘One thing, and it’s for the care home. On the night of Friday the 11th the bank breaks at the patch by the last house. That’s thirty metres from your back door. Get them out of their beds by 2. Dressed, in the corridor, with the minibus at the glass doors and its tank full. The ones who can walk go first, from the front, because that fills a minibus fastest. Then the walking frames. Then the ones who have to be carried.’

‘And who do I say told me?’ Coralie said. ‘I’m a care assistant, and you cut roadsides.’

‘Say nobody. Write it as a list and pin it up. People do what’s pinned up.’

Coralie wrote it on the pad, under the soup. Then she stacked the bowls and carried them to the sink. She spoke from there.

‘I don’t know who I’ve had at this table. The café says you’ve got a mayor in you. Whatever I asked, I got an answer. One answer was Jeannot Castagnet, and one was what to do with forty-two people.’ She turned on the tap. ‘I’d have this one again.’

Under the dish towel Maylis closed her fingers on the cut.

* * *

At 9:05 Coralie turned off the light over the sink and went to the stairs.

Maylis felt her legs lift her out of the chair. Lesca walked her along the passage. Coralie had stopped on the fourth stair with her hand on the rail. She was looking down.

The eyes went up the stairs to Coralie and stayed on her. Maylis knew what Lesca wanted. Maylis had kept still for the village’s sake, not for this.

Lesca put the left foot on the first stair.

Maylis grabbed the post at the bottom of the stairs. The dish towel fell off, which left her bare palm on the wood, cut and all. She locked her arm. The post would hold.

Lesca took the body up one stair. Maylis’s arm pulled it back down. Lesca pulled again with both legs and with the left hand on the rail. The pull went straight into the cracked rib. Maylis held on.

‘What is it?’ Coralie said. ‘What are you holding on to that for?’

Lesca pulled, and didn’t answer her.

Cold came down the stairs from the landing, where the bedroom door stood open. Coralie pulled her arms in against her body and looked up behind her. There was nothing there for her to see. Maylis saw it. Inguma was coming down one stair at a time, with the sound a full sack makes when it is lowered down a step. It kept its back to Coralie as it passed her, and its leaves brushed her ankle.

It came off the last stair and onto Maylis’s chest, where she stood. The pulling stopped. Lesca was gone. Inguma had taken her.

Maylis had her legs back, and her mouth, and her left hand on the rail. Her right hand was still locked around the post, though there was nobody left to hold on against.

Coralie hadn’t moved from the fourth stair.

‘Are you coming up?’

6

‘I’ll lock up,’ Maylis said.

She got her fingers off the post. She turned the key in the front door, then washed two bowls that were already clean. She had nothing to say on the fourth stair that would have been worth the wait. When she went up an hour later the light was off and Coralie had turned to the wall.

* * *

‘I’ve come about yesterday,’ Maylis said. ‘I want to take it back.’

It was 9 on Tuesday morning, and she was standing in the rain at the window of Larrère’s car.

‘Yesterday,’ Larrère said through the gap. ‘There’s the question of yesterday. It won’t be settled by taking it back. It’ll be settled in writing, and you’re not to worry about the wording, because I have it. Write.’

He passed a sheet of the town hall’s paper out to her, with his pen. She wrote on the roof of his car while he dictated. First came her name and her job. Then came I resign from the job and give up the key, with effect from.

‘Stop there. Leave room for a date, and sign under it.’

‘I haven’t resigned.’

He opened a cardboard folder with LESCA M. on the label and held the top sheet against the glass. The sheet was blank.

‘Four winters of nets at your platform, and you hold the key to its lock. I’ve written none of that down. If I do, it goes to the prosecutor at Bayonne with Kévin Labat’s name at the top. That’s prison, for eels.’

She signed with her left hand. Larrère laid the sheet in the folder, on top of the blank one. Eighteen years of work made a thin folder.

‘I keep it. The day you go against me, I put the date in. That gate moves when I say so. And you stay away from the Castagnet house, and from her. What did she tell you?’

‘I asked about her gutter.’

* * *

At 4 that afternoon Sarthou sat her down at the window table of the café and laid a form straight in front of him. He was Jeannot’s cousin. Jeannot himself stood at the counter in his rubber boots, with his back to them.

‘Complaint of Castagnet, Jean, against persons unknown,’ Sarthou said. ‘The complainant’s duck hut was drained on Monday the 23rd. The gate was raised by its keyholder, on an order. Was the order the mayor’s, or the president’s?’

Larrère was both.

‘Either,’ she said.

‘Either is not a person. I cannot summon Either to the station.’ He wrote, then put his cap on. ‘Then this file contains one name, and it is the name of the person who turned the handle.’

He took the form out to his car.

‘I didn’t want you,’ Jeannot said, to his glass. ‘I go to the gendarmes for the man who drained my hut, and I come away with little Lesca.’

She went over to the counter. She kept her voice low.

‘There are green nets under the floorboards in your hut, folded wet.’

He put the glass down.

‘Who’s been down through my trapdoor?’

‘Nobody. There’s a number to ring about nets, on a poster in the town hall entrance. Take the complaint back, and I won’t ring it.’

‘You’ve looked the other way for the Labat boy at that gate for four winters, and asked nothing for it,’ he said. He put coins down beside the glass. It was still half full. ‘That’s how Larrère asks for a thing, and you’ve come in here and done it to me. Friday. You’ll have your complaint back.’

He went out.

* * *

When the light began to go she stood on top of the bank and watched Jeannot’s van go out along the track to his hut. Then she drove up to the high ground. Larrère’s car was turning into the lane behind the Castagnet house with its headlights off. It stopped at the fourth back gate.

In ten days she would be on that platform at night, and all she had of the night was what Lesca chose to tell her. Over there it was written down, in a file in the mayor’s office. She wanted to read it for herself. She also wanted one day as the woman who signed things. Over there, Larrère got three minutes and a kitchen timer.

So she would do what Lesca had done to her, and stay sitting.

At 11, on her back in bed, she said the words quietly to the top of the wardrobe. She gave Inguma its work for the morning: to count every metre of roadside she had cut in this village since 2008, once she was across and before it came to take her home. She worked out that the count would take it one working day: eight hours from 6, when Lesca woke up.

Coralie would wake up beside a body with its eyes open and nobody in it. So Maylis wrote Back at 2 on the shopping pad and stood the pad against the clock radio.

That night, when the warmth beside her in the chair got up to go, she stayed sitting. In the other village Lesca woke up, and Maylis went with her.

* * *

On Wednesday there was frost on the window. Maylis woke up in the mayor’s bed, under a dry roof. She was the one driving.

She breathed in. The breath went all the way down and nothing caught. This body had all its ribs. The bed had one pillow.

The right hand was on the blanket in a fist, and a fist was how it stayed. That one was Lesca’s.

Maylis walked Lesca’s body across the square from the church. The square said good morning. Josiane, who was putting out the café’s chairs, said Madame le maire. Nobody asked whether she was still in the box.

She liked it. She slowed down to get more of it.

The stone stood in front of the town hall door, in the space where Larrère parked at home. The top line said JEAN CASTAGNET. She was the line under him, with no word in between.

At home her name wasn’t even on her raincoat. Here it was cut in granite. She liked seeing it there. She didn’t like what it was under.

Somebody had cleared every drain cover in the square. A mower came around the church. Kévin was driving it, in a yellow raincoat with COMMUNE DE PEYREBARTHE across the back. When he saw the mayor he stopped the mower, took off his cap and waited to hear what he had done wrong.

‘You haven’t done anything,’ Maylis said.

He kept the cap in his hands.

Lesca’s right hand waved him on towards the road. He put the cap back on and went.

* * *

At home, in Maylis’s bedroom, it was raining. Coralie hadn’t gone in to work.

She sat on the edge of the bed in her uniform. Maylis was lying on her back beside her, with her eyes open. Under Back at 2 on the pad, Coralie had ruled a chart, the kind she kept for a resident. Every half hour she put two fingers on the wrist and wrote down the pulse.

It had been 64 at 6. At 10 it was 58, and it hadn’t once gone up.

The pad said 2. She would wait until 2.

* * *

At 10 Maylis stood in the council room with the committee for the twelfth commemoration of the flood, for its minute of silence. A white kitchen timer was set to one.

Maïté stood on the other side of the table in a black coat. On Sunday this same woman had come to her own door in a blue dressing gown, with a potato in her hand. Here she was a widow. Larrère stood next to her and held his hat against his chest.

The minute was for Jean Castagnet. Maylis kept her head down and tried to think of him as dead. That was easy at a funeral. Here all she could find was Jeannot and his letter seventeen, the one that billed the village for two wheel tracks its own tractor had left when it pulled him out.

The laugh started low down. Her own rib would have stopped it. This body had nothing to stop it with.

Lesca’s right hand came up and shut over the mouth, so the laugh came out through the mayor’s nose.

‘You’re laughing,’ Maïté said. ‘In his— In his minute. What is there to laugh at, Madame le maire?’

Lesca’s hand came away and left Maylis the mouth. Everything she could have put in it was about Jeannot, who was alive.

‘I’ll lay the flowers on the 12th, because they’re his,’ Maïté said. ‘After I lay them, you— Don’t kiss me, up there. Not this year.’

She went out. Then the timer rang.

Larrère put his hat on and went out after her.

* * *

That left Maylis alone in the town hall until 2. She took the flood file for 2014 down from the cupboard, sat down at the mayor’s desk and turned to the last page. The gendarmes had typed the night in three lines.

02:45. Water through the bank at the repair beside the last house, across the width of a door. The keyholder gave the bank ten minutes.

02:55. Gate raised by the keyholder, by hand. No order had been given to raise it.

First light. CASTAGNET Jean recovered from the cellar of his duck hut. Trapdoor locked on the inside. Watch stopped at 03:11.

She read it the way she read a job sheet, for what the job would need. The repair was her patch. And Castagnet had locked himself in, so somebody would have to walk down that track and knock. Not one of the three lines said whether anybody had.

When she went to shut the folder, the left hand was slow. She had to tell it twice.

At home she had left a body on its back with nobody in it.

At 2 the secretary opened the town hall. She turned over the day’s letters on the mayor’s desk, and Lesca’s right hand signed each of them without waiting to be asked.

The church clock struck 2, the hour on Maylis’s pad. Maylis sat still and waited for the cold.

Outside, something dry was being dragged across the ground. She took hold of the edge of the desk with the left hand and shut the eyes.

The right hand went on signing.

‘That’s Kévin, Madame le maire,’ the secretary said. ‘With the leaves.’

He was crossing the square in front of the stone, pulling a plastic sheet by one corner. It was piled with plane leaves. Maylis would have taken the trailer.

Nothing came at 2:05. Nothing came at 2:30.

‘Give me ten minutes,’ Maylis told the secretary.

When the door had shut Maylis got down on the mayor’s floor, on her back, beside the filing cupboard, with her arms by her sides. She said the words to the ceiling. Then she told Inguma to leave the roadsides and come for her now.

It didn’t come. Next to her, Lesca’s right hand tapped its fingers on the floorboards.

Maylis had worked out the count at the speed of the village tractor. Inguma was doing the roadsides on foot. Nobody had taught her any words for calling it back.

The door opened. The secretary bent down and put the next letter on the floor beside Lesca’s hand, and the hand signed it.

‘Council is at 6:30,’ she said.

If Inguma never came, all of this was Maylis’s: the desk, the stone, a square that said good morning. That morning, standing in front of the stone, she might have said yes to it. But all of it came with Kévin’s cap and one pillow.

* * *

By 3 Coralie was an hour past what the pad said.

She wrote 47 under 48. At work she always rang the doctor for any pulse under 40, without asking anybody first. She brought his number up on her phone.

She didn’t ring it. A doctor would put Maylis in an ambulance to Bayonne, and this was the bed Maylis had to come back to.

* * *

Thirteen people stood behind their chairs in the council room and waited for the mayor to sit. Maylis had never kept anybody waiting in her life, so she took her time over it.

The white kitchen timer stood on the table by Lesca’s right hand.

Maylis pointed at it. ‘I want that.’

‘The timer?’ the secretary said. ‘You have it, Madame le maire. It’s in the inventory.’

‘An item from the chair,’ Maylis said. ‘The night of the flood. What order did the keyholder have?’

‘You were the keyholder, Madame le maire,’ the secretary said.

‘For the minutes,’ Maylis said.

‘On that item,’ Larrère said, and stood up.

Every head turned to the timer. Lesca’s right hand already had the dial between finger and thumb. Maylis pinned its wrist to the table with the left.

‘A president gives his order by phone, at the hour it’s needed,’ he said. ‘And no president will ever put a river on three hundred hectares for a flood that hasn’t come.’

At three minutes he stopped for the bell. No bell came. He looked at the timer, then at the one hand lying on top of the other. Then he went on, like a man who has found a door unlocked.

‘Eleven years I’ve been timed like an egg,’ he said. ‘I have sentences in that register with no ends on them. The secretary will find one under the item on the gate cameras. “A man can no longer—” The timer rang there. It ends: stand at his own gate without the prefecture watching. I’m told I wave at the camera.’

The council put its pens down to watch.

‘The Labat boy got a sentence out of those cameras too,’ Larrère said, ‘and his had an end on it. Six months in prison, for a few kilos of eels. The mayor sent the film to the gendarmes herself. His mother was the mayor’s deputy, and she handed her own sash back across this table in a plastic bag. When he came out, the mayor gave him a mower and the village’s coat.’

Marie-Jo kept her eyes on the wall. Maylis looked at the hand she was holding down.

* * *

At home, in Maylis’s bedroom, the clock radio said 18:40.

The last line on the chart said 36. Coralie sat on the edge of the bed with her phone in her hand. The doctor’s number was on the screen, with her thumb over it.

She had been in room twelve when Madame Etcheto told them what happens to the one Inguma can’t take home. She had made a joke of it then.

* * *

‘And this morning she laughs in the minute, in front of his widow,’ Larrère said. ‘I walked Madame Castagnet to her gate myself. Since nobody’s ringing that bell, I’ll say what Josiane says at her counter. Twelve winters the mayor has been up at that house with a ladder, and no vote sent her. A woman keeps going to a house because she’s after the husband or because she’s after the wife. In that house the husband is on the stone.’

The deputy mayor looked at the water jug.

Maylis had crossed from one village to the other, and Josiane had still got there first.

‘I go in at the front, Guy,’ Maylis said. ‘In daylight, and she holds the ladder. I’ve never had to leave my car in the back lane with its lights off.’

He stopped with his mouth open on the next word.

She hadn’t known whether he had a back lane here. He had.

‘Whose car, Madame le maire?’ The secretary had her pen up. ‘For the minutes.’

‘Monsieur Larrère has the floor,’ Maylis said.

He had the floor, and he didn’t want it any more. He started on the corn and lost his place in it. He looked at the timer for help. By then he had been on his feet for twenty minutes.

‘My order that night was to keep the gate shut. If that gate had stayed shut, Jean Castagnet would have stayed dry, and he would have walked up that track in the morning. Madame Castagnet still leaves her kitchen light on, every night of the season. So I move that on the 12th the prefect speak alone. The mayor is the only one who was on the platform, and I have never asked her. When you stood on that platform, did you—’

Lesca’s wrist went out from under the left hand. The right hand took the dial, turned it all the way around and back to nothing, and the bell rang.

‘That’s the member’s time,’ Marie-Jo said, to the wall above Maylis’s head. ‘It goes to the vote.’

Maylis didn’t know how his question ended. Lesca’s hand knew. It had the timer face down on the table and was leaning on it.

‘Those in favour,’ Maylis said.

Seven hands went up. Larrère’s was the highest.

‘Against.’

Six went up. Then Maylis put up the left hand, and Lesca’s right hand let go of the timer and went up beside it.

‘The chair votes once, Madame le maire,’ the secretary said. ‘Seven and seven. In a tie the chair’s vote decides. The motion fails. The mayor speaks after the prefect.’

Larrère shut his book and spoke to the table.

‘She’s voted herself onto the stage. A metre from that stone, with a camera on her. I’ll have my answer there.’

The church clock began to strike 7.

Out in the corridor something dry was dragged along the floor. This time it wasn’t a plastic sheet.

It was five hours late.

Inguma came along under the table, past a row of shoes, and climbed onto her chest where she sat. Its leaves pressed against the register. She couldn’t lift a finger of either hand, and the council of Peyrebarthe watched its mayor not lift one.

7

Something wet touched Maylis’s left eye and she blinked. It was the first time she had blinked since 6 that morning.

She was back in her own bed, with rain falling on the roof. Coralie sat on the edge of the bed in her uniform, with a square of wet cotton in one hand and her phone at her ear. Maylis could hear it ringing at the doctor’s end.

Coralie saw the blink. She ended the call, laid the phone face down on the blanket and took the wrist.

‘Your pulse was 34 when I rang him,’ she said, when she had counted. ‘Now it’s 44.’

Maylis wanted to close her hand over those two fingers. The hand was cold and it stayed open.

Coralie had put her own pillow under Maylis’s knees. The pad was on the blanket. The pulse went down the chart from 64 to 34, beside two columns of ticks headed eyes and turned.

‘Your pad said 2,’ she said. ‘It’s 7. Thirteen hours, with your eyes open, and I haven’t gone in to work. Where have you been, Maylis?’

‘At work.’

Coralie stood up.

‘That’s where I was meant to be,’ she said. She pulled her pillow out from under Maylis’s knees. ‘I’m taking the spare room, because I can’t lie next to that and sleep. Ask me back across the landing and I’ll come. But you’ll have to ask.’

Coralie waited in the doorway with the pillow under her arm. When nothing came, she gave her the rest in the voice she used at work.

‘And tomorrow you’ll want to make up the day. No sacks. You don’t lift with that rib, and you don’t push against anything. A cracked rib mends if it’s left alone. Push on it and it goes through into the lung. I’ve seen one do it. The man was eighty-one. He pushed himself up the bed on the rail to reach his glasses. When I got to him his lips were blue. He said his mouth tasted of coins. He coughed, and it came out pink on the sheet. His breath stopped halfway down, and then at his collar. He was dead before the doctor had his coat off.’

She crossed the landing. She left both doors open, and Maylis heard the pillow go down on the single bed in the spare room.

* * *

That night Maylis set out to keep herself out of the chair.

She got into bed on her left side, at the edge, with her knees up, so that Inguma would have nowhere to sit. The rib let her have an hour of it and then put her on her back.

At 2:51 Inguma came down off the wardrobe and sat on her chest. Maylis pushed. Every breath went through the crack in the rib. Inguma pressed down just as hard. The first night it had got down after three minutes. This time it stayed. Maylis pushed until the church clock struck 4, when Inguma got down and left the way it had come. Afterwards she took a breath all the way down and ran her tongue around her mouth. Nothing.

On Thursday night she took a blanket down to the kitchen and got down on the floor between the table and the stove. At 2:51 Inguma came down the stairs, with the sound of a sack being lowered down a step, and it found her. She pushed until 4 again.

When it had gone she stayed on the floor and took a breath. The breath stopped halfway down. Her mouth tasted of coins. She pulled herself up by the leg of the table, coughed into the sink and looked. It was pink.

She ran the tap until the sink was white again. She was back in her own bed before Coralie’s alarm rang.

* * *

At 10 on Friday 4 December Maylis was crossing the square when Bordenave knocked on the café window. She went in. He had the pad open at the page headed Madame le maire.

‘Friday the 4th, in the morning,’ he read out. ‘Seventy under the line.’ He looked at her over his glasses. ‘I walked down at 9. It’s a metre, and the road’s dry.’

‘Put a cross by it,’ the dealer said. ‘That’s her first.’

Bordenave drew the cross. Jeannot turned around on his stool to watch him do it.

‘Your mayor’s thirty centimetres wrong,’ Jeannot said. ‘If she can be wrong about Friday the 4th, she can be wrong about Friday the 11th.’ He lifted his glass to Maylis. ‘That’s the best thing on that pad.’

Maylis had kept out of the chair for two nights. She meant to have a third before she asked Coralie back.

That night she got into her own bed, on her back. Her breath had stopped halfway down all day. Inguma came down at its minute. Maylis took what breath there was and held it. Then she let it go and kept still.

The arms of the chair were warm.

When Maylis got up out of the chair at 6, Lesca stayed sitting in it. It was Lesca who got her out of bed. By 6:20 Lesca had sent Inguma off to count the lake again.

* * *

By noon on Saturday it was raining.

That afternoon Lesca walked her down through the Port to the bank. The cellar door at the side of Bordenave’s house was open, and a row of jam jars stood in the rain beside it. He was at the bottom of the steps in his rubber boots, in water over his ankles.

‘This floor was dry yesterday,’ he said, and held another jar up to her. ‘The cross stays. Friday was a metre.’

Maylis took the jar in her right hand and stood it with the others.

On the platform Lesca read the pillar.

‘Fifty-five under,’ Lesca said out loud, to the right hand. ‘I read the same, that day.’

On Wednesday and Thursday nights Maylis had pushed Inguma off until 4 and never reached the chair. Thursday and Friday had been the only dry days since the 24th. Last night Maylis had kept still. Lesca had got her out of bed at 6, and by noon it was raining again.

It was only two days. Bordenave would have called that weather. Maylis didn’t, but she had nobody to say it to.

Lesca had taken the watch off Maylis’s right wrist and was holding it in the left hand, out of the rain.

‘From this handle to Castagnet’s trapdoor,’ Lesca said. ‘On foot, in this mud.’

Lesca waited for the watch to reach the full minute, then walked her down off the bank and onto the track.

The track ran straight out across the flood fields for a kilometre. Wheels had cut two lines along it and both were full of water, so Lesca kept to the grass between them. Every few steps the rib caught. Lesca didn’t slow down for it.

A little past halfway came the dip, where the track went down into a hollow that was under water from one side to the other. The legs went through it with the water over the ankles of the boots.

The track ended in a circle of bare mud, where Jeannot turned his van around every night. Beyond the mud was the duck hut. From outside it was only a low bump in the tall grass, with a chimney pipe sticking up out of it and a wooden trapdoor on top. There was no van and no smoke.

The legs took her up onto the hut and stopped on the trapdoor. The wood sounded hollow under the boots.

‘Twenty-five minutes,’ Lesca said.

Twenty-five minutes was a trailer of leaves to the dump and back.

The legs didn’t step off. Maylis felt the eyes go down between the boots to the boards and stay there, while the rain ran off the hood onto them.

‘When that bank starts to go, it has ten minutes,’ Lesca said. ‘I had my hand on it.’

Maylis held out her right hand for the watch and Lesca gave it to her. Maylis looked back the way they had come. The bank was a dark line across the end of the fields, with the roofs of the Port at one end of it. The gate was a post on top of that line, no bigger than a nail.

Maylis stood on Jeannot’s trapdoor in the rain. It had been dry out here on Thursday, and on Friday. She couldn’t step off the trapdoor until Lesca did.

8

Lesca was walking Maylis back along the track when Jeannot’s van came down off the bank towards them. Lesca stepped into the wet grass to let it by. He drove past at walking pace. He looked at the mud on her boots and at the watch in her hand. Then he looked down his track to where it ended, and drove on.

* * *

That evening was bingo night in the village hall.

‘Thirty-four. Three, four.’

Maylis stood behind the bar, beside the big coffee pot, while Lesca did the serving with the left hand. From there she could see three rows of tables on the waxed floor, under strip lights. They had paper tablecloths, and at each place there was a card and a small dish of corn to mark it with. Marie-Jo was calling from the stage, each number whole and then in parts. Coralie had brought the residents up in the minibus and sat them at the table by the radiators.

Every head in the hall went down over a card. By the radiators the two Lassalle sisters shared one card and held hands under the tablecloth. The woman next to them marked hers, with her tongue between her teeth.

Maylis had her own teeth shut. Inguma would come for Lesca tonight. All Maylis had to do was keep the mouth shut until it came, so that Lesca couldn’t give it anything else to count.

Josiane came up to the bar between two numbers and put a coin down.

‘A coffee. Is that pot hot this year, or do I wait?’

Lesca pulled at the jaw and Maylis held it, so all Josiane got was a noise through the nose.

‘Is that a yes?’

‘Seventy-one. Seven, one.’

‘Here,’ Monsieur Daguerre said, from his wheelchair.

‘Not yet, Monsieur Daguerre,’ Coralie said. ‘You want seventeen for the line. That was seventy-one.’

The left hand was holding a cup under the tap of the coffee pot. Lesca was still pulling at the jaw and wasn’t watching the cup. The coffee came up to the top and went over. It ran along the bar and off the end, onto Josiane’s shoes.

‘That’s full,’ Josiane said, and stepped back. ‘Maylis. It’s full.’

Maylis reached across with her right hand and shut the tap.

‘Have you got toothache, Maylis?’ Marie-Jo said into the microphone. The whole hall looked up from its cards at the bar. ‘See the dentist on Monday. Fifty. Five, zero.’

‘I am reporting a line,’ Sarthou said, two tables down. He put his cap on to go up for his prize, a grass trimmer. It was better than the one in Maylis’s shed.

That night Maylis kept still under Inguma, and then she was in the chair. The arms of the chair were cold. Lesca wasn’t in it yet, but the night of the flood came to Maylis anyway, the one Lesca had gone over every night for twelve years.

A phone rang in the dark beside Lesca’s bed. The wall side of the bed was empty, because Coralie was working nights that week. Lesca picked up the phone without turning on the light. The screen showed a landline number with no name. It was Larrère. He said the river was ninety centimetres over the red line and rising. The floodgate was to stay shut. She was to go down and stand by it and do nothing until he rang again. Behind his voice a clock was ticking close to the phone, the kind that hangs in a kitchen.

When she drove past the care home, Coralie’s car was in the yard. At the end of the road water was coming through the bank. Before she fitted the handle to the gate, she turned the van around on top of the bank so that its headlights pointed down the track across the flood fields.

The headlights lit up the rain. At the far end of the track they lit the back of a white van. Low on its left-hand door a round reflector, screwed on at an angle, shone red.

Only one van in the village had a reflector like that. It was Jean Castagnet’s. That week he had written to the town hall that there was hardly any water at his duck hut. He had his water now. The ducks would have come down onto it, so he would be in the hut, in the cellar under the trapdoor, wearing the long rubber boots he hunted in. If she opened the gate, the river would cover that trapdoor.

The trapdoor was twenty-five minutes away, on foot, in the dark, with the dip under water. The bank had four.

She took out her phone. It had his number in it because of the gate. The screen said 02:51.

His number rang once and nobody answered. She didn’t wait for the second ring. She ended the call and put the phone in her pocket. Then she took the handle in both hands and turned it.

She turned it faster than it had ever been turned. At some point the catch slipped. The handle spun backwards through her right fist and tore a line of skin out of her palm. She caught it and kept turning. The gate came up. Under her feet the river went through the opening with a noise she had never heard it make, and out on the fields the dark ground began to shine.

She went back to the van and sat in it with her hand in her lap. At the end of the track the reflector went on shining in her headlights for four more minutes. Then the water rose up the door and covered it, and the red light went out.

She kept the torch on the pillar of the gate. Eighty over the line. Sixty. The sheet of water on the bank shrank to a thin line. By 4 no house in the Port had water in it. Behind the shutters nobody had even woken up.

The cellar came later, at first light. There was a boat. There was the trapdoor, with the torch shining down through it. The water was black and still and came up to the second step of the ladder. Under the water the torch found the sole of a rubber boot.

Only the priest knew she had looked down the track first. She had told him in the confession box, while a bucket in the aisle caught the drips.

The brown leaves and the rivets were just under her chin.

On her night Lesca had forty houses to open the gate for. She also had Coralie’s car in that yard.

Inguma got down.

On Tuesday Maylis had written out her own Friday night as a job. The handle was near the top, with the trapdoor under it. Lesca had done hers in that order.

Maylis’s phone was on the chest of drawers. Jeannot’s number was in it, because of the gate. It was Friday morning. Jeannot was down there in his hut.

She heard the trapdoor go up, then wings, hundreds of them, lifting off the water.

‘One. It’s 5 in the morning. Two. I had three hundred ducks sitting on water that I waited two weeks for. Your phone has just scared off every one of them, and they won’t come down again before tonight. Three is the village. It drains my water on a Monday. It sends a girl to my kitchen with a pot of flowers on a Sunday. And at 5 on a Friday morning it rings a man in a hole in a field to ask if he’s in. Four is your father. For thirty years he raised that gate four turns less than he was told, to leave a man his water, and he never rang me once. He’d have cut his hand off first. You’ve got his key and none of his sense. Five is the association, and for that I go back to 1962, when they dug their gate where my grandfather had his hut and gave the handle to a Larrère.’ He took a breath. ‘And six. Bugger off.’

At 5:10 she went down to the kitchen, turned the shopping pad over on the table and changed her own plan. She crossed out the line about the trapdoor and wrote it again at the top, above the key, with before dark after it.

11

Lesca’s mother stood in the doorway of the mayor’s office in her coat, with her bag on her arm.

‘I said I’d never set foot in here,’ she said.

It was 9 on Friday morning and Lesca was at her desk in the town hall. Her mother hadn’t been inside the building since the day the truck came for her ponies. Lesca had signed the gravel company’s contract at this desk. The company had taken her mother’s field with the rest, and the flood fields were a lake now. Arlette came three steps into the office and looked around it once, at the desk, at the green safe and at the window, where the back of the stone showed.

Behind her the secretary, who had come in early all that week for the ceremony, had the visitors’ book open and her pen ready.

‘Don’t put me in that,’ Arlette said.

‘I write down every visitor.’

‘Then write that I never voted for her to be in here. I’ve voted for Larrère against her three times. I don’t like the man. He drives through my yard as if it was the road. But I voted for him, and I’ll say so while I’m standing in here. Write that. Leave my name off.’ She waited until the pen was down.

Lesca had won all three times.

‘Sit down,’ Lesca said.

‘I’ve got my coat on.’ Arlette kept hold of her bag. ‘Coralie rang me again last night. She rings at 9, when I’ve got my shoes off, and the phone’s in the passage, on the tiles. She says you’re planning something for tonight. She doesn’t know what, and neither do I. But I know what my mother’s words were for. She said them so she could stay in her own bed till morning, and you’re using them to leave yours.’

‘I heard what you said about a night’s sleep.’

‘You wanted a whole day off Inguma, and you had one. I sat by that bed from noon on Saturday till breakfast on Sunday. Your hand was colder than on the Monday. What did you give it to count?’

‘The lake,’ Lesca said. ‘Every stone the gravel company has taken out of it. Each load is weighed, so the loads are in a book.’

‘My field,’ Arlette said. She had counted her ponies up the ramp of that truck.

She looked at Lesca’s hands on the desk. ‘Sleep on your side, Maylis. Tonight. With your knees up, so it has nowhere to sit.’

Over there Castagnet would be at the end of the track tonight. Lesca had left her own Castagnet there. She was going over to get the other one out.

‘I’m going across tonight,’ Lesca said. ‘And I won’t be brought back.’

‘I’m not asking where,’ Arlette said. ‘I want you at my table at 7 in the morning. Before the prefect gets you.’

She walked out past the secretary and down the steps. She had been in the building five minutes. Lesca watched her cross the square towards the church. Arlette went around the new stage with her face turned away from the names on the stone.

* * *

At 11 Lesca was standing on the stage in the square, on the cross of white tape that marked her place beside the stone. The young man who had stuck it there had come from the prefecture at Pau, with a clipboard and a roll of tape, to take her through tomorrow’s ceremony. It would be her twelfth. He read it to her off his sheet, from the anthem to the photograph.

‘A point of order,’ Lesca said. ‘You have the flowers as one item and the kiss as the next. They’re one item. Madame Castagnet lays the flowers and stands up, and the mayor kisses her on the left cheek, because your photographer stands on the left. That item has been carried eleven times. This year the widow has given notice against it, so you can take the kiss off your sheet.’

He looked at his sheet. He left the kiss on it.

‘And the medal isn’t an item. It comes on the coat. Your office rings every December to tell me which side.’

The last line on his sheet was the reception, in the big tent on the bank at the Port. The tent went up on the plots where four houses had stood.

Monsieur Darrigrand had come up from the Port with a broom over his shoulder and was standing at the foot of the stage. He used to live in one of the four. A doorstep was all that the State’s contractor had left of his house, and he kept it swept.

‘The tent goes on the plots, Monsieur Darrigrand, as it does every year,’ Lesca said. ‘The State’s new bank went through four houses. It was thirty-six houses and the care home against four.’

‘It’s four when you’re the one counting,’ he said. He leaned on the broom. ‘When it’s your house it’s one, and they knock it down. Put your tent up. All I ask is that nobody puts a glass down on my step.’

The young man looked down his sheet for a step. Darrigrand put the broom back over his shoulder.

‘You’re holding on to that stone, Madame le maire,’ he said. ‘I’ve watched you up there eleven Decembers. You never needed to before.’

Lesca took her hand off the stone. Darrigrand went down the road to the Port.

‘Which one is his?’ the young man said.

‘The one that’s been swept.’

He wanted the front row for the councillors and the emergency services, because it looked better on camera.

‘The front row belongs to the care home,’ Lesca said. ‘It does every year.’

Coralie had come up from the Port with the list. In this village she was the one who ran the care home.

‘The emergency services can stand,’ she said. ‘Mine can’t.’

She took the tape from the young man without asking.

‘And Madame le maire will sit down on the edge of that stage,’ she said. ‘She’s the colour of this tape.’

Lesca stayed standing. Coralie walked along the front of the stage and pressed strips onto the ground, one wheelchair’s width apart, while he followed her and counted them out loud.

‘Eight of them were in their beds that night,’ Coralie said. ‘Madame Etcheto. She says she heard the river go away, like a bath emptying. Monsieur Daguerre, the baker. He’ll call out “Here” when the prefect reads out the date. He does it every year.’

‘Can he be asked not to?’

‘You can ask him. He was asked in 2019, and he said “Here”.’ She tore off another strip. ‘The two Lassalle sisters. Madame Poey, who doesn’t know what year it is now but knows that one.’

She pressed down the eighth strip.

‘Eight,’ the young man said.

‘There were forty-two in their beds that night. The others have died since then, in those beds, of the things people die of.’

He stopped counting.

‘There were two of us working that night,’ Coralie said. ‘Nadine’s in the third row and I’m in the second.’ She gave him back his tape. ‘Madame Etcheto goes at this end, nearest the stone, because she asks for that place. Mark it on your plan.’

He marked it.

* * *

When Lesca got home that evening, Coralie’s car was already in the yard. The kitchen was dark, but there was a light on the landing.

She went up. Coralie was standing in the bedroom doorway with the speech in her hand, four typed sheets clipped together.

‘You left this on the kitchen table,’ she said, ‘and I’ve read it. I’d never read it before. I’ve only heard it, eleven times, from the second row.’

‘It’s the same every year. I change the date.’

‘I know. I could say it along with you.’ Coralie turned to the third sheet. ‘Jean Castagnet was at his duck hut that night, as he was on any night of the season. No one came to tell him.’ She read it in a flat voice. ‘Every December you say that into a microphone. It’s true, every word of it. And every December I stand there in my good coat and think about your phone.’

Lesca left her coat half off.

‘The morning after,’ Coralie said. ‘The 12th. I finished my shift at 7, and you came in at 9 in your wet clothes and got onto this bed with your boots on and your hand wrapped in a dish towel. Your phone was on the chest of drawers, with the battery nearly dead. I picked it up to put it on the charger, and the screen was open at the list of calls. Incoming, a landline number, no name. Then outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. 02:51.’

She looked up.

‘2:51,’ she said. ‘I’ve known that for twelve years.’

Lesca looked at the top of the wardrobe.

It was 6:45 in the evening. On top of the wardrobe there was a folded blanket, and dust. Nothing was sitting there.

‘You shout at it in the night,’ Coralie said. ‘Across the landing I look at my watch, and it’s always that time. For eleven years I told myself it was a coincidence. I’m trained. I know what a coincidence is.’

‘You never asked,’ Lesca said.

‘No.’

‘Why not?’

‘Because you’d have told me.’ Coralie sat down on the edge of the bed, on the side that used to be hers. ‘I was in the care home. I was working nights that week. The shifts were on the fridge door, and you’d read them. There were two of us working, and only a few in those beds who could have walked out. At 2:30 water was coming in under the back door and I was putting down towels. By 4 it had stopped coming, and at breakfast they told me what had been done. So for twelve years I’ve had two answers. Either you opened it for forty houses, and I live with the mayor. Or you opened it because I was in that building. Then his death is mine as well, and I’ve stood beside his widow for eleven Decembers. I’ve never wanted to hear which one it was. Don’t tell me now. If I’d asked, I’d have had the answer in the house with us, at every meal. I wanted you in the house, and I thought I could have you without it, if I kept a landing between us.’

‘It rang once.’

‘The phone doesn’t say how long it rang. It says not answered.’ She put the speech down on the blanket. ‘That’s all I’ve got. You rang him, so you thought he might be out there. I don’t know what you saw, and I don’t want you to measure it out for me now. I’m telling you I’ve known that much since the morning after. I’ve had it with me in the next room, and you’ve had the rest by yourself. You didn’t have to.’

Lesca’s right hand had closed into a fist. When she opened it the scar was there, where it had been since the night of the dish towel. She sat down on the bed, on the other side of the four sheets.

‘I thought one person knew,’ she said. ‘And he’s not allowed to speak.’

‘The priest.’

‘Yes.’

‘Then there are two of us. And nobody’s told me I can’t speak.’ Coralie didn’t touch her. ‘You’re going somewhere tonight. I’ve sat beside this bed three times with the phone in my hand, and by now I know what you look like when you’ve gone. Who is it for?’

‘For a man who hasn’t drowned yet. There are two of me after all. The other one still holds the key to that gate, in a Peyrebarthe where the river comes up tonight. I’m going to get him out.’

‘I thought it might be. It’s twelve years too late for anybody here.’ She looked at the third sheet. ‘Read that line on Saturday or take it out, Madame le maire. It’s your speech. You voted for it. But I’ll be in the second row, and I’ll know which you did.’

She went across the landing and left her door open.

Lesca stayed on the bed with the speech and tried to say the answer out loud.

‘I opened it for forty houses.’

It sounded like a line from the council’s minutes. She had said it too many times to hear whether it was true.

‘I opened it for Coralie.’

That sounded no more true, and no less.

She had the sum. Forty houses, forty-two beds, a car in the yard of the care home and a van at the end of a track. She tried to remember what she had been thinking when she made it, but all she could remember was turning the handle.

She read the two sentences on the third sheet again. Then she clipped the four sheets back together, as they had been typed.

At 10 she got into bed the way her mother had told her to, on her side with her knees up.

She was facing her open door. Across the landing Coralie’s was open too, four steps away, with the lamp on inside. Its light reached the foot of Lesca’s bed.

‘If I come in and you’re lying there with your eyes open,’ Coralie said, from across the landing, ‘I’m ringing the hospital in Bayonne.’

Lesca didn’t answer. She watched the light until the church clock struck 11. It was still on.

Her own roof was dry. Over there it would be raining on forty houses with their shutters closed.

‘It’s the tide,’ she said, too low to cross the landing. In this room it came out.

Then she straightened her legs and turned onto her back.

12

At 11 on Friday morning Maylis let herself into the town hall, out of the rain. The printer had put out one sheet, from the prefecture at Pau. Flood watch, river Adour, orange.

She put the sheet in the mayor’s tray and picked up the phone on the secretary’s desk.

One woman couldn’t raise the floodgate and also get Jeannot up out of his duck hut. She had tried the café first, but the nine who had signed on Tuesday had watched Jeannot’s hand go up. A vote was a vote. So she rang the town hall at Hontaube, the next village. She asked to borrow its outdoor workers for one night, at the Port. Hontaube had two. Peyrebarthe had her.

‘Who’s calling?’ the woman at Hontaube said.

‘The keyholder. At Peyrebarthe.’

‘Oh, you’re the one with the gate.’ She sounded pleased. ‘Is this a request, or a notification?’

‘A request,’ Maylis said.

‘Has your mayor made it?’

‘I’m making it.’

‘Then it isn’t one yet,’ the woman said. ‘A loan of staff goes from mayor to mayor. There’s a form, and I can post you one. It needs his signature and ours.’

‘When would you post it?’

‘Monday.’

‘It’s for tonight,’ Maylis said.

‘Then it should have gone last Monday.’

A hand went over the phone. Behind it Maylis heard a man’s voice, then a second, then a chair being pushed back.

‘They say they’d come,’ the woman said. ‘They’ve got their coats, but I’ve told them they can’t. The agreement between the two villages starts on 1 January, and before that our men aren’t insured on your side. If one of them hurts his back on your bank tonight, nobody pays him for it. From 1 January you won’t need a form. You’ll belong to us.’ She said it kindly. ‘I’ll post it today anyway, so you have it.’

She hung up.

* * *

Larrère spent ten minutes in the town hall. When he came out he had the orange warning in his coat pocket and his cardboard folder held over his head. Maylis was standing on the steps, between him and his car.

‘Pau’s sent an orange warning,’ she said.

‘Pau has sent an orange warning, and Pau doesn’t own that gate. The association owns it, and its president says it stays shut. You’ll take the van down at dusk and stay by the gate. All night. Keep your phone on the dashboard, charged, and touch nothing until I ring.’

‘Water’s coming through the bank at the patch. There are forty-two people in the care home, right under that bank, and most of them can’t walk.’

‘They have staff,’ Larrère said. ‘It’s orange. Red is when people are moved, and I haven’t had red. If you raise that gate tonight, you put the river over whatever’s standing on those fields.’

‘And if the bank breaks?’

‘It leaks. It has leaked before, with the river over the line, and it held. Your father slept on that platform with the gate locked shut, and he never rang me once.’

He brought the folder down and turned it, so that she could read her name on the label.

‘I have a letter in here in your handwriting. It’s your resignation, with a gap where the date goes. If that gate moves tonight before I ring, the letter gets tomorrow’s date. You know what’s under the letter.’

‘I saw your car on 1 December,’ Maylis said. ‘At dusk, in the lane behind the Castagnet house, with its lights off. Jeannot had gone down to his hut.’

He didn’t put the folder back over his head. The rain ran down his forehead.

‘I’d be up that lane every night of the year if she’d let me,’ Larrère said. ‘She has the kitchen door open before I’ve switched the engine off. Nobody else in this village has ever opened one before I knocked.’

He went down the steps past her and got into his car. The window came down its usual few centimetres.

‘Stay by the gate,’ he said through the gap. ‘You’ll hear from me.’

The window went up.

* * *

There was no sand in the shed. Behind the village hall, though, there was a bin of it, kept for icy roads. Kévin was standing at the bin when she got there. Nobody had asked him to come. He held the bags open while she shovelled with her left arm. He kept each one wide. Most people let a bag fall shut.

‘I was up the ladder on Tuesday when he held that sheet against his car window,’ he said. ‘That was my name, wasn’t it? Is it the eels?’

‘It’s for the eels. Four winters of them.’

‘What’s that, six months?’ He tied the neck of a bag. ‘I’d do them, Maylis. I want you to know I would. Don’t keep a gate shut for me.’

She went on shovelling. The sand filled seventeen bags, which he carried to her van.

At 4 Maylis took the bags down to the back door of the care home. The door opened onto the yard, at the end of the building nearest the bank.

The bank rose above the yard wall. Through the open door Maylis could see down the whole length of the building. The old wing came first. At its far end a ramp went up to a fire door, which stood open. Beyond that was the main corridor, where the night shift had lined up the wheelchairs. Each one had a folded blanket on the seat and a name on a strip of tape. The minibus was at the other end of the building, in the front yard.

Coralie had the list on a cord around her neck. Every name in the building was on it, each with a letter beside it to show whether that person walked, used a frame or was carried. She was going down the line. At each chair she pressed the tape flat with her thumb, read the name against her list and turned the blanket so that it would open towards the door. Maylis stood on the step with a bag of sand held against her good side and watched Coralie do eleven chairs before Coralie looked up.

‘I’ve put the ones who can walk nearest the front,’ Coralie said. ‘If the siren goes, Bixente fills the minibus, takes them up to the village hall and comes back for more. I haven’t timed it in water.’

‘It doesn’t have enough seats for that,’ Maylis said.

‘It has enough seats when nobody’s counting,’ Coralie said.

‘Seven minutes up, in the dry,’ Bixente said. ‘I did it for bingo night.’

Twelve seats and forty-two people made four trips. That was an hour, in the dry.

‘Then take them up now,’ Maylis said. ‘All of them, while it’s light.’

‘I asked at noon. The director rang Pau, and Pau said orange. On orange nobody leaves the building. On red Sarthou sounds his siren, and I move them.’ Coralie lifted the list on its cord. ‘This is as far as I go without a siren. I’m a care assistant, Maylis. I’ve got a corridor full of chairs and a name on every one, on the word of a woman who cuts roadsides.’

Madame Etcheto was sitting in the last wheelchair, at the top of the ramp, with her coat buttoned over her nightdress and her handbag on her knees.

‘You got stuck halfway through the words on bingo night,’ she said to Maylis. ‘I said the rest of them for you, over my duck.’

‘I heard.’

‘So did Inguma. It comes to my room at night. Tonight they want me out here in a chair, and it won’t know where to find me.’

Maylis laid the bags across the bottom of the door with their necks folded underneath, the way Sarthou laid his. Madame Etcheto counted each one out loud as it went down.

‘Seventeen,’ she said. ‘That’s not many.’

‘There are more on the gendarme’s step,’ Maylis said.

Madame Etcheto folded her hands on her handbag. ‘I won’t sleep in a chair. I’ve slept in room twelve since I came here, and I’ll make them put me back at 10.’

Coralie came out onto the step with Maylis and pulled the door almost shut behind her.

‘Something’s been on your mind since this morning,’ she said. ‘It isn’t on the pad. I looked. Don’t tell me now. I’ve got all of them to think about until 7.’

Maylis hadn’t meant to say it on a doorstep, with the bank over their heads. But she might not get another chance, and that frightened her more than asking did.

‘Come back across the landing.’

Coralie stood still.

‘Say it again.’

‘I’m asking. Come back across.’

‘I told you I’d come if you asked.’ Coralie’s voice shook. ‘I finish at 7 in the morning. I’ll carry the pillow across myself. Be in the house.’

* * *

By 5 Maylis was up on the bank, with the village van parked facing the gate. The river was over the red line. It had gone the colour of the sky, and it was very wide. It went past, a metre under the boards of the platform.

On her other side, far below, the flood fields were under a few centimetres of rainwater. The track ran across them for a kilometre, out to the hunters’ huts.

Jeannot’s white van came along the bank from the Port with its headlights on. It stopped beside hers, door to door, and Jeannot rolled his window down.

‘You rang me at 5 this morning,’ he said.

‘I wanted to hear you answer.’

‘You heard me.’ Jeannot wasn’t laughing. ‘What are you sitting here for?’

‘I’ve been told to stay by the gate and the phone. And keep the gate shut.’

‘Then I’m dry.’ He pointed his chin at the fields. ‘The river over the line on that side, and a hand’s width of water on my side. That’s what a bank is for.’

He was above ground, an arm’s length from her window. It was still daylight.

Maylis got out. With the key on her belt she unlocked the padlock on the platform, then the box under the rail. The handle was lying in the box on a cloth. She lifted it out with her left hand and fitted it in place. It went on with a knock.

A hundred metres back along the top of the bank, Larrère’s car was sitting with its engine running and its window up.

She leaned her weight on the handle. The chain came up out of the water one link at a time until it was tight, and the catch dropped into its first tooth with a click. The gate hadn’t moved yet. Eighteen turns would lift it off the stone. The nineteenth would need her shoulder. After that the handle would turn freely and the gate would go all the way up, in daylight, while Jeannot was standing on the bank behind her.

A van door slammed.

Jeannot came up the steps onto the platform in his rubber boots.

‘That’s my hut you’d be filling,’ he said. ‘I waited two weeks for water on it, and you’d put the river in my cellar up to the trapdoor. You were at the counter on Tuesday. You heard the vote, and my hand was up.’

‘The river comes through tonight.’

‘So you say. He says shut.’ He pointed his chin along the bank at the car. ‘I’ve never been on that man’s side of anything in my life. Today I am, so I want to know exactly where I stand. It’s your hand on the handle. Which is it going to be? Shut, or open?’

The river went by under the boards.

Larrère’s car hadn’t moved. Jeannot stood on her boards.

‘Either,’ Maylis said.

‘Then it’s shut,’ Jeannot said. ‘That’s how the vote left it.’

He was off the platform and down the steps before she could take it back.

She lifted the handle off. The catch let go of its one tooth, and the chain went loose and sank back into the water. She put the handle back on its cloth and locked the box.

Larrère’s car turned around in three moves and drove up the hill towards the village. At the last four houses its lights left the road and went in behind them, along the back lane. The Castagnet house was one of the four.

Jeannot had his engine running when she got to his window.

‘Don’t ring tonight, and don’t knock,’ he said. ‘My phone stays in the van, because I won’t have it going off in the hut a second time. And don’t come down in that van. The track’s soft at the dip, softer than where I got stuck, the winter you towed me out. You’d sit there till Easter.’

He looked down the track, with both hands on the wheel.

‘It’s a hole in a field,’ he said. ‘I know what it is. But I light the stove and lock the trapdoor, and nobody can get at me till it’s light. Not him, not the café, not my own kitchen. You’ve got your shed. Leave me my hole.’

The white van went down off the bank onto the track. She turned her own van a quarter turn, so that her headlights pointed after him.

The headlights lit up the rain. They lit the back of the white van as it went away down the track, throwing up mud. Low on its left-hand door the round reflector, screwed on at an angle, shone red.

She looked at it, looked away at the pillar and looked at it again. In a dream it would have changed. It hadn’t.

She watched the reflector all the way. It got smaller until it stopped, where the track ended. When his lights went off, hers were the only ones shining on it. A torch came on low down beside the van. Then it went under the ground.

She turned the van back towards the gate. From the top of the bank she could see Coralie’s back door, across Sarthou’s roof. It was open onto the yard. Coralie was standing in the light from it with her arms folded, looking up at the bank. Then she went in, and the door shut behind the sandbags. Seventeen bags, thirty metres from the patch.

* * *

At 5:30 the light was nearly gone. Maylis left her phone on the dashboard, where she had been ordered to keep it. She shut the van door and went down off the bank on foot.

She walked on the grass between the wheel tracks, which were full of water to the top. Her rib let her walk, but no faster. At the dip the track was under water from one side to the other. She went through it with the water halfway to her knees. Jeannot was right about the van.

The white van was parked where the track ended. Beyond it the hut was a low bump in the tall grass, with a chimney pipe smoking in the rain.

She knocked on the trapdoor with her left fist.

A lock slid back. The trapdoor lifted a hand’s width and stopped. In the gap she saw lamplight and one eye. She could smell his stove.

‘I told you not to.’

‘I walked,’ Maylis said.

‘I can see how you came.’ The eye went to her boots, then past her, along the empty track. ‘Who’s at the gate?’

‘It’ll be ninety over the line by 3, Jeannot. The bank goes at the patch. When it does, that gate has to come up, and four minutes after that this trapdoor is under the river.’

‘Pau gives less than that for high tide,’ Jeannot said. ‘I heard it on the radio. Who gave you ninety?’

‘I was told.’

‘You were told what’s under my floorboards. You were told the way to my kitchen as well.’ The gap got narrower. ‘The gate’s shut. Larrère’s told you shut, and with it shut I’m sitting in the driest hole in the village. I’m not coming up out of it for a number. Go back to your gate. If that phone rings on your dashboard while you’re standing here, he’ll have your job for it, and I won’t write you a letter.’

The trapdoor came down. The lock slid across underneath it.

She knocked two more times. The chimney pipe went on smoking.

She walked back with the rain behind her. It was dark by the time she reached the dip, so she crossed it by the sound of the water.

On the dashboard her phone showed two missed calls from G. Larrère, at 17:50 and 18:15. Under them was one message.

Where were you? I’ll ring again tonight. Be in that van.

* * *

Kévin was on the platform by the gate with a torch in his teeth, pulling a net up out of the water, hand over hand. The net was green and as fine as a stocking. It was empty.

‘Nobody nets with the river over the line,’ he said, around the torch. ‘It would take the net, and me after it. I’m bringing my nets in.’

He dropped the net into a cool box and took the torch out of his mouth.

‘The café says you’ve got a mayor inside you. Did she have a night like this one?’

‘She had a van at the end of the track,’ Maylis said. ‘Not yours. Jeannot’s.’

‘That’s something.’ Then he saw her face, and the river coming up towards the boards. ‘What else did she have?’

‘Nobody. She was by herself. How many seats has your van got?’

‘Me and one,’ Kévin said. ‘And the boxes.’

‘Castagnet’s in his hut, at the end of that track, with the trapdoor locked. I’ve been down, and he won’t come up for me.’

‘Would he come up for me? I’m in three of his letters.’

Kévin could turn the handle while she went back down for Jeannot. If that gate moved, Larrère would send the sheet with Kévin’s name on it. Kévin had said he would do the six months. She wasn’t going to have him turn the handle that would send him to prison.

From the top of the bank she could see Coralie’s back door, shut, with the light on over it. The minibus had twelve seats.

‘Take the van down to the care home, to the front yard,’ Maylis said. ‘Coralie’s working tonight, and she’s short of seats.’

‘And him?’

‘He’s mine.’

‘It’s still orange,’ Kévin said. ‘What do I say I’ve come for?’

‘Say I sent the van,’ Maylis said.

‘It wasn’t Either at the dip,’ Maylis said. ‘The van went in, and I had to pick one. I picked.’

‘I know what you picked. I was the other one.’

Coralie reached over to the fruit bowl and took a new envelope from behind it, from the place where her form used to stand. It had been cut open along the top. She laid it beside the pad.

‘Bayonne’s written. The geriatric floor, on nights. I start in January.’

‘Stay.’

‘That’s what you want,’ Coralie said. She turned the envelope around on the table. ‘I want that floor. I trained on it, under Ithurbide. A floor and a night of my own to run, where I say who gets turned and who’s left to sleep. I wanted it before Friday, and I want it more now. I can’t stand at that door at lights-out again.’

‘It’s twenty minutes. You could drive in from here.’

‘Don’t give me the measurement. I could. I’m not going to.’

She went upstairs. Maylis followed her as far as the bedroom door and found she had brought the pad with her.

Coralie packed one bag on the bed, out of the chest of drawers. Three uniforms went in, then the good coat, folded, then the clock she woke up to. Maylis stood in the doorway holding the pad in both hands and watched the bag fill. It wasn’t much. She took more than that to the dump in a morning.

‘I’ve asked you one question for sixteen years, and drawn you boxes for it,’ Coralie said, without turning around. ‘You’ve got a week of them in your hands. I’ll ask it once more, with no box. What do you want, Maylis?’

‘Madame Etcheto’s being buried here on Tuesday. I want to dig the grave myself.’

Coralie shut the bag.

‘Then you’ll dig it. With that side. I’ll strap it first.’

She carried the bag down and stood it by the front door. In the kitchen she got out the first-aid box she had brought home from work. Maylis sat while Coralie strapped the rib, with three turns of the wide bandage, pulled tight. She tucked the end in. Her hand stayed on it a little longer than a bandage needs.

Then she went upstairs. Maylis heard her cross the landing to the spare room and come out again. She heard her on every stair coming down, all eleven, slower than she had gone up. She heard the passage, then the front door, then the car door shutting on a pillow.

* * *

That night Maylis got into the middle of the bed, on her back, with the card from her raincoat in her hand, and waited for Inguma. There was one pillow on the bed.

At 2:51 Inguma came down and sat on her. She kept still under it. Then she was in the chair with Lesca. They got up out of it and faced each other, while Inguma waited just inside the door.

The wall where the hall had been was steel again. Lesca’s right hand hung open at her side. She didn’t call anything to order.

Maylis still had her card in her hand. left. Lesca was holding the one from the shelf. right. It was the same handwriting, with the t crossed low.

‘Forty-one,’ Maylis said.

Lesca didn’t answer.

‘Is yours in the house?’ Maylis said.

‘Across the landing. Four steps. Yours?’

‘She took the pillow.’ Maylis looked at the door. ‘Don’t come again.’

‘I won’t.’

It came out whole.

One lamp went out. Then the other.

In the dark the dry sound started on the steel floor, but Inguma didn’t come to the chair. It went out over the step of the door. As it went, the steel under its leaves knocked against the steel of the frame, like one part of the room touching another. Then the door shut behind it on the rubber seal.

Neither of them sat down.

Maylis woke under her own lampshade. Her phone said 02:54. There was nothing on the wardrobe.

* * *

The cemetery was above the village and had stayed dry. On Monday morning, when Maylis came up, the priest was at the gate under his umbrella.

‘Tomorrow at 10,’ he said. ‘Madame Etcheto’s daughter wants the words her mother said each night to be said over her. I’ve had them written out for me, and I’m not sure they’re a prayer.’

‘They’re for a thing that sits on you.’

‘They’ll do.’ He looked at the place by the wall, next to Monsieur Etcheto’s grave. ‘You’ll dig it? Monsieur Larrère tells me the village has no outdoor worker this morning.’

‘Then it gets dug for nothing,’ Maylis said. ‘Yes.’

‘It’s the eighth this year. I keep count.’

He went down to his car. She took the pegs and the string from the tool hut, set the first peg at the head, measured the length and the width from it and ran the string around the four pegs.

She cut the grass along the string with the edge of the shovel, left-handed. It was a morning’s work for two arms. She had one.

Jeannot came up the path from the gate with an envelope in his hand and stopped at the foot of the string.

‘I’ve got a letter,’ he said, ‘and I’ve brought it by hand. I paid to register forty-one of them, to get Larrère’s name on a card, and I never got it once. I’m not paying for this one.’

He held it out. In the top left corner he had written No. 42.

‘It’s not a complaint,’ he said. ‘I started it as one. It’s the first one that isn’t. It’s for my cousin Sarthou, with a copy for the mayor’s tray. It says that at 3 on Saturday morning the keyholder was at the trapdoor of my hut, a kilometre from her gate, pulling me out of it by the neck. It says why she thought she had to. It says the cellar was dry. It says the handle stopped at nineteen, and who had been written to about it. It says the gate went up under my hands at 3:20, and that it took two arms and a knee. You had the one arm.’

‘The arm is the excuse you’ve written for me. It isn’t mine,’ Maylis said. ‘At 3 I never tried the handle.’

Jeannot turned the envelope over. ‘It says who had rung her, and from whose kitchen. My cousin got that off the screen of your phone on Saturday, and he’s my cousin.’

‘Maïté left on the bus.’

‘I saw the wardrobe.’ He turned the envelope back. ‘You told me twice to put it in forty-two. It’s in. It’s four pages, and it doesn’t thank you. I was in no danger, and I’ll say so to anyone. But they’re saying you went for a walk, and I was what you walked to. That goes in writing.’

She took the letter and buttoned it into her chest pocket.

He looked at the string, at the cut grass, at the way she held the shovel.

‘Whose is it?’

‘Madame Etcheto’s.’

‘She gave my mother her first communion dress.’ He looked around. A second shovel was leaning against the wall of the tool hut, where Maylis’s father had kept it. ‘You won’t get that down two metres with one arm, Lesca.’

He went and got it. Then he stepped over the string in his rubber boots and began to dig.
