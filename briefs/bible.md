# Brief: the bible

You are the first writer on a novel of about 50,000 words, written in English for a literary prize. Read `PLAN.md` first: it holds the prize constraints and everything that is already decided. Your job is to write `bible.md`, the document every later writer will work from.

## What is fixed

The "Locked" section of `PLAN.md`. Do not reopen it. Everything else is yours to invent.

## What went wrong before

An earlier version of this premise had a physicist sealed in a box, with two versions of him wanting the same wife and child. That is Dark Matter by Blake Crouch. So:

- Nobody crosses between worlds, ever. Only dreams connect, and only things leak.
- He is an ordinary local man who was paid to sit in a sealed room for an afternoon. He never understood the experiment. The team left with what looked like a null result in both worlds, so there is no lab to return to and nobody to believe him.
- The difference between the two lives is not "family versus career".

A second earlier fault: the ideas "the monster is your own fear" and "the higher being is all of us" were delivered as late reveals. A reader guesses both early, so they cannot carry the book. The withheld thing is the event (see 4 below).

## Direction on tone and setting

The tone is not settled. A later trial writes the opening chapter two ways: deadpan and often funny on the surface with real loss underneath, or played straight and dark. Write a bible that serves both.

What we want from the setting, whichever tone wins:

- A village where everyone has a small motive and a secret, and the secrets interlock.
- Procedure applied seriously to small things: the council, the festival committee, the parish, the gendarme near retirement.
- Local politics that are current and specific, not generic.
- Working assumption: an invented village in the south of the Landes, on the Adour, within reach of Bayonne. Research it so the details hold: the flood meadows, the crops, the water disputes, the calendar of fêtes. Do not use a real village's name.

Three more points on the village:

- **It stays a village, and the achievement shows in it.** The register is fame, jealousy and grit. Fame here is local first: what one man achieved changed his village, so the two villages now differ, and the other man's village is the one nothing happened to. The same people live in both.
- **Callbacks.** Every villager is doubled. Give each a trait or a running bit that returns in the other world with a different outcome, traceable to what the man did or did not do. A leak can be knowledge: one man knowing a neighbour's secret because it came out in the other world.
- **The village knows about the experiment and is not in on it.** The council rented the room out, there are minutes, and the afternoon he was paid to sit in a sealed room is a standing joke. Nobody knows it did anything. No conspiracy: a town that is secretly in on it is Wayward Pines.

One idea to carry as theme, never to state: abolishing distance does not bring nearness. These are the two closest people who can exist, and they cannot reach each other.

## What `bible.md` contains

1. **The man.** Who he was on the day of the experiment: age, work, household, what he wanted. Choose his name. Both versions share it, so say how the text tells them apart without a gimmick.
2. **The village.** Six to eight recurring people, each with a want, a secret, and a tie to at least one other secret. Say what differs for each of them between the two worlds.
3. **The experiment.** What it was from where he sat, in words he would use. Get the physics right in outline and keep it out of the way. Say how long ago it was.
4. **Three candidate events.** Each is one thing that happened after the split and sent one life elsewhere. All three fit one frame: one man has achieved a great deal and it cost him a great deal, and he wants the other's life in order to forget; the other has achieved little and wants the first man's life for what it holds, knowing something of the price. The event is the start of that achievement and of its cost. Note the trap in the frame and use it: merging adds memories, so the man who wants to forget would get the opposite. The three must differ in kind, in what was achieved and what it cost, not in detail. If the frame fails for a reason you can show, say so in the bible. For each give:
   - what happened, and why it happened in one world only;
   - both lives as they stand now, and what each man wants that the other has, such that neither is the better version;
   - the glimpses: four or five things one man sees of the other's life in dreams, in the order the reader gets them, the wrong conclusion they invite, and the reveal;
   - what merging would cost, and who loses him.
   Then say which one you would write and why.
5. **The dream.** How they find each other, what can pass and what cannot, what the paralysis figure is and does, and why it is right to do it. A ladder of five leaks from trivial to grave. What it costs to get better at this. What the collective looks, sounds and feels like in the one or two glimpses the book allows: concrete and sensory, with nobody explaining it.
6. **The ending.** The choice, both ways it could go, and the one you recommend.
7. **Shape.** Ten to twelve beats from opening to end. A chapter outline comes later, once the event is picked.
8. **Neighbours.** A table of the closest existing novels, films and series, what each does, and how this book differs. Search for them; do not rely on the list in `PLAN.md`. If a candidate event turns out to be someone else's book, replace it.
9. **Titles.** Five candidates.

## Rules

- The bible is a working document. Write it plain and concrete, with no sales language. Aim for 2,500 to 3,500 words.
- Load the `stop-slop` skill before you write and audit the file against it before you finish.
- Invent every name. Do not use "Giovanni" or "Pasta".
- Nothing you write into this directory mentions any writing tool, model, vendor or session. No such wording in file names either.
- Write `bible.md` only. Do not run git, and do not start on chapters.
- When done, reply with five lines at most: what you chose for the man, the three events in a phrase each, your recommended event, and any question you could not settle.
