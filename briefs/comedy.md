# Brief: the comedy and the switch between tones

You are a comedy writer brought in on a novel that is nineteen chapters into a draft of twenty-five. JC, whose book this is, has read it and says the humour is not landing for him. He also says the line between dark and comic is a hard one to walk and wants someone whose only job is that line. You are that someone.

Read `PLAN.md`, `bible.md` and `outline.md`, then `manuscript/01.md` to the last chapter present. Read `audit/cold-read.md`, Part 3 question 11 above all, and `audit/laughs.md` if it exists by the time you get there: a first reader's account of what made them laugh and what did not.

## What we think went wrong

Take this as a hypothesis to test, not as the answer.

- The book's comedy is one device: a narrator who reports village procedure with a straight face. Votes, sums of euros, minutes, registered letters. A first reader stopped reading the numbers by chapter 6 and called the two running lines worn by chapter 9.
- Each chapter was drafted twice, once deadpan and once dark, and merged into "one voice, where a reader cannot tell where one draft ends". That may have flattened both. The result is one even register that is neither funny in the comic scenes nor frightening in the dark ones. JC's word for what he wants is "alternate".
- Understatement is carrying everything. Almost nothing in the book is funny as a situation.

## What JC finds funny

From his own writing, described and not quoted: farce run with procedural seriousness. A trivial crime investigated with full apparatus. Every character has a petty private motive and a secret, and the secrets collide. A priest who commits the crime for the cause and also because he wants the thing for himself. A hanger-on who joined a movement by accident on the way home from a party. People accusing each other to cover themselves. It is broad, it is situational, and it is driven by character and motive, not by the narrator's phrasing.

This village already has the material: a hunter who nets eels and writes forty-one letters, a mayor who pumps without a meter and visits the hunter's wife on hunting nights, a gendarme who tips off his cousin and wants only to retire, €3,400 gone from the fête cash, a priest with nine parishioners and a leaking roof. The draft states these and rarely lets them run into each other in a room.

## What to deliver

1. **A diagnosis**, in `audit/comedy.md`. Go through chapters 1 to 19 and list each thing the text means to be funny, with the line, and say whether it works and why. Be blunt. Say where a dark scene is weakened by a comic reflex, and where a comic scene is smothered.
2. **A method**, in the same file. Six or seven rules for how this book is funny: where the comedy comes from, what the narrator may and may not do, how a running line is built and paid, how many per chapter. English humour only: nothing that depends on a French pun.
3. **A tone map**, in the same file. For each of the twenty-five chapters, each scene marked comic, dark or held between, and where the switch falls. State the rules for switching: what may follow what, and how a chapter gets from one to the other. The creature, the flood night in chapter 19 and the last night stay dark.
4. **Two samples**, so JC can judge with his own eyes before anything in the book is touched. Rewrite the daytime of chapter 1 (from the mayor at the car window to the end) as `trial/comedy-01.md`, and one mid-book scene of your choosing where the secrets can collide, as `trial/comedy-mid.md`. Keep every event and fact the later chapters rely on. Say at the top of each, in two lines, what you changed.

## Limits

- JC has asked that little change. Events, people, dates and the order of the plot stay. You are changing how scenes play, not what happens in the book. If a scene needs an added exchange or a small new incident to be funny, add it and say so.
- The narrator still never winks, never remarks that something is absurd, and never jokes. The comedy is in what people want, say and do.
- Follow section 10 of the bible. The two women are not softened and are not made the butt.
- Load the `stop-slop` skill and audit your samples against it.
- Do not edit `manuscript/`, the bible or the outline. Nothing you write mentions any writing tool, model, vendor or session. Do not run git.
- Reply in six lines at most: your diagnosis in two sentences, the method in one, which mid-book scene you chose, and what you would need from JC.
