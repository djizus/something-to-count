# Brief: stop certifying

Two juries of five have now read the book, and the two jurors who would not put it forward for a prize gave one reason: "the wish to explain and certify what its best actions already leave a reader painfully able to judge". Most of the others named the same two habits. JC has agreed to cut both. Read the fault lists in `audit/jury2-double.md`, `audit/jury2-guilt.md`, `audit/jury2-jury.md` and `audit/lit3-jury.md` before you start.

Writer one has chapters 00 to 08. Writer two has chapters 09 to 17.

## 1. The steel room stops catching every lie

"The steel room is a lie detector. Ishiguro makes the reader catch the lie. Here the room catches it for us, six times."

Words stick in that room **twice in the whole book**, and nowhere else:

- **Chapter 9:** "'It's the ti—' Lesca said. She started again, and it stuck on the same word." with "'It came out in my kitchen.' 'This isn't your kitchen.'" This is where the reader finds out what the room does. It stays as it is. So do its two echoes: chapter 11, where "It's the tide" comes out in her own bedroom, and chapter 13, where she remembers that the word had not come.
- **Chapter 17:** "'I won't.'" and "It came out whole." Cut "They both heard it."

Everywhere else the women can say what they like in that room, and the reader has to hear whether it is true:

- **Chapter 4, "Either".** The word does not stick. Maylis opens her mouth to say Either and Lesca stops her, as she does now: "Don't. Not in here. I gave that word up when I was thirty-one, and I won't have my own face say it to me." Then Maylis has to pick, and picks. Cut "But Maylis wasn't saying it… It stuck in the same place", "'I can't,' she said. That came out easily", and "It had always come when she needed it. It had never been true, and it wasn't true now. She did want one of them, badly." What may stay, in a sentence, is that she had written the word on the pad on Tuesday.
- **Chapter 4, the bank.** Lesca's ready answer comes out whole: nobody has looked after that bank for twelve years. Maylis disputes it from what she knows. She built the patch herself in April, one wheelbarrow at a time, and she has written three letters about the handle. Lesca hears that and has no second answer. Then "'Then why does it come?' Maylis said." and "'I don't know why it comes.'" Cut "That I can say." Two people argue; nothing sticks.
- **Chapter 9, the last meeting of the chapter.** "'I w—' … 'I can't stand here and say I won't'" and "'This time I'm coming to get—' It stuck there… 'To stay,' she said. 'That's the one that comes out.'" Nothing sticks. Asked not to come on Friday, Lesca does not promise. Asked what she is coming for, she says the man at the end of the track first, as she believes it, and then says the rest herself, because she has written the motion and has never been afraid of an item: to stay. Keep "To stay". Cut "That's the one that comes out".
- Any other line in your chapters that says or shows that an untrue sentence cannot be spoken in that room: cut it, or let the sentence be spoken.

When you have finished, correct the entry for this in `state.md` part 2 (writer one does this) so that it says what the text now shows: twice, the tide and "I won't".

## 2. No sentence reads a gesture for the reader

"The prose too often interprets an image immediately after giving it."

In every chapter, find each sentence of narration that tells the reader what the line or the gesture just before it meant, or how to take a joke, and cut it. The jurors quoted these; there are more like them:

- "Maylis had never stood and smiled at Jeannot Castagnet in her life, and the whole café was watching her do it." The café's faces say it in the next line.
- "Nobody in Peyrebarthe had ever heard Maylis's mouth forbid anything."
- "Her left hand came across and closed over Coralie's fingers. Maylis hadn't told it to." and, two lines on, "Maylis hadn't said it." One of the two is enough.
- "One hand for, one against.", if it is still in chapter 3.
- "…the only form she had for it was a motion, the kind the council voted on." The last six words explain a word nobody needed explained.
- Chapter 17, Coralie: "I know that word. I've cooked it." stays. "That was Either, with the river in it." goes.

Expect three to eight in a chapter. Do not replace what you cut.

**These are not glosses and stay word for word:** "She wanted it not to be her."; "Lesca was gone. The water kept coming."; "Forty-one."; every line of speech not named above; and the short lines of each woman's own judgement added in the last pass ("A lot of steel for one chair.", "The paper lampshade: hers.", "It was more people than had ever voted for Lesca."). A judgement on a thing is hers. A sentence that explains what she or somebody else just did is the narrator's, and that is what goes.

## Rules

- Nothing else changes: no event, fact, number, date or time, and no other speech.
- The plain style of `briefs/line.md`. The simplest word. No "lay" or "lain" as the past of "lie". Inguma is "it". Clock times in figures. Every pronoun has an owner a reader can name at once.
- The book ends above 41,000 words.
- Nothing you write mentions any writing tool, model, vendor or session. Do not run git.

Reply in four lines: what you did under 1, scene by scene; how many sentences you cut under 2, with the three you are surest of quoted; anything you chose to keep and why; and the word count before and after.
