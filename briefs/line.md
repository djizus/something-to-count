# Brief: the line edit

JC is reading the book now, and these are his words, from the first pages:

- "She tried her right hand first, because she was right-handed." → "this is stupid honestly"
- "The night of the eleventh of December 2014" → "don't write the date in all letters", and "same for the hours"
- "you're using words I'm not used to such as the winch, the sluice. Are you sure these are the most common names for it? Always use the simplest."
- "Maylis woke on Wednesday with a second picture in her head, and it was still raining." → "I don't think this is a real writer formulation"
- "feels like you're mashing sentences together with no regard to the flow"

He is right on every one, and the last is the biggest. An earlier pass joined short sentences with ", and" to make them longer. There are 928 of those joins in 43,000 words. Most of them tie together two facts that have nothing to do with each other. That is what he is hearing.

This pass changes no event, no scene, no line of dialogue that is funny or that hurts, and no order of anything. It rewrites the narration sentence by sentence so that it reads the way Harlan Coben or Blake Crouch reads: you do not notice the sentences.

## 1. Flow

- **One thought to a sentence, and each sentence follows from the one before.** Before you write a sentence, know how it connects to the last one: it happens next, it is the cause, it is the result, it is what she sees, it is what she thinks about it.
- **", and" joins two parts of one action, or an action and its result. Nothing else.** "She took the pillow and went out." Yes. "She woke with a picture in her head, and it was still raining." No. Two unrelated facts get two sentences, in the order a person would tell them.
- **When two things are related, say how.** Use the ordinary words for it: when, while, after, before, until, so, but, because, which, as. Or use a full stop.
- **Vary the sentence the way speech does.** A short one. A fragment where a person would think in one. Then a longer sentence that carries a whole movement from its start to its end. Not the same shape three times running.
- **Stay in her head.** The narration can have her opinion in it. "By her count that was a tank of diesel a week, to go nowhere." That is the voice.
- **Open a scene by putting us somewhere**, simply, and then go.
- Read every paragraph aloud. If you would not say it that way to a friend across a table, rewrite it.

## 2. Nothing obvious

Cut any clause or sentence that tells the reader what he can see for himself, or gives a reason nobody needed. "…because she was right-handed." "He kept the cap in his hands, because he was waiting for the rest of it."

## 3. Dates and times in figures

- Dates: "11 December 2014", "Tuesday 24 November 2026", "Friday the 11th", "the 12th".
- Times, in narration and in speech: "2:51 a.m.", "at 5 p.m.", "at 7", "at 3 in the morning". "Nine minutes to three" is "2:51" everywhere. A clock or a phone screen shows "02:51" as now, and the time stamps of the flood night stay as they are.
- Counts stay in words: nineteen turns, forty houses, forty-one.

## 4. The simplest word

## 5. A model

This is the opening of chapter 1 as it should now read. Match it.

> Tuesday 24 November 2026, 2:51 a.m.
>
> Maylis Lesca was awake in her own bed and couldn't move.
>
> She tried her right hand. Nothing. She tried her left, then both feet, then her jaw. The only things that still obeyed her were her eyes. She could see the ceiling, the paper lampshade, the top of the wardrobe and the red numbers of the clock radio. Rain was falling on the roof.
>
> Something was sitting on the wardrobe.
>
> It was about the size of a sheep and darker than the ceiling behind it. As she watched, it came down. It hit the floor like a full sack dropped off a trailer, then crossed the room with a dry sound, like dead leaves being dragged. It jumped onto the bed the way a dog does and sat down on her chest, with its back to her.
>
> She wanted to scream. She couldn't.
>
> Its back was all she could see. It was covered in brown leaves that overlapped like roof tiles, and where the leaves parted she could see grey steel underneath, with a row of rivets. The thing weighed as much as a sack of cement. She lifted those for a living, but never off her own chest. It smelled of cold steel and old tobacco, in a house where nobody had ever smoked.
>
> She knew that smell.
>
> She pushed up against the thing with all the breath she had, which wasn't much. She had cracked a rib on Monday, and that was why she was sleeping on her back in the first place. The thing pressed down just as hard. Beside her Coralie slept on, turned to the wall.
>
> At 2:54 the little finger of her right hand moved against the blanket. The rest of the hand followed. The thing got down off her and left the way it had come, and by the time Coralie turned over there was nothing on top of the wardrobe.

And two more, before and after:

> *Before:* Towards morning she slept, and she woke with a picture in her head. It was a picture of a stone that stood in front of the town hall, in the space where the mayor parked.
> *After:* She slept a little towards morning. When she woke there was a picture in her head that hadn't been there before: a stone, standing in front of the town hall where the mayor parked his car.

> *Before:* Maylis woke on Wednesday with a second picture in her head, and it was still raining.
> *After:* On Wednesday it was still raining. Maylis woke up with another picture in her head.

## Rules

- Every event, fact, name, number and line of speech stays. Speech changes only for sections 3 and 4.
- The cold open, `00.md`, gets this pass too. Its heading becomes "The night of 11 December 2014".
- Each chapter ends within five per cent of its present length.
- Nothing you write mentions any writing tool, model, vendor or session. Do not run git.

Reply in five lines at most: the count of ", and" before and after in each chapter, the word count before and after, and any other words you simplified.
