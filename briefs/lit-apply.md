# Brief: the literary readers' faults

Five widely read critics placed this book in its traditions (`audit/lit-double.md`, `lit-village.md`, `lit-uncanny.md`, `lit-guilt.md`, `lit-jury.md`). All five would put it on the shelf and forward for a prize. They agree on its faults. JC has read their reports and agrees with them: fix all of them. Read section 5 and section 6 of all five reports before you start. Section 6 of each is what must not be lost.

The decisions are made below. Everything you write keeps the book's plain style (`briefs/line.md`) and JC's own notes: dates and times in figures, the simplest word, nobody she knows is "a man" or "a woman", Inguma is always "it", a named speaker in any run of untagged speech.

## A. The rules are found, never read out

"The uncanny is administered." "A rulebook, read out."

- **Chapter 4.** The meeting stays, with its jokes: nobody in the chair, "All those in favour", a tie the chair decides. **The list of points goes.** Cut "The second item is how this works. There are three points, and I'll say them once" and every point. The reader has watched each one happen: Lesca stayed sitting and woke in the other bed; the right hand was not hers; the old woman gave the thing something to count. Where one of them has to be said for the scene to move, a person says what happened to her, in a line, as something that happened and not as a rule.
- **The truth is never announced as a rule.** Cut "So that's a fourth point… Standing up in this room, a woman can't say a thing she knows isn't true", in whatever form it stands, here and anywhere else. Keep what happens: "Either" sticks in Maylis's mouth, Lesca's ready answer about the bank dies on the same word, the tide will not come out in chapter 9, "To stay" is the one that comes out, and at the end "I won't" comes out whole. The reader sees words stick in that room. Nobody says why.
- **The rain is never a law.** Chapter 9: cut "One night matters. If I'm in your village at 2:51 on Friday night, your bank goes, as mine was going. If I'm not, it holds", and any sentence that states it elsewhere. What there is: two dry days and a cross on a pad, Coralie's chart, and a lie that will not come out. Maylis believes it. She can never be sure. "Don't come on Friday" follows from that.
- No other sentence in the book says how any of it works. Where the narrator or a person recites a mechanism, cut it.
- **One scene in which the ordinary explanation is believed.** Chapter 4, at breakfast: Coralie is a nurse, and she has an ordinary name for a weight on the chest, open eyes and a voice that is not yours after three nights without sleep. She says it, kindly and with a case from work, and for a moment Maylis takes it. Six to ten lines.

## B. The book stops explaining itself

"It explains its own moral." "The narrator explains the scene just shown."

- **Chapters 13 and 14.** Strike every sentence that states an equivalence between the two nights or the two women: "Twelve years ago she had let his phone ring once and then done what she had come to do. Holding this wheel was the same thing." "Lesca had let his phone ring once and then turned the handle. Maylis had let it ring nine times, and Jeannot was alive." And the others of that kind. Lesca sees the hand on the brake and the reflector, and lets go.
- **The dip keeps "She wanted it not to be her."** It is the book's truest line. So chapter 17 does not say it again: to Kévin she says he told her not to keep the gate shut for him, that she didn't, and that it wasn't why. She does not give the reason a second time.
- **The last meeting in the steel room** is: "Forty-one." "Is yours in the house?" "Across the landing. Four steps. Yours?" "She took the pillow." "Don't come again." "I won't." Then the two cards into the envelope, the lamps, Inguma, and neither sits. Cut "I sent you down that track…", "At the gate I thought I could do better than you…", "you know what you went up my stairs for" and Lesca's answer about the stairs.
- **Chapter 1.** "What she wanted was… so Maylis didn't ask" is used three times in one chapter (the wrist, the duck, the car window). Keep it once, at the wrist. The shopping pad does the rest.
- Cut "It was going to cost her Coralie" (5), "They shut you in a box once, to see whether there were two of you. Would the other one know what she wants for dinner?" (1), and "Maylis knew why, from the chair. Lesca had put cameras on the gates…" (6). In chapter 6 Maylis meets Kévin's cap in his hands with no reason given. She learns about the cameras and the six months later that day, from Larrère's mouth at the council, where he already speaks of the cameras.
- Glosses no speaker would give go: "The prefecture at Pau, the State's office over all these villages" is "The prefecture at Pau".

## C. The author's hand

- **Chapter 3** opens on the bed, with rain on a roof that should be dry and two pillows where hers has one. Cut "The other Maylis Lesca lived in a Peyrebarthe that had its flood in 2014. Afterwards it made her mayor. Call her Lesca." She is Lesca from her first sentence, and the reader counts the differences with her.

## D. Tags, numbers and clocks, each used less

"Tics run past their yield." "Everything rhymes." "A thriller's clock in a village."

- Each tag is set, turned and paid, and that is three times, not eight: "I'll be brief", Monsieur Daguerre's "Here", "a hand's width", "That's my door". Jeannot numbers his points in chapter 1, on the phone at 5 in the morning, and once more; elsewhere he just talks. Cut "Jean's for my gravestone."
- **Nineteen** is the turn the handle sticks at. It is not also the days of rain, the bags of sand and Coralie's age when she trained. Change the others to plain numbers that rhyme with nothing: eighteen days of rain where the count allows, seventeen bags, Coralie at twenty. Check the cold open and the speech in chapter 15 agree with each other.
- **Chapter 14.** No clock time stands on a line by itself. The times that matter are told in a sentence: the bank goes at 3:11, and Kévin's phone buzzes at 4. Half of the others go; the rest are folded into sentences.
- **Two things are left unpaid.** Cut "The card from the steel room wasn't in there any more" on the last page, and "The rib's in, as I said it would be" from Jeannot's letter.

## E. People who were catchphrases

- **Larrère, once, not through glass.** Chapter 12: when he comes out of the town hall with the orange warning he does not get straight into his car. The exchange happens on the steps, on foot, in the rain. When she tells him what she saw in the back lane, he says one thing about Maïté that is his own and that he means. Then he gets into the car and the window goes up. Take out his counter-threat about the café's pad and the chrysanthemums there, and take out "Hontaube opens that file and decides who it keeps" in chapter 5: the resignation letter and the kitchen clock are enough levers.
- **Jeannot, once, off his routine.** Chapter 12, at his van window at dusk or at the trapdoor: one unguarded thing, with no number on it, before the night.
- **Lesca knows what she brings, and the reader sees her know it.** Chapter 11, the scene where she lies on her side and then turns onto her back: one or two lines before the turn that make it plain what she is choosing to carry across. Nothing explains it after.
- **Five o'clock, chapter 12.** One beat before "Either": what she sees, in two short sentences. Larrère's car a hundred metres back. Jeannot on her boards. Then the word.

## F. Inguma is the box

"Inguma has no will… leaves cover grey steel with a row of rivets, which promises that the folk demon and the laboratory box are one thing. The book never collects on it."

- One sentence, late, in chapter 17, as it goes out over the step: the reader sees the steel under the leaves and the steel of the room together. Do not show its face and do not explain.

## G. The sentence, twice

"The prose never risks a long sentence, even in the hall of the dead."

- In the hall of the dead in chapter 13, and once in the square in chapter 15, let one sentence run long, carrying the whole room from one end to the other. It must still be a sentence a person could say, and it must not join unrelated things with ", and".
