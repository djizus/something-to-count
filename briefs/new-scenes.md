# Brief: the new scenes

A third round of critics read the book and called it a 7 with a 9 inside it: the last third at the bar, the middle not. `revision3.md` is the plan that answers them. The swap becomes the middle of the book, the two women argue in the room from the midpoint, each decisive act is played from inside, and the endings cost. Read `revision3.md` in full, then the sections of `bible.md` it says changed, then the reports in `audit/round3-*.md` for what each scene is answering.

The chapters the plan works from are now in `manuscript-v3/`. The plan names their scenes by chapter and letter, in the order of the section breaks: 05b is the second scene of chapter 5.

## What to write

Section 2 of `revision3.md` lists the new scenes, each with its writer. Write every scene that is yours, each into its own file in `drafts/v4/`, named by its number: `N01.md`, `N02.md` and so on.

- Read the old scenes that will stand on either side of yours in the new chapter, so that yours joins them. Another writer assembles; give them clean edges.
- Keep to the length given, within ten per cent.
- One register to a scene, as the plan marks it. `audit/comedy.md` has the method: the comedy is in what people want, say and do; the narrator never winks; a comic scene leaves the reader more worried than it found them.
- Follow the line pass in section 3 of `revision.md`: who owns which habit of speech, the creature called Inguma once named, the woman named in the first sentence, few figures, few negated verbs.
- Section 10 of the bible stands. Neither woman is softened, excused or made the butt.

## The scenes that carry most

- **The arguments in the room.** Two versions of one stubborn woman, either side of a chair with Inguma in it. Each knows the other's evasions and neither can lie to the other. One is short and wrong, the other long and certain. These must be the funniest scenes in the book and the most dangerous, and they must move the plot: after each, something is different.
- **The days in the wrong village.** Long, in public, with the owner inside fighting for her hand and her mouth. Let them get out of hand, and let each leave the reader more afraid.
- **Lesca's letting go, from inside.** The hall's empty chair is offered to her as a third way out, and she refuses it too. The reader is in her head for it.
- **"Both."** Maylis has answered "Either" all book. At the gate she says what she wants, in a word, and goes. It is her choice and the reader must feel her make it.
- **What the confession costs, and what the last chapter costs.** Shown, not reported.
- **Larrère using his hold.** At length, as a man who can hurt her.

## The prose, this time

- **The pluperfect is back.** The last pass cut "had" and broke the clock. Wherever the story steps back in time, say so with the tense.
- **People do not share one wit.** Each has an idiom, and can be surprised out of it.
- **Pace follows the scene.** Longer sentences in calm, short ones in fear.
- **The narrator does not keep score, recap before a payoff, or announce a feeling flat.** No "for some time", no stock gesture twice, no year counts.
- **No noun withheld.** If a reader has to stop to work out what "it" or "the word" is, name it.

## Rules

- Load the `stop-slop` skill before you write, and audit each scene before the next.
- British spelling. Nothing you write mentions any writing tool, model, vendor or session. Write only your scene files. Do not run git.
- Reply when all are written: one line a scene with its word count and anything you decided that the plan did not.
