# Brief: the second revision plan

Five fresh critics read the revised book. Their reports are `audit/round2-thriller.md`, `round2-comedy.md`, `round2-characters.md`, `round2-reader.md` and `round2-prose.md`. Read all five in full, section 3 and its "Do" lines above all.

**The verdict.** Better, and still one step below the bar. Every score for the last third is at 8 or 9. The verdict on the whole is "read as far as chapter 8 before judging", and that sentence is the fault. They agree on what is left, and so does the reviewer. Most of it is structural, and most of it comes from one thing: the book keeps its two women apart and polite. They want each other's lives and never touch them.

Write `revision2.md`, in the same form as `revision.md`: the chapter list with keep, move, cut and new; the new scenes with their writer; the cuts and the line pass; changes to the bible; the tracks; the checks. Old scenes are now named from the current `manuscript/` chapters. Update `bible.md`.

## What this plan must achieve

1. **The danger is known by the end of chapter 4.** Lesca has the rain, the date and the file by then. The reader is ahead of her by chapter 3: a gauge reading that audibly matches a figure in her minute. Nothing that needs chapter 8 to make it a thriller.
2. **What she hid is marked as hidden.** In chapter 2 the reader must know that her account of that night leaves something out, so that chapter 17 reverses it.
3. **The double touches the marriage.** One crossing puts Lesca in Maylis's body with the Coralie who still shares that bed, for long enough to speak to her with that mouth, and for Coralie to know something is wrong. After it Lesca's motion says what it is: she would be taking her. Maylis learns that the other one wants her bed, and from then on the two are against each other.
4. **The swap is played at full length, as farce, in daylight.** Each woman gets one long scene in the other's village, with witnesses. Lesca, who has chaired for eleven years, in the yellow waterproof with a man instructing her through a gap in a car window. Maylis, who has never given a straight answer, in the kitchen where three councillors ask the mayor to keep quiet. The owner is inside, fighting for her own hand and mouth. This is the book's own comedy and it has been rationed to a beat.
5. **The fight for the chair happens.** Both still want it when they meet; cut whatever talks either of them out of it beforehand. In the room Maylis speaks to her: "You rang once." Lesca sits and holds. Maylis wakes in the van with a second will in her right hand, one reaching for the handle and one for the ignition, with the telephone ringing. Lesca lets go there, and the five words are what she says as she does.
6. **Someone is dangerous before the last twenty pages.** Larrère has a hold on Maylis that the reader can see by chapter 9 and that he uses on the Friday: four winters of men netting at a gate she held the key to, a staff file, a pension. His order has to cost her something to disobey.
7. **The ten hours at the gate are shut on the page.** Either she tries the winch at dusk against orders and it stops at nineteen, or she goes down and the hatch is shut on her. The reader must not ask why she waited.
8. **Each crossing is told once, from the side that learns most.** The other side gets a line. At least five crossings move to the middle of a chapter and those chapters end in the village. At least four things that now arrive at night arrive by day, in front of people. The alarm, "she wrote before she moved" and the way in stop being the last section of every chapter.
9. **The cause of the second flood is a finding, as blunt as the rule of the chair, by chapter 10**, and it drives her: she is going over to stop what she started.
10. **The reader knows about Larrère and Maïté by chapter 13**, so the farce of the chrysanthemums plays to a full house. Chapter 22 keeps only the telephone and the clock. One line gives what he was to her.
11. **The commemoration is played in full**, and the choice at the third sheet is made at the microphone. Lesca pays in public. Her confession lands before anybody else's is added to it, and one of her two rewards on the last night has to wait.
12. **The death is not signposted, and the reader fears for Coralie until the count.** Cut the numbers called through the steel that give it away.
13. **Maylis is funny on purpose until the flood.** "Either" goes on causing visible trouble in public. Her wish for the sash is shown in something she does. She is not built out of other people's verdicts on her.
14. **The mayor's history is argued in the present.** No decision of hers is summarised if two people can fight about it in a room. The laugh in the minute's silence has to make the reader laugh a line before the widow turns.
15. **The rules are said aloud by someone pedantic, and then broken.** Kévin has a forum behind him. The hall gets one person's plain guess at what it is.
16. **The line pass, harder.** No noun is withheld: "the word", "the item", "it" where a reader has to stop. "Had" by half: back-history goes into mouths that are arguing. The three best "as a man would" similes and no others. The numbers that stay are 02:51, nineteen, forty houses, forty-two, forty-one, ten against twenty-five; the rest are rounded or cut. The gate and the tonne can be pictured in chapter 1. Glosses come once, at rest, never on an entrance. The two Coralies do not sound alike, and no villager is a single tag.
17. **Length 55,000 to 60,000 words.** What is added is paid for out of the repeated crossings.
