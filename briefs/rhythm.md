# Brief: the rhythm pass

The book was rewritten into a fluent, plain voice (`briefs/style.md`, `briefs/style-words.md`) because JC, who reads Harlan Coben and Blake Crouch in English with ease, found the old one hard to follow and not smooth. The new voice is easy to parse. It is not yet smooth. A reader measured it: the average sentence is under ten words in every chapter, whether a woman is making eggs or running for her life. Page after page of short flat statements reads as choppy.

Coben and Crouch are smooth because their rhythm moves. Go through your chapters and give the prose that movement.

- **In calm, in thought, in comedy:** let a sentence run when it carries one movement from start to end. Join two statements only when the second is the next step or the result of the first, and say how they connect: when, while, after, so, but, which. Never join two unrelated facts with ", and". JC read the result of doing that and said: "feels like you're mashing sentences together with no regard to the flow". `briefs/line.md` has the rule and a model.
- **In danger and action:** keep it clipped. Fragments are allowed. One-line paragraphs where a thing lands.
- **At a turn:** a long sentence, then a short one.
- **Vary how sentences begin.** Not "She" five times running, not "It was" as the default opening.
- **Read each paragraph aloud.** If it sounds like a list, it is one.

Limits:

- No word gets harder and no sentence gets harder to follow. Subject, verb, object, in that order. A longer sentence is still one a fluent non-native reader takes in at speaking pace.
- Change rhythm, not content: every fact, event, line of dialogue and joke stays. Dialogue is touched only where it was chopped by the same habit.
- Keep each chapter within five per cent of its length.
- The flood defence is the bank.
