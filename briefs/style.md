# Brief: the voice of the book, changed

JC has read the book. His verdict: the story is good and the prose is hard to follow. "Weird vocabulary quirks, it doesn't feel smooth, and the sentence construction leaves a lot to be desired." He is not a native English speaker. He reads Harlan Coben and Blake Crouch in English, and watches The Good Place in English, with no trouble. This book he finds tough.

He is right, and it explains what fourteen rounds of critics kept saying without naming: "the sentences give up their meaning slowly." The narrator was built to sound like a dry English minute-book. That voice is the fault. It goes.

## The new voice

Write so that a fluent non-native reader never stops. The model is the ease of Coben and Crouch: you read a page without noticing the sentences.

- **Ordinary sentence order.** Subject, verb, object. Who did what. No inversion, no sentence that holds its point back to the end, no clause stacked on a clause.
- **Ordinary words.** The word a person would say aloud. "Phone", not "telephone". "Headlights", not "headlamps". "Raincoat", not "waterproof". "Torch", the British word, as the spelling is British. "Looked at", "saw", "knew", "felt".
- **The simplest word, always.** If a reader who learned English at school would need a dictionary, use the plain word or say the thing plainly. No "winch", "sluice", "pawl", "gauge". The table is in `briefs/line.md`.
- **Dates and times in figures.** "11 December 2014", "Friday the 11th", "2:51 a.m.", "at 5 p.m.". Counts stay in words.
- **Nothing obvious.** No clause that gives a reason nobody needed, and no sentence that says what the reader can see.
- **Short paragraphs.** A new one when the beat changes. One-line paragraphs where a thing lands.
- **Say what she feels and thinks, directly, where nothing has shown it.** "She was scared." "She wanted that." Stay inside her head. But if an action or a line of speech has just shown the feeling, do not name it afterwards. A sentence that tells the reader what the scene has shown is a caption, and captions are cut.
- **Say what is happening, directly.** If a rule, a plan or a danger matters, state it in a plain sentence the first time. Never make the reader work it out.
- **Mark time and place plainly.** "That night." "The next morning." "Twelve years earlier."
- **Contractions** in dialogue and in thought.
- **Present danger in short sentences; calm in longer ones.**

## What goes

- The bookkeeping and council idiom in the narration: "priced it", "took note", "made ten", "had the room", "put it at thirty-five kilos", "by the church clock he had been nine minutes".
- Perception as possession: "she had the ceiling", "she had its back". Say she saw it.
- "As a man does who...", "as she would have...", "for some time", "it was the kind that...".
- Period and regional diction: "of a night", "nor", "all the same", "was to", "he'd not".
- French institutional words where English has one: write "the town hall", "the village", "bingo night", "the marsh" or "the flood meadows", "the hunting blind". Keep a French word only where it is a name or a taste of the place, and never more than a few in the book.
- Lists of exact figures. Keep the numbers the story turns on: 02:51, nineteen turns, forty houses, forty-two beds, forty-one, ten minutes against twenty-five, and the river gauge.

## What stays

- Every event, scene, fact, name and date. The order of everything. This is a change of voice, not of story.
- Every line of dialogue that is funny or that hurts, in words as close to the present ones as fluent English allows. The comedy is in what people want, say and do, and it should now be easier to hear. Dialogue is contemporary and natural; people may keep a turn of phrase that is theirs.
- The dark scenes stay dark and the comic ones comic. The narrator does not joke and does not explain a joke.
- The three rules of the crossing, stated plainly.
- Third person, past tense. One woman is Maylis and the other is Lesca, by name, always.
- The flood defence is the bank.

## A test for every sentence

Read it aloud at speaking pace. If you would have to say it twice for a listener who learned English as a second language, rewrite it.

## Rules

- Load the `stop-slop` skill and audit what you write. Plain is not flat: no filler, no clichés, no stock gestures.
- Keep each chapter within ten per cent of its present length.
- Nothing you write mentions any writing tool, model, vendor or session. Do not run git.
