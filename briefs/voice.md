# Brief: the prose pass

Ten critics have scored this book's prose at 6 to 7 while scoring its people, comedy and ending at 8. They say the same thing in different words: "exact, clear, and at one pace throughout"; "almost never wrong, never once a voice"; "the two women are narrated identically"; "nobody is telling it". JC agrees: the prose is the weakest part and has to come up to the rest.

A first version of this pass was tried on chapters 1 and 3 and put in front of five blind readers, three of them fluent readers of thrillers whose first language is not English, two of them prize jurors. This brief is what that test showed. Their reports are `audit/voicepair/r1.md` to `r5.md`; read two of them before you start.

## What the test showed

All five said the same two things.

**The voice is carried by short lines of the woman's own judgement.** Every reader named these as the lines to keep, and said that with them the page tells you whose head you are in:

- Maylis, who lifts things for a living: "A lot of steel for one chair." "Grey granite, as high as her shoulder. Somebody had put it up straight." "Maylis would have pulled it up in October." "Wherever she started, she raked her way back to it."
- Lesca, who runs meetings: "The paper lampshade: hers. The top of the wardrobe: hers." "Clock radio: 06:20. Pillows: one." "It took her half a minute."
- And plainer wording of what was roundabout: "Only her eyes still worked."

**Everything else that was tried made the book harder to read and was sent back.** Do none of these:

- **No joining.** Every sentence that folded two facts into one was read twice: "…the floodgate down in the river bank, where the handle had stuck and thrown her against the rail"; "Any member could speak for three minutes on any item, a rule Lesca had made in her first month as mayor, for Larrère, who had been mayor before her". Each fact keeps its own sentence. Where the present text has two short sentences, they stay two.
- **No turned-around word order.** "Since 2018 she hadn't spoken to Lesca directly", "Between her and the wall was Coralie", "For the last item, the stone, Marie-Jo stood…". The subject comes first, as it does now.
- **No decoration.** A child's glove on a bench "belongs to an author, not to a woman with a rake, who would have moved it". An object is added only if she does something about it or has an opinion of it, and that opinion is the point.
- **No wit laid on top of the work.** "…before the drains noticed. Drains do not notice."
- **No sentence that announces the device.** "She took the room the way she took a meeting, one item at a time" explains the list that follows. The list is enough.
- **No device at a bare moment.** "Deferred.", at the door of the spare room, was the one place that should have been left alone.
- **No rarer word for a plain one.** "Put her question" went back to "asked".

## What you do

Three things, and nothing else.

**1. A few short lines of her judgement, in her words.** About one every 250 to 300 words of narration, fewer in fast scenes. One short sentence, set down beside the thing she is looking at, saying what she makes of it. No "she thought". It must be something only this woman would think:

- **Maylis** is the village's outdoor worker and gravedigger. She judges things as work and materials: whether a thing was done properly, what it weighs, how long the job would take, what she would have done with it. She counts in turns of a handle, trailers, metres, bags. She is short of words and never clever.
- **Lesca** has chaired a council for eleven years. She takes the world as items, votes, minutes, speaking time, who is present. She rules on things: hers, not hers, carried, noted, out of order. She times people. She is drier and colder than Maylis.
  - Her itemising is used once or twice in a chapter, not on every page, or it becomes a tic.
  - **It thins as she loses the office.** In chapter 3 it works. In chapter 11 it holds and leaks. In the van and the hall of the dead (13) there is no item for what is happening and she has no ruling to give. By the last pages nothing is called to order.

The line must not explain the sentence before it, and it must not be a joke the reader has to work out.

**2. Cut the sentences that explain or repeat.** The readers picked these out of the old text unprompted: "Its back was all she could see" (it had turned its back one line earlier); "She kept looking at it"; "She read it over"; "The only things that still obeyed her were her eyes" (the long way round); "she could see" three times in seven lines. Every chapter has some. For each line you add under 1, find one of these and cut it or shorten it.

**3. Leave the rest alone.** Sentence length, word order, and which facts share a sentence stay as they are. At the worst moments (a death, a count, the confession, the last meeting in the steel room) add nothing at all.

## What you may not touch

- **Speech.** No line of dialogue changes.
- **What happens**, in what order, and every fact, number, date and time. `state.md` parts 1 to 3 hold them. A line you add may not bring in a new object, person or fact that a later chapter could contradict, unless it is something plainly in front of her that nothing else touches.
- **The sentences the critics quoted with praise** stay word for word. Among them: "She watched her sentence travel back through the square and down the road, row by row, at walking pace."; "Then the water rose up the door and covered it, and the red light went out."; "It rang once."; "She wanted it not to be her."; "Lesca was gone. The water kept coming."; "Somebody pulled a chair in to a table."; "Then he stepped over the string in his rubber boots and began to dig."; the hall of the dead; the flood night's counts; the last steel-room scene.
- **JC's rules:** the simplest word, always; nothing obvious; no two statements joined by ", and" unless they are parts of one action; one comparison to a sentence, and few; dates and clock times in figures; no "lay" or "lain" as the past of "lie"; Inguma is "it"; every pronoun has an owner a reader can name at once.
- **Length.** Each chapter ends within 2% of its present word count.

## How to work

Read your chapter twice: once as a reader, once sentence by sentence. Expect to change between eight and twenty sentences in a chapter, most of them by adding one short line or cutting one. When you have finished, read each line you added and ask two questions. Would a fluent reader have to read it twice? Could the other woman have thought it? If either answer is yes, cut it.

Nothing you write mentions any writing tool, model, vendor or session. Do not run git.

Reply in three lines for each chapter: how many lines you added and how many you cut, with the word count before and after; the two added lines you are surest of, quoted; and the one you are least sure of, quoted.
