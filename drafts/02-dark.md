# 2

At nine minutes to three on the morning of Tuesday 24 November, the mayor of Peyrebarthe was awake in her own bed and could not move.

She did not try her hands. She had tried them on other nights, years back, and had learned what trying cost. The weight lay on her breastbone as it had on most nights since December 2014. It was as heavy as a sack of cement, its chill came through the blanket, and it smelled of cold steel and old tobacco. It pressed as hard as she pushed. If she lay under it without pushing and let her breath go, it put her back to sleep inside ten minutes. For years she had not needed the clock to know when it came. She turned her eyes to the clock now, and it read 02:51.

Tonight she had something in her head that kept her from letting go. It had not been there when she put the light out.

Someone in a yellow waterproof was turning the hand winch on the sluice gate. She saw the back, with the hood up and COMMUNE DE PEYREBARTHE printed across the shoulders, and she knew the waterproof for her own. She had handed it in with the keys in April 2015. Rain was coming straight down on the platform and the planks were black with it. The one in the waterproof counted the turns aloud. At nineteen the handle stopped. A shoulder went under it and pushed, and the handle gave without warning and threw the yellow back into the platform rail. The rail caught it under the right arm. Through the rain she heard the rib go, with the sound a stick makes underfoot. Whoever it was hung over the rail for the length of six or seven breaths, each one cut short, and then finished the turns left-handed.

Beyond the platform lay the low barthe, the common flood meadow below the dyke, with a hand of water standing in the grass. Ponies were grazing it.

She could not have driven down and looked at any of it. The commune had scrapped that winch in 2016, when the new dyke went in and the gates were made automatic, and a dealer from Dax had cut it off the platform with a torch and paid for the iron by weight. The ponies had left in a lorry in 2019. No rain had fallen on Peyrebarthe since the ninth, and outside her window the gutter was silent.

She had turned that winch for seven years and had come away from it with her ribs whole.

The landing light came on under the door. The door opened, and Coralie stood in it with one hand on the frame.

'You shouted.'

The mayor told her mouth to open. The weight came down on the bone by as much as she had pushed, and her mouth stayed shut.

'Nineteen,' Coralie said. 'You said it twice.' She did not put the lamp on, and the bed was in the dark. 'Are you awake?'

She pushed again, with the jaw alone. Her breath stopped halfway in, and the light from the landing lay across the blanket over her feet, which she could not draw up out of it.

Coralie stayed about as long as she would have stayed at a resident's door on her rounds. Then she pulled the door to, and the light under it went out.

The mayor let her breath go and stopped pushing, as she had on some three thousand nights, and the weight put her under.

* * *

She woke at six knowing that Jeannot Castagnet had sent the mairie forty registered letters. Twenty-six of them concerned the water in the low barthe, which he held to be too low for duck, and all forty stood in a box file in the cupboard behind the secretary's desk. She knew it as she knew that the committee met at ten. She had both feet on the cold boards before the other thing she knew came back, which was that he had sent twelve and had been dead for twelve years.

She sat on the edge of the bed and pressed along her right side with two fingers, one rib at a time, from the spine round to the front. None of them hurt.

Coralie was in the kitchen in her coat, with the keys of the care home beside her bowl. She ran the home, down in the Port, and was at her desk there by seven.

'You shouted at three.'

'I was asleep.'

'You were counting. You got to nineteen.' Coralie rinsed the bowl and stood it on the rack. 'Madame le maire has her committee at ten. She might sleep tonight.'

Madame le maire was what the village called her to her face, and had been since April 2015. Once she had left a room it said Lesca. Coralie had used her first name for five years, and now used the title when she wanted to be heard.

She took the keys and went out. The windscreen of her car was white, and she scraped a patch of it clear with the edge of a card and drove off looking through the patch. Lesca stood at the sink and opened and closed her right hand under the hot tap until the fingers answered at once.

* * *

The cold had come with the east wind and stayed two weeks. At twenty to eight the verges were stiff with frost, and Lesca left her car by the church, because the mayor's parking space had a stone in it.

The stone was a slab of grey granite as high as her shoulder, set in front of the mairie door in the white rectangle where Guy Larrère had parked for seven years. Two lines were cut into it in capitals. The top line read JEAN CASTAGNET. The line under it read MAYLIS LESCA. Frost had settled in the foot of each letter.

A man who had sent forty letters would be alive to send the forty-first. She stood in front of the top line with her keys in her hand, and it read as it had read at the unveiling and at eleven wreaths since. She had forty in her head all the same, with the box file they stood in, and the cupboard.

A loaded lorry came up from the Port and changed down at the church. It crossed the square at walking pace, and the mairie's shutters hummed in their frames until it had gone. She unlocked the door.

* * *

The commemoration committee met at ten in the council room, nine members at the table and the secretary at the end of it with the register. The sheet in front of each of them was headed Twelfth commemoration of the flood of 12 December 2014. Under the heading it gave Saturday 12 December, the prefect at six, the wreath laid by Madame Castagnet, the mayor's address and, at ten, the bull. The talk in the room stopped when Lesca came in. It started again, lower, once she had sat down and uncapped her pen.

The meeting opened as each of its meetings opened, with a minute's silence. They stood. Lesca kept the minute on the clock over the door. Maïté Castagnet stood across the table from her with both hands on the back of her chair. She was his widow and had sat on the committee since its first year. At forty seconds a lorry came up from the lake and changed down under the window, and the water jug rang against the glasses. Twenty-eight loads a day went through the square under the lease the commune had signed in 2019. The lease paid four hundred thousand euros a year. Lesca had worked out once that each load passing the stone was worth fifty-seven euros to Peyrebarthe. At sixty seconds she said 'Thank you', and nine chairs scraped.

There was one sum to divide. The commune's festivities line for the coming year was €9,600, and it had two events to pay for. She proposed nine thousand for the firework bull on the night of the twelfth, with the man from Mont-de-Marsan who was hired to light it, and six hundred for the fête in July.

Marie-Jo Labat ran the July fête. She had been Lesca's first deputy until 2018, and since 2018 she had spoken to her through the chair, with her eyes on the wall above it.

'Through the chair. Six hundred euros hires one band for one evening.'

'Last December brought six thousand people, and the bar returned eleven thousand euros to the commune,' Lesca said. 'July brought three hundred and cost four thousand.'

'It's a fête, Madame le maire. It isn't there to return.'

'It's there to be come to.'

Marie-Jo looked down from the wall, at her, for the time it takes to read a figure. Then she looked at her pad.

Guy Larrère lifted a finger. He grew maize, and he had been mayor until April 2015. The secretary set a white kitchen timer on the table and turned the dial to three. No member of any body of the commune had spoken for longer than that since June 2015, under the second deliberation Lesca had signed.

'I'll be brief,' he said.

The river was forecast high for the twelfth, he said, and the committee ought to understand its gates before it stood six thousand people in the Port. He explained them to her across the table. The flaps shut themselves on a rising river and opened on a falling one. He held one hand upright and let it drop against the other palm, twice, to show her how a flap seats in its frame. Anyone who had known only the old winch, he said, might suppose a gate wanted somebody standing by it. She had turned the old winch for seven years, on his telephone calls.

The timer rang while he was in the middle of the word hydraulic. He finished the word, laid the hand flat on the table and kept his eyes on the timer until the secretary had stopped it.

'Thank you, Monsieur Larrère. Those in favour.'

Six hands went up, and hers was one. Marie-Jo and Larrère voted against. Maïté Castagnet kept her hands on the bag in her lap, as she had at eleven such votes, and the secretary wrote one abstention. Marie-Jo wrote 600 on her pad and underlined it twice.

Maïté was waiting in the corridor afterwards, by the coat pegs, with her coat already buttoned.

'I've a tile gone over the back bedroom. It went in the wind on Sunday.'

'I'll come at four.'

* * *

When the secretary had gone to lunch, Lesca opened the cupboard behind her desk and took down the box file with CASTAGNET on the spine. She counted the envelopes out onto the desk. He had numbered each one in the top left corner in his own hand, No. 1 to No. 12. She counted them a second time, back into the file, and there were twelve.

Eleven had a copy of the reply stapled to them, each signed G. Larrère. The twelfth had nothing stapled to it. It bore the mairie's stamp, received 9 December 2014. In it he asked that the sluice be kept shut until the end of January, so that the low barthe would hold its water for the duck.

She put it back behind the eleventh. Then she ran her thumb along the bottom of the file, behind the envelopes, where twenty-eight more would have stood, and brought it out with dust on it.

* * *

The Castagnet house was the last on its lane, four hundred metres from the mairie, where the terrace dropped towards the Port. Lesca was there at four in her old boots. The ladder hung where it had hung in his time, on two hooks under the lean-to. She lifted it down and set it against the eaves at the back.

Maïté held it. She put one shoe on the bottom rung and a hand on each rail.

'The prefect's office telephoned,' she said. 'They'd like a few words from the widow this year.'

'Will you?'

'I'd ask what I ask. Who was meant to go round the tonnes that night.'

'Keep your foot on that rung.'

Maïté kept it there. 'He went up in his socks,' she said. 'He said boots broke more tiles than the wind.'

Lesca went up in her boots. The ladder was aluminium and so cold that she felt each rung through the leather of her soles and each rail through her palms. At the eaves she stepped across onto the roof, and a tile shifted under her and settled.

The one that had gone had slid two courses and lodged above the gutter. She took it in her right hand and went up the slope on the overlaps, bent, with her left hand flat on the roof. It was a half-round clay tile, rough with lichen and cold from the wind. It lay across the scar in her palm, a white seam from the heel of the hand to the first finger, which the old winch had put there. The gap was in the top course. She reached the ridge and stood up to get her footing.

From the ridge she could see over the roofs of the Port to the dyke. Past the dyke, where in the night ponies had grazed in a hand of water, the lake ran flat to the far trees, with the dredger moored in the middle of it and the gravel heaped grey along the near shore.
