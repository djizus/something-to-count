# 2

At nine minutes to three on the morning of Tuesday 24 November, the mayor of Peyrebarthe was awake in her own bed and could not move.

Lesca did not try her hands. She had tried them in the winter of 2014 and learned what came of it. Her eyes turned, and they gave her the ceiling, the paper lampshade, the top of the wardrobe and, on the chest of drawers, the red figures of the clock radio, which read 02:51. They had read 02:51 on most nights for twelve years. The tiles were dry and made no sound.

There was a weight on her breastbone.

It had no hands and no face, and she no longer looked for either. Its chill came through the blanket. It smelled of cold steel and of old tobacco. She had a procedure for it, which was to leave it alone. Pushed, it pressed back exactly as hard. Left alone, it bore down evenly until she went under, which took about four minutes, and she woke at six having slept. She had kept to the procedure for twelve winters and had not mentioned it to anyone.

That night she did not go under. She had brought an afternoon up with her.

She had been at the old sluice gate with the tide about to turn. Two hands were on the handle of the hand winch, in the cuffs of a yellow waterproof, and the waterproof was the one with COMMUNE DE PEYREBARTHE across the back that she had handed in with her keys in March 2015. The hands counted turns by the click of the pawl, as she used to. At the nineteenth the handle stopped. A shoulder went under it and pushed. The handle gave, the platform rail came in under the right arm, and a rib went. Beyond the rail her mother's ponies were grazing the low meadows with their tails to the river.

She remembered it the way she remembered the code of the mairie alarm, and it could not be hers. The commune had put a motor on the gate in 2016 and sold the hand winch by weight to a dealer in Dax for sixty-two euros, by a deliberation she had signed. She had turned that winch for seven years before then, and it had never put her into the rail. It had opened her right palm once, in December 2014, and the palm had healed with a ridge across it that she felt each time she closed her hand on a pen. Whoever was in the waterproof had broken a rib. Lesca had all of hers.

She also knew, lying there, that Jeannot Castagnet had sent the mairie forty registered letters. She knew it as a figure, the way she knew that the commune had nine hundred inhabitants. She knew that twenty-six of the forty concerned the level of the water in the low meadows.

Jeannot Castagnet had sent the mairie twelve. He had been dead for twelve years less nineteen days.

For three minutes she had both figures. She signed the commune's accounts and was used to columns that agreed. These two did not, and she could strike out neither. Forty letters wanted a man alive to post them. That frightened her as the weight had not for years, and at 02:54 she went against her procedure and pushed. The weight pressed back. She pushed again, a shout came out of her, and the weight was gone.

The landing light came on. Coralie came in from the room across the landing with her hair flat on one side. She had run the care home in the Port for some years and no longer took pulses for a living, but she sat on the edge of the bed, found Lesca's wrist and laid two fingers on the inside of it, watching the clock.

'Ninety-six,' Coralie said. 'You shouted.'

'What did I shout?'

'Nineteen.'

'Nineteen what?'

'You didn't say. You said it twice.' She kept the wrist. 'Will you sleep?'

'Yes.'

Coralie gave the wrist back and went out, leaving both doors open. Lesca put her right hand to her right side, under the arm, and pressed. Nothing hurt. She did not sleep. At five the light in her mother's kitchen came on across the yard and showed on the ceiling, and at six she got up.

Coralie left for the home at seven. On the kitchen table she left a saucer with one of the chemist's sleeping tablets on it, as she did after a night with shouting in it. Lesca put the tablet back in its tube, which was nearly full, and stood the saucer in the sink.

* * *

Tuesday was dry, with a white frost.

She left her car by the church at ten past eight and crossed the square on foot, past the green bench with the slat missing. The commune's outdoor man had hung the Christmas lights in the plane trees the week before, nine strings, and all nine were lit. The mayor of Peyrebarthe had no parking space. The one in front of the mairie door had gone to the stone in 2015.

The stone was grey granite, shoulder-high, and faced the square. The council of 2015 had sat three times on the wording. A verb was proposed at each sitting and lost at each, and what carried in the end, by eight votes to six, was two names. She had left the room for all three votes, as a councillor with an interest must.

On other mornings she passed behind it. That Tuesday she went round to the front. The top line was JEAN CASTAGNET. Under it, in the same capitals, was MAYLIS LESCA, and frost lay in the foot of each letter. Apart from the stone, the one person in the village who still used the first of those names was her mother.

She read the top line through to the T, and went in.

* * *

The commemoration committee met at ten in the council room, as it had on the last Tuesday of November since 2015. Seven of its nine members were present, with the mayor in the chair.

It opened with a minute's silence for Jean Castagnet. The members stood behind their chairs. Marie-Jo Labat, president of the fête committee, set a white kitchen timer to one minute and stood it on the table, and the room listened to it. Maïté Castagnet, his widow, held her handbag in front of her in both hands and looked at the radiator. For the length of the minute Lesca had forty letters again, and a man to post them. The timer rang, and she had twelve.

Correspondence came first. The prefecture confirmed that the prefect would attend on Saturday 12 December and would speak. Its letter began 'Monsieur le Maire', as its letters had for eleven years, and she read it out as written. The commune of Hontaube asked, for the second time, to open talks on joining Peyrebarthe, and was referred to the full council.

The second item was the order of the ceremony. The prefect would speak first, and the mayor after him for eight minutes, from a typed text. Since May 2015 no member of the council or of its committees could speak for more than three minutes on any item. The rule applied to all members and had been needed for one, and it did not extend to the ceremony. Guy Larrère had raised that point in 2016, 2019 and 2023. He did not raise it again.

The third item was the wreath. Maïté Castagnet would lay it, as at each commemoration. She was asked whether it should be white chrysanthemums again and said that it should.

The fourth item was the firework bull. It was to be hired with its operator from a firm at Mont-de-Marsan and lit on the dyke at nine in the evening, for nine thousand euros. Six thousand people had come the year before. Larrère asked for the floor. He was president of the landowners' association, which owned the gates, and he had been mayor until April 2015. Marie-Jo turned the timer to three and stood it in front of him.

'I'll be brief,' Larrère said.

He was three minutes. He gave the committee the association's upkeep of the gates since 1962 and the tonnage of maize grown behind them, and he had reached the tidal coefficient of 13 December 2014 when the timer rang. He finished the word and sat down. He had not come to what he was asking for, and the committee voted without it. The bull was carried by six votes to one.

'Against,' Marie-Jo said. 'And I'd like it minuted.'

'It's minuted every year,' Larrère said.

'Then it's no trouble.'

The fifth item was the subsidy to the fête committee for the fête in July, at which Marie-Jo had counted two hundred and ten people that summer. Six hundred euros were proposed, as in each year since 2018, and carried by six votes, the president of the fête committee not taking part. Marie-Jo wrote the figure on her pad and drew a line under it.

Lesca closed the meeting at twenty past eleven and signed the attendance sheet. She had worked the signature up on the back of an agenda in April 2015, when it became clear how many times a day a mayor signs, and it had a flourish under it.

Maïté Castagnet waited until the others had their coats on.

'There's a tile gone over the back bedroom,' she said. 'It's coming in on the wardrobe.'

'I'll come at two.'

'And the gutter, while you've the ladder out.'

It was not an item and was not minuted.

* * *

When the council room was empty Lesca went to the grey cabinet in the secretary's office and took out the file marked CASTAGNET J. She had not opened it since 2015.

It held registered letters, each numbered by the sender in the top left corner, with the acknowledgement card stapled to it. Each card was signed M. Lesca, with a tall L and no flourish, by the commune's outdoor employee, who signed for the post when the mairie was shut. Seven of the letters concerned the level of the water in the low meadows. All but the last had a reply clipped behind, signed by Larrère.

The last was No. 12. The mairie had received it on Tuesday 9 December 2014. It reported less than a hand of water at the sender's tonne on the night of the sixth, and required the mairie to state what it meant to do. The mairie had two months in which to answer, and the sender drowned on the fourth day.

She counted the letters from the front and then from the back, and made twelve both times. Then she looked behind the file, and in the drawer below, where the cemetery plots were kept. There were no others. The file was a centimetre thick. She put it back, shut the drawer and went home for the ladder.

* * *

The Castagnet house stood four hundred metres from the mairie, on the edge of the terrace above the Port. The commune employed a man for its outdoor work, and he did not do the Castagnet roof. Lesca had done it since January 2015, with her own ladder and with tiles from the stack behind her mother's barn, and no deliberation covered it.

Maïté was in the yard at two in her black coat. She took the foot of the ladder in both hands before Lesca had it against the wall.

'Third row from the top,' she said. 'On the chimney side.'

Lesca went up. She was at the eaves when Maïté spoke again, to the ladder.

'Nine thousand euros. For a bull.'

'Six thousand people came last year.'

'He'd have sent the mairie a registered letter about it.'

Lesca stopped with one hand on the gutter. Below her Maïté shifted her grip on the ladder and said nothing more, and after about as long as the committee's silence Lesca went on up.

The frost had gone from the south slope. She had two tiles in a feed sack over her shoulder, and she crossed the slope on her knees. The tile had slid a hand's width out of its row and lay loose on the one below. She pulled a fistful of plane leaves out of the gutter on her way past, since she had been asked, and dropped them into the yard.

'That's not the gutter done,' Maïté said.

'No. After the twelfth.'

She got astride the ridge, worked the slipped tile out from under its neighbours, and straightened up with it in her hand to see how the row ran.

From the ridge she had the roofs of the Port, then the grass bank of the dyke, then the low meadows. There were no low meadows. Since 2019 there had been a lake, forty hectares of it so far, dug by a gravel company to which the commune had let the common grazing for thirty years. The lease brought in four hundred thousand euros a year, and nine thousand of it had gone that morning on a bull. The lease carried her signature, with the flourish. A dredger was moored out in the middle, and a conveyor ran from it to the grey heaps on the far shore. Ice had formed along the near edge.

In the night there had been grass there, and nine ponies on it with their tails to the river. She sat on Maïté Castagnet's ridge with the tile in her hand and looked at the water where they had been grazing.
