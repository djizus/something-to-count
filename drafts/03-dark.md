# 3

It came again at nine minutes to three on the Wednesday. Maylis had lain awake for it since midnight with her hands outside the blanket, opening and closing them, and had gone under without knowing it. Then the clock read 02:51 and her hands lay where they had stopped.

She did not push. The rib had taught her that on Tuesday. She let her breath out and gave the weight nothing to press against, and it sat on the bone at its thirty-five kilos and bore down no harder. Coralie breathed beside her. Rain ran in the gutter. Maylis kept her eyes on the red figures for as long as she could hold them open, and they read 02:53 when she lost them.

At six the alarm went, and she had a sash.

She remembered wearing it. It lay over her right shoulder and crossed to her left hip, blue, white and red, with a gold fringe that knocked against her leg, and it was heavier than it looked on Larrère each 11 November. She was standing in the square with the grey stone at her back. The square was full. People stood from the mairie steps to the church wall and along the benches, in coats, with their breath showing under the lamps, and they had their faces turned to her. A child sat on a man's shoulders by the pump. A microphone stood on a stand in front of her, and the square was quiet enough for her to hear the fringe move. They were waiting for her to begin. The tarmac was dry, and she had on a pair of black shoes she did not own.

She sat up by stages with her forearm against her side. Peyrebarthe had had one mayor since she was twenty-five, and she had raked the leaves out of his parking space the day before.

Coralie was doing up her tunic at the foot of the bed.

'You were awake at three. I felt you.'

'I was on my back. Like you said.'

'That isn't what I asked.'

She left at half past six. Two paracetamol lay on the shopping pad on the kitchen table, and under them she had written Doctor in Dax, and Thursday and Friday with a box beside each. Maylis swallowed the tablets and stood her bowl on the pad.

* * *

Kévin Labat was in the square at eight, parked by the church with his lights off and the rain running down his windscreen. He was Marie-Jo's son and thirty. He got out when the commune's tractor came round the corner with the eight boxes in its bucket.

'My mother says you've done a rib, and you've nine strings to hang by Saturday.'

'Eight. One's dead.'

'Then you've a man for the day.'

'The commune won't pay you.'

'I know what the commune pays.'

He opened the back doors of his van for his gloves. Three blue cool boxes were strapped along the side, each with an air pump ticking on its lid. It was the season. From November to March the glass eels came up the Adour on the night tides, each one the length of a finger and as clear as water, and a kilo of them fetched four hundred euros from a man in Irun. He netted them at the sluice gate. She held its key. She turned her back on the open doors and checked the pins on the loader arms, which did not need checking.

'You could look,' he said. 'Four winters, and you never have.'

'I've lights to hang.'

'I offered you two hundred a night to leave the padlock off.' He pulled the gloves on. 'You said no. I waited for the gendarmes and they didn't come, and you never said a word to me after. So I don't know what you want. I'm getting in your bucket.'

He put his phone on the tractor's dashboard, out of the rain, and climbed in among the boxes.

Nine plane trees stood round the square, and her father had screwed a hook into each at the height of a raised bucket. He had cut each string to its span and numbered the boxes. She could not lift her right arm above the shoulder, so Kévin went up and she stayed in the seat, working the loader lever with her right hand held low. She raised him to the first hook by the church. He tied on the end of string one and she drove him at walking pace to the second tree, paying the cable out over the tarmac, with the rain coming straight down on him and into the open boxes.

At nine the phone buzzed against the dashboard. Up in the bucket he let go of the cable and held both hands in front of his face. He touched his right forefinger to each finger of his left hand and then did the other hand, and his lips moved. Then he picked the cable up again.

At ten the church clock struck and the phone buzzed inside the same minute. She owed the rib ten breaths on the hour, and she took them with her hand off the lever while he counted over her head. The seventh breath stopped short. She took it again.

'What is it you count?' she said, when he was down for string four.

'Fingers.'

'You know how many you've got.'

'Awake I do.' He turned his hands over in the rain. 'In a dream they come out wrong. Six, or four, or they won't hold still to be counted. I'm in the van from eleven till the tide's done, and I sleep in pieces. The phone goes on the hour and I count. Do it enough awake and you start doing it asleep. The night it comes out wrong you know you're dreaming, and then you can do as you like.'

'And what would you do?'

'Go up. Off the roof of the van and over the river.' He looked at the hands a moment longer and put them back in the gloves. 'It's been five since September.'

She did not ask him anything else. From the seat she could see the mayor's parking space, with Larrère's car in it and the oil stain under the car. In the night she had stood on that spot and the square had waited for her, and she had known it for the square by the green bench with the slat missing. She had not counted anything.

String three should have gone from the third tree to the fourth, across the front of the mairie. Its box was in the shed. They hung four, five and six before noon, and the gap stayed over the mayor's space, forty metres of nothing between two trees.

'He'll park under that,' Kévin said.

'I know where he parks.'

He went to his van to sleep. She stayed in the tractor with the rain on the roof and found the string on her phone, forty metres, warm white, for outdoor use, at €74.90 with delivery on Friday. The mairie could have ordered it. That took a voucher signed by the mayor, and the mairie was shut until Friday at two, and the lights were for Saturday. She took her own bank card out of her jacket and typed the number in with her left thumb. For the delivery address she gave the mairie. In the box for the name she put M. Lesca.

* * *

The church roof had let in water for six winters. Seven buckets stood in the nave where the drips fell, two of them on pews, and in a wet week she emptied them at midday through the side door into the drain. With one arm it was seven journeys.

She was on the second when Père Honoré came in from the porch with his Mass kit in a sports bag. He was from Togo and had fourteen churches. He had said Mass at Hontaube at eleven and had a burial at three, and he had stopped to see what the roof had done since Sunday. He set the bag on a dry pew and looked at how she carried the bucket.

'Which side?'

'The right.'

He took the full ones two at a time, without a word, and walked them to the side door, and she heard them go into the drain. He came back and set each on its mark. The marks were crosses of yellow tape she had stuck to the floor in 2021.

'The estimate is forty-one thousand,' he said. 'Monsieur Larrère tells me it is for Hontaube to decide, in January.' He looked up at the stain over the third pew. 'There were nine on Sunday. Two of them sat under that.'

She carried the last bucket out through the porch, since it stood nearest. A marble tablet was let into the porch wall beside the door. She had dusted it every week for fifteen years and had not read it in ten. THIS PORCH WAS RESTORED IN 2011, it said, GUY LARRÈRE BEING MAYOR. The letters were cut as deep as a fingernail and painted gold.

'Father. When was that cut?'

'Before my time.' He came and stood beside her with his bag. 'They cut them for the blessing, as a rule. The mayor likes to be there to pull the cloth off.'

'So he was in office. With his name up.'

'Mayors and donors.' He was looking at the roof again. 'I would cut anyone's name for a roof.'

He went out to his car at a run with the bag over his head. She stood in the porch with the bucket and read the tablet a second time. Guy Larrère was forty metres away behind the mairie's lit window, signing something. Whoever wore that sash had her name on a stone as he had his on a wall, and she was no more dead than he was.

She emptied the bucket over the step and put it back on its cross.

* * *

Her mother had the bales stacked by the yard gate at half past one and was sitting in her car beside them with the engine running. Maylis loaded six into the boot and the back seat, left-handed. Her mother watched her do it over her shoulder, through the gap between the seats.

They drove down through the Port and over the dyke. The low meadows lay in a hand of water, as Jeannot Castagnet wanted them, and the ponies stood on the one dry rise by the fence with their rumps to the rain. There were eleven of them, small and dark, of the breed the meadows had been grazed with before the maize. Her mother counted them aloud from the car before she got out.

Maylis cut the strings and forked the hay over the wire with the fork held short in her left hand. Each forkful pulled at the rib from the wrong side, and she took them small. The ponies came in on the hay and shouldered one another. Her mother stood at the fence in her coat with a hand on the top wire.

'You were sitting in the dark on Tuesday. At three, with no light on.'

'I had a drink of water.'

'For two hours.' Her mother pushed a pony's nose off the wire. 'Your father did two ribs on that winch and mowed the same week. I've stopped asking what goes on in that house. I only say what I see from my kitchen.'

'I know you do.'

'Hontaube won't let me keep them here. Larrère as good as said so at the cemetery.' She counted the ponies again, as far as eleven. 'Get the last bale. They've trodden half of that into the water.'

* * *

She was back in the seat at three. At three and again at four the phone went on the dashboard. Each time he counted, and each time she took her ten breaths and watched his hands in place of the clock. At four he lost his place at the second hand and began again from the thumb.

By ten to five the light was going, and seven strings were up. Kévin tied the far end of string nine to the church tree with the bucket at full height and the tractor's work lamp on him. Maylis walked across to the box on the mairie wall and threw the switch.

Eight strings came on round the square. They ran from tree to tree at the height of the first-floor windows, and the wet tarmac took the light from them. Between the third tree and the fourth there was nothing. Larrère's car stood under the gap with rain on its roof.

'It's coming Friday,' she said, back at the tractor.

'Who's paying?'

'It's paid.'

He looked down at her from the bucket and then at the gap. He did not say what he thought of that. She climbed into the seat and took hold of the lever to bring him down.

The church clock began to strike five. The phone buzzed on the dashboard. In the bucket Kévin opened his hands under the lights. In the seat she let go of the lever, held her own up in front of the windscreen and counted them, the left and then the right.
