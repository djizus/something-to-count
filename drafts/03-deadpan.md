# 3

The weight came back on Wednesday at the same minute, and this time Maylis let it lie.

On Tuesday she had pushed and the rib had stopped her, so on Wednesday she did not push. She lay under thirty-five kilos of cold with her eyes on the clock radio, which read 02:51 and then 02:52, and with Coralie asleep against the wall. The weight did not press harder for being left alone. It bore down evenly, and some time after 02:53 she was asleep.

At six the alarm went and she had a second thing in her head.

It was a sash. It lay across her from the right shoulder to the left hip, three bands of silk in blue, white and red, with gold tassels that knocked against her hip when she moved. She was standing in front of the mairie door with it on. The stone was there too, a few paces off, and she had its back, which was blank. Beyond the stone the square was full. There were more people in it than lived in Peyrebarthe, standing between the plane trees as far back as the church, and they had stopped talking. They were turned her way. They were waiting for her to begin.

She knew the sash. Guy Larrère wore it on 11 November, on 14 July and at weddings. She had been asked to iron it once, in 2016, when the secretary was on leave. She did not know what it was doing on her, and she remembered the pull of it on her shoulder the way she remembered the stone.

Coralie was on lates and still asleep. Maylis left her there and went out to the shed in the rain.

* * *

Wednesday was Saint Catherine's, by which date her father had always had the lights up. By half past eight Maylis had the commune's tractor at the first plane tree, with eight boxes in the loader bucket. The ninth was on the shed floor with its string inside. She had changed its fuse on Tuesday and tried it in two sockets, and it had stayed dark in both.

Her father's method wanted one person at the levers and another in the bucket, and the commune employed one person. He had got round it by raising the bucket, leaving the engine running and climbing out along the loader arm, a practice on which the commune's insurer had not been consulted. With the rib she could not have climbed onto the tractor's bonnet.

Kévin Labat had been waiting at the shed door when she unlocked it at eight. Nobody had asked him. He had turned up for the lights in 2023, 2024 and 2025 in the same way.

His van stood by the church. In the back of it were two blue cool boxes and a small pump that ran off the battery and could be heard from the pavement. Maylis went round the back of the van twice that morning to reach the socket on the church wall, and did not look in. She had not looked in since December 2022.

He was Marie-Jo's son. On winter nights glass eels came up the Adour on the tide, each the length of a matchstick, and fetched four hundred euros a kilo over the border at Irun. They were netted where the water narrowed, which was at the gates, and Maylis kept the key to one. In December 2022 Kévin had got into the commune's van beside her and counted two hundred euros in twenties onto the dashboard. That was for one night with the platform left unlocked, he said, and there were forty such nights in a winter. She pushed the notes back across to him. She had not told the mayor or the gendarmes or the men in green from the biodiversity office, and she had not gone down to the gate after dark since, and for this she was paid nothing. It had puzzled Kévin for three winters. He had settled it by turning up.

He went up in the bucket with string number one over his shoulder, and she worked the levers left-handed with her right elbow against her side. Her father had numbered the trees as he had the boxes, from the church round to the café. Kévin clipped each string along loops of fencing wire that her father had twisted onto the branches in 1998.

At nine the church clock struck and a phone buzzed on the tractor's dashboard, where Kévin had left it out of the wet. Maylis owed the rib ten breaths and began on them. Up in the bucket Kévin let go of the string. He held his left hand in front of his face and touched each finger of it with his right forefinger, moving his lips. Then he took up the string again.

At ten he did the same. She was on her sixth breath.

'What are you counting?' she called up.

'Fingers.'

'How many have you got?'

'Five.' He leaned on the rim of the bucket. 'It's an app. It goes off every hour and you check. Five, and you're awake.'

'And if it isn't five?'

'Then you're asleep and you've caught it. In a dream the hand comes out wrong. Six, or seven, or they won't keep still. Or you read something twice. Writing won't hold in a dream, it's changed when you look back. You look and you know. And once you know you're in one, you can do what you like in it.'

'What do you like?'

'I'd fly,' Kévin said. He was four metres up in a loader bucket with string number four round his neck and rain running off his cap.

'Have you?'

'Not properly. In September I got up level with the gutter of the hall. Then I looked down and woke up.'

'And you do this at night. Down there.'

'There's four hours between tides,' he said. 'It passes the time.'

She did not ask what else passed in the four hours. She lowered him to the next branch.

'When you know you're in one,' she said. 'Is it you in it? Your own hand?'

He looked at the hand. 'Whose would it be?'

From the tractor seat she had the mairie door in front of her for most of the morning. It stayed shut, with the card of its hours in it. Four people crossed the square, and none of them looked up. By half past eleven eight trees were strung. The third, which stood in front of the mairie beside the mayor's parking space, had nothing in it but its loops of wire.

* * *

The mairie was shut on Wednesdays. A purchase by the commune required an order form signed by the mayor, who signed on Tuesdays and Fridays, and the commune's supplier delivered within ten working days. The loto was on Saturday.

At noon Maylis sat at the kitchen table with Coralie's laptop and her own bank card. She ordered one string of two hundred warm-white lamps, twenty metres, for outdoor use, at €38.90, with delivery by Friday for nine euros more. She gave the mairie as the address.

Coralie was ironing her tunic for the late shift. She stood the iron on its heel, came round the table and read the screen over Maylis's shoulder.

'That's the commune's to buy.'

'The mairie's shut till Friday.'

'Forty-seven ninety. On your card.' Coralie went on reading. 'You haven't bought a thing since the boots.'

'It isn't for me.'

'No. What do you want it for, then?'

'There are nine trees,' Maylis said.

Coralie looked at her for longer than the answer needed. Then she laid two fingers under Maylis's right arm, on the place.

'Ten an hour?'

'Ten an hour.'

'And who's doing the lifting?'

'Kévin Labat's in the bucket.'

'Kévin Labat,' Coralie said, and took her fingers away. 'He just turned up, I suppose.'

'He turns up.'

The shopping pad lay by the laptop. It said Tonight, and omelette, and what's left of the soup, with a box beside each. Maylis wrote the order number underneath and left the boxes as they were.

* * *

The church had been built before 1905 and so belonged to the commune, roof and all, and the buckets under the roof were the commune's too. There were seven of them, numbered on the side in marker, standing in the nave wherever the water came through. In a wet week Maylis emptied them on Monday, Wednesday and Friday.

Père Honoré was in the porch at two, shaking out an umbrella. He had fourteen churches and a Clio. He came from Togo, where the rain, he said, arrived, did its work in an hour and left, and he had driven over from Hontaube between a funeral and a catechism class to see how the buckets stood. He took number four himself, which was the full one, and carried it down the aisle in both arms without spilling it.

'Nine on Sunday,' he said. 'I counted twice. Ten, with me.'

'Seven buckets.'

'It was five in October.'

'The roof's on the council's agenda. Under any other business.'

'Since when?'

'Since 2018.'

He set number four down on the porch step and watched her take it up by the handle, left-handed.

'You are carrying on one side.'

'A rib.'

'I will put it with the roof,' he said, and she understood that both were now prayed for. 'I am calling the numbers on Saturday, at the loto. Madame Labat asked me.'

'She asked you?'

'She told me the time,' said Père Honoré.

Maylis tipped the bucket into the drain. Above the drain a marble tablet was let into the porch wall. She had passed it with a bucket three times a week in every wet week for eighteen years, and that afternoon she read it. It recorded in gilt capitals that the bell of the church had been re-hung in 2009, GUY LARRÈRE BEING MAYOR.

Guy Larrère had told her through his car window the evening before that the lights were for Saturday. A name could be cut in capitals in a public place, then, and the owner of it go on driving round the square giving instructions. She had dug seven graves that year and had read the stone as an eighth. With the sash laid across it, it read another way. A dead woman is not put into a sash and stood in front of the mairie with the village waiting for her to speak.

That left the date. A commune's employee could not sit on its council, and from 1 January there would be no mayor of Peyrebarthe to hang a sash on. So it was not to come, and it had not been, and she remembered it.

'Is there something wrong with it?' Père Honoré said. He was looking at the tablet as well.

'No. It's spelt right.'

She carried the bucket back in and stood it under its drip.

* * *

Her mother's ponies were on the low meadows below the dyke. They were Landais ponies, bay, and in a wet November they wanted hay. Arlette was seventy and no longer threw bales. At half past four she came down in the passenger seat of the commune's van, with two bales in the back, to see it done.

The ponies had heard the van on the dyke road. They were already coming across the meadow when Maylis got out, the whole herd, heads up, and they stopped along the fence and waited for her. Out beyond them the hump of Jeannot Castagnet's tonne stood in mud.

She cut the twine and forked the hay over the wire with her left arm.

'You're forking with one hand,' Arlette said through the window.

'I did a rib.'

'Marie-Jo told me. On that winch.' She watched another forkful go over. 'Your father forked with a hernia from 1996. I'm not asking how you came to do it. I've stopped asking you things.'

'I know. You said so on Sunday.'

'A Labat in the commune's bucket,' Arlette said. 'And one tree with nothing in it. Your father had nine trees lit.'

'He had nine strings.'

'Guy says Hontaube has two men of its own.'

'He told me.'

'He says you're not to worry about it.'

'He told me that as well.'

'Then don't,' Arlette said.

The old mare took hay off the fork before it reached the ground. Arlette counted the herd through the windscreen, as she did each evening, and wound her window up.

* * *

At half past five Maylis unlocked the box on the mairie wall and threw the switch. Eight plane trees came on round the square. The third stood dark between the second and the fourth.

Kévin had waited to see it. He sat on the mudguard seat of the tractor with the door open and his phone on the dashboard, and she climbed up beside him out of the rain.

'It looks like a tooth out,' he said.

'There's one coming on Friday.'

'The commune's bought a string?'

'No.'

He looked at the dark tree. Then he took a fifty-euro note from inside his jacket and laid it on the dashboard between them, next to the phone.

She pushed it back across to him with one finger.

'It's fifty euros, Maylis. It's for a tree.'

'It's forty-seven ninety,' she said. 'And it's paid.'

He put the note away. The church clock struck six, and the phone buzzed on the dashboard a second behind it. Kévin held his left hand up against the windscreen, where the lights were, and began to touch off the fingers. Maylis owed the rib ten breaths. She looked down at her own hand on the wheel and counted that first, starting with the thumb.
