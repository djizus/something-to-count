# 4

On Thursday at eight two people were waiting for the mayor in her mother's kitchen.

The kitchen was across the yard from her own door, twelve metres of frozen gravel, and its light had been on since five. Arlette Lesca had not set foot in the mairie since a morning in October 2019, when a lorry backed into that yard and took her ponies. Peyrebarthe had learned within the year that whatever it had to say about the lake could be said at her table. The table was open seven days and had coffee on it. When the mayor came across to answer, she answered in front of her mother.

The man had the chair by the range. He was from the Port and kept half a hectare of kiwis behind his house, watered from a well. The woman lived on the lane the lorries took down to the weighbridge. Each had a bowl in front of them, and neither got up.

'Madame le maire,' the man said, and turned to Arlette. 'Tell her it's down a metre forty since Easter.'

'She can hear you. Sit down, Maylis. Have you eaten?'

'No.'

Arlette put the loaf in front of her, and the knife. Lesca opened her notebook beside it. The notebook was blue, with squared pages and COMMUNE DE PEYREBARTHE stamped on the cover. The mairie bought them by the dozen each September, and she filled five a year with what people told her.

She wrote the well down, with the figure and the month. 'The lease lets them dig to twelve metres,' she said. 'It also makes them keep three gauges in the ground, and connect any house whose well fails, at their cost. I'll have the gauge readings by Tuesday. If your metre forty is in them, they lay you a pipe and pay for what the orchard drinks.'

'I don't want their water. I want mine.' He said it to Arlette.

'Yours is under forty hectares of lake,' Lesca said. 'The lake pays for the third class at the school and for the surgery. I can get you a pipe.'

He looked into his bowl. The woman had unfolded a page torn from the post office calendar and laid it face down. She had written nine dates on the back in pencil, with a time against each, and the earliest was 06:38.

'Seven o'clock, the paper says. They come past my bedroom at twenty to.'

'May I keep that?'

'It's the only one I've got.'

Lesca copied the nine lines into the notebook in a column and pushed the page back across the table. 'The weighbridge prints a ticket for each load, with the minute on it. I'll ask for those nine mornings. Each ticket before seven costs the company two hundred euros.'

'And the dust? I've had sheets out since Monday that I can't bring in.'

'There's no clause for dust. I'll tell them to cover the loads.'

'You could tell them to stop.'

'Until 2049 I can't.'

The woman folded the calendar page in four and put it in her coat pocket.

'You could have come to the mairie with this,' Lesca said, to both of them. 'Tuesday or Friday, two till six.'

'Your mother's is open,' the woman said.

They left together. On the step the woman turned to Arlette and did not lower her voice. 'She was always hard. At school she was.'

'Mind the yard,' Arlette said. 'It's iced.'

She shut the door and carried the two bowls to the sink. Seven halters hung on nails beside the door, each under a name written on a strip of plaster, and the leather of them was dry and grey. Through the window over the sink Lesca could see the stable door, shut, and the frost lying whole on the yard where nothing had walked.

'He wants his water,' Arlette said, with her back turned. 'I wanted my grass. You've a pipe for him.'

'You had the grazing money.'

'It's in the dresser drawer, in the envelope it came in. Your signature's on it, with the tail.' She rinsed a bowl and stood it on the rack. 'I've not set foot up there and I've not spent a euro of it. That's all I've to say about the lake.'

She rinsed the second bowl.

'Coralie tells me you shout. I've stopped asking what goes on over there. I'm telling you what I'm told. Numbers, she says.'

Lesca had kept it from everyone for twelve winters. She had her pen in her hand and the notebook open at the nine times, and she asked it as she would have asked the prefecture for the name of a form.

'Something sits on my chest at night. I'm awake, and I can't move anything but my eyes. What did the old ones call that?'

Arlette turned off the tap. 'How long?'

'Since the flood.'

'On your back?'

'I go to sleep on my side. I wake on my back.'

'The sarramauca.' She said the word in the old speech of the meadows, and then put it into French for her daughter. 'The chest-squeezer. My mother had it the year your grandfather went, and she had rules for it.' She dried her hands and held up the left one, and took hold of a finger for each rule. 'You don't lie on your back. If you wake before it's light, you get up, and you don't lie down again that night. You don't tell a dream before you've eaten. And when you wake, you count your fingers. All ten.'

'What for?'

'To be sure you're back.'

'Back from where?'

'She didn't say. I didn't ask her.' Arlette let go of the fourth finger. 'Twelve years, Maylis.'

'Last night I had,' Lesca began.

'Not before you've eaten.' Her mother put her hand flat on the table between them. 'A dream told on an empty stomach stays. That's the third.'

Lesca looked at the loaf. Then she capped her pen, shut the notebook and said she was due at the mairie. The knife lay where it had been put.

'On your side,' Arlette said, at the door. 'I'm not asking. I'm telling you.'

* * *

The mairie was shut to the public on a Thursday, and the secretary came in at ten. Lesca let herself in at twenty to nine by the back door, behind the stone. From the mayor's office she had the stone's unlettered side, and past it the square, with the nine strings of lights off in the plane trees and a loaded lorry waiting at the church for a van to clear the corner.

She had not eaten. She went to the stationery cupboard, took a new notebook from the dozen and sat down at her desk with it. On the first page she wrote Thursday 26 November, and under it Before breakfast. She ruled a margin and numbered it, as she did for the council's agenda.

It had come at 02:51. She had kept to her procedure, and it had put her under in four minutes. On Wednesday that had left her with nothing by morning except the sound of rain, in a week without any. This morning she had woken at six with a Tuesday in her head from end to end, and it was not the Tuesday she had chaired.

Against 1 she wrote that a registered letter had come for the mairie, and that he had numbered it 41 in the top left corner in his own hand. She had signed for it herself, against the side of a post van, in a yellow waterproof. She had signed M. Lesca with her father's tall L, which was the signature she had given up in 2015, and rain had spread the L.

Against 2 she wrote his name in full. Jean Castagnet had driven a white Trafic into the square at dusk with mud up to the door handles, and had put his window down. He had gone grey. He was twelve years older than she had ever seen him, in waders and two jackets, and he had called her little Lesca, which nobody had called her since 2014. She had told him to put it in forty-two. He had laughed, in the rain, with his elbow on the door. She would have laughed with him, and a rib on her right side had stopped her. She wrote that down as well. He had seen it stop her and asked what she had done to herself.

Against 3 she wrote where he had parked. He had sounded his horn at her once and she had stepped up onto the kerb, and he had driven into the space in front of the mairie door and stopped with his front wheels where the stone stood. No stone had been there. There had been a white rectangle with the paint worn through on the driver's side, and an oil stain, and rain had run off his roof onto the tarmac. Then Guy Larrère had walked across from the church with a folder held over his head. He had gone round the van and in at the mairie door to sign his post. She wrote his post, and drew a line under his.

She had been wet to the knees in that square, and had woken dry.

She put the pen down and looked out of the window at the back of the stone. The lorry had the corner now. It came through the square at walking pace and the window hummed.

She had written in front of witnesses for eleven years, minutes and deliberations and the register of deaths, and nothing in those was hers alone. She read the three entries through. They had come out in the mairie's hand and in its order, with no word in them for what they were.

Under them she wrote 4 in the margin, and beside it Friday, and left the line empty.

Then she laid both hands on the blotter, palms down, and counted her fingers, the left hand and then the right. She made ten. The seam across the right palm pulled when she spread it.

She locked the notebook in the drawer with the commune's seal. When the secretary came in at ten, the mayor was on the telephone to the gravel company's site manager about nine weighbridge tickets. He said the tickets were the company's. She read him article nine of the lease, under which they were the commune's on demand, and gave him until Tuesday. He asked whether Monsieur le maire would put that in writing. She said she would, and spelled her name for him.

By noon she had eaten nothing. She went out by the front door and round to the foot of the stone. The granite stood on a concrete footing a hand wider than itself, and beyond the footing, on the side where a driver would have got out, a strip of the old white line still showed on the tarmac, worn through to the black in the middle. She put the toe of her shoe on the worn place. A lorry came round the church, and she went back in.

* * *

Coralie's car came into the yard at seven. She ran the care home in the Port, forty beds and a staff of twenty-two, and she came back from it with the keys still in her hand.

She set a saucer on the kitchen table with one of the chemist's tablets on it.

'Your mother rang me at the home.'

'I thought she might.'

'Twelve years. I took my pillow across that landing in March 2015 because you shouted, and I thought it was him you were shouting at.' Coralie put the keys down beside the saucer. 'There's a thing on your chest. It has a name. And you tell Arlette.'

'I didn't know there was a name for it till this morning.'

'I'd have taken it without one.'

Lesca had no answer to that which was true, and she did not give a false one. Coralie waited for about as long as she had waited at the bedroom door on Tuesday. Then she filled a glass at the tap and stood it by the saucer.

'She says on your side. Madame le maire will please take her tablet and lie on her side.'

She went up. Lesca heard her cross the landing and shut the door of the box room. It held a single bed, made up for a week eleven years ago, and the cartons of Lesca's father's papers stacked to the window, and Coralie's uniform on a hanger over the door. The light under that door went out at ten.

Lesca put the tablet back in its tube. Across the yard her mother's kitchen had been dark since nine. She went up without the landing light and undressed in the dark. The tiles overhead were dry and made no sound. The clock on the chest of drawers read 22:14, and she turned it a few degrees on its base, so that she would have the figures later without moving her head.

She had gone to bed on her right side for forty-three years. She pulled the blanket back, sat down on the edge of the bed and counted her fingers once more, by touch. Then she lay down on her back, in the middle of the mattress, and put her hands outside the blanket.
