# 4

Complaints against the mairie of Peyrebarthe were heard in the kitchen of the mayor's mother, across the yard from the mayor's house, on any morning from six.

No deliberation had set this up. It dated from 14 June 2019, when a lorry came for Arlette's ponies and Arlette said she would not set foot in the mairie again. She had kept to it for seven years. On the two occasions when she had needed the mairie's stamp, the stamp had crossed the yard in the mayor's coat pocket. In that time the village, which had things to say about the lake and did not care to say them across the mayor's desk, had noticed that the mayor's mother was at home. The complainants also held that her coffee was better than the mairie's, and it was.

A complaint made over a kitchen table is not a complaint within the meaning of the code. The mairie's register therefore showed none received about the lake since the lease was signed, and Lesca had quoted that figure to the prefecture twice.

On Thursday 26 November two were waiting. Arlette kept them on the backs of envelopes from the feed merchant, who still wrote to her, under the sugar bowl.

Lesca crossed the yard at half past six with her coat over her arm. She had been awake since five, when the kitchen light came on and showed on her bedroom ceiling. It had come on at five for forty years, there being ponies to see to for thirty-three of them. She had three things in her head from the night and no heading to put them under.

The halters hung on their nail by the kitchen door, oiled. Arlette poured the coffee, put on her glasses and took the first envelope from under the bowl.

It came from the Port road, third house up from the weighbridge. Under clause 9 of the lease the company's lorries ran from seven in the morning. The householder had had them under his window at twenty to seven on nine mornings since All Saints, and had written down the nine dates.

'He asks if you can hear them from your side of the yard,' Arlette said.

'Clause 9 carries a hundred and fifty euros a breach. Nine breaches is thirteen hundred and fifty. Has he the number plates?'

'He has the dates.'

'I'll take the dates.'

Arlette passed the envelope across and took up the second. It came from the terrace, from a woman whose well stood, by her own string, a metre and ten lower than in the October before the digging began. The company's hydrologist had found in 2019 that the workings would not affect the wells of the terrace, and his finding was annexed to the lease.

'She says the string hasn't shrunk.'

'I'll send the man down with a tape on Monday. If she's right, it goes to the company with the annex. If she's wrong, the well is hers to deepen.'

'I'm to tell her that.'

'In those words.'

Arlette wrote Monday, tape on the envelope and put it back under the sugar bowl.

'They could bring these to the mairie,' Lesca said. 'There's a register.'

'So I'm told. I've not seen it.'

Both complaints concerned forty hectares of water that had been common grazing until 2019. The lease on it paid for a third class at the school, with twenty-two children in it, and for a surgery where a doctor sat on Tuesdays and Fridays. Lesca could give both figures without notes. She had given them in this kitchen in the spring of 2019, across this oilcloth, and her mother had replied that she could count as well and had counted her ponies every evening since 1986.

Clause 4 required the common to be cleared of livestock at the company's expense. The company sent a lorry. Arlette counted the ponies up the ramp, signed the driver's sheet and went indoors. Through the window behind her the paddock gate still stood open, on grass that nothing had grazed since.

'That's the two,' she said. 'Mine you've had.'

Lesca drank her coffee.

'You were awake at five,' Arlette said. 'I saw your curtain.'

'There's a weight on my chest at night. I wake and I can't move, and it sits on the bone. Most nights.'

'Since when?'

'Since the flood.'

Arlette set the coffee pot down on its tile. 'Twelve years. And who have you told?'

'You.'

'Not Coralie.'

'Coralie knows I shout.'

'That's not the same thing.' Arlette took her glasses off and folded them. 'It's the sarramauca,' she said, in the voice she used for naming a weed. 'The chest-squeezer. It sat on my mother. She had four rules for it.'

She gave them in her mother's order, and touched the handle of a spoon to the oilcloth at each.

'One. You don't lie on your back. You lie on your side with your knees up, and it has nowhere to sit.

'Two. If it wakes you before it's light, you get up and you stay up. Lie down again and it's waiting.

'Three. You don't tell what you saw before you've eaten. A dream told fasting comes back.

'Four. When you wake you count your fingers, both hands. Then you know all of you is back.'

'Did they work?' Lesca said.

'She died in her bed at ninety-one. On her side.'

Lesca had lain down on her right side on each night of the twelve years, with her knees up, and had woken on her back.

'What does it show you?' Arlette said.

'Last night,' Lesca said, and got no further.

'Have you eaten?'

'Coffee.'

'Coffee isn't eating.' Arlette pushed the bread across the oilcloth, and the butter dish after it. 'Eat something, Maylis. Then tell me.'

She was the last person in Peyrebarthe who used the name. Lesca looked at the bread. Then she took her coat off the back of the chair, and the envelope with the nine dates, and went out across the yard to her car. It was ten to seven.

The first lorry of the day came up from the Port as she reached the square, and changed down beside the stone. The clock on her dashboard read 06:58. She wrote the time on the envelope under the nine dates, which made ten, and fifteen hundred euros.

* * *

The mairie opened to the public at nine. She let herself in at seven.

The stationery cupboard held four dozen exercise books, ninety-six pages, squared, with a blue cover. The commune bought them for the school at sixty-one centimes each. She took one, entered it on the stock sheet as issued to the mayor, and sat down at her desk with the door shut.

She headed the first page as she would have headed minutes.

*Night of Wednesday 25 to Thursday 26 November. Woke 02:51. Did not push. Under by 02:55.*

*1. A registered letter from J. Castagnet. No. 41 in the top left corner, in his hand. Signed for in the square, on the card, in rain. Signature M. Lesca, tall L.*

*2. J. Castagnet at the window of his van, in waders and two jackets, laughing. Rain. He stopped laughing.*

*3. The same van in front of the mairie door. White Trafic, mud to the door handles, front wheels where the stone is. No stone. A white rectangle painted on the tarmac, worn through on the driver's side.*

She wrote it with the pen lying along the seam in her palm, and read it over. Wednesday's night had left her nothing she could find on waking, and she made no entry for it.

From the desk she had the back of the stone through the window, blank, with frost on its shoulder. In the night the Trafic had stood on that spot with rain running off its roof, and there had been room for it.

All of it could have been before December 2014. She had signed for his letters in the square in those years, with that L. He had parked in the mayor's space whenever Larrère was out of it. No rain had fallen on Peyrebarthe for a fortnight, but it had rained before 2014.

Two things could not have been. One was the rib she had woken with on Tuesday, since she had never broken one. The other was the figure. He had got as far as twelve, and the twelve were in a box file in the cupboard behind the secretary's desk. On Tuesday she had woken with forty. On Thursday the corner of the envelope said 41.

She added a line.

*4. He has sent one since Tuesday.*

Then she went back up the page and underlined one word, which was *laughing*. The committee printed his photograph on the order of ceremony each December. Maïté had chosen it in 2015, and in it he held up a brace of duck by the necks and did not smile. She closed the entry as minutes are closed, with the date of the next sitting.

*Next sitting tonight, 02:51.*

The fourth rule was the only one she had neither kept nor broken that morning. She laid both hands on the desk and counted the fingers, and made ten.

The secretary came in at nine with the bread. By then the page was written, and Lesca ate. At ten past she rang the gravel company's site office about clause 9 and read out nine dates. The site manager asked whether the mairie had this in writing from a resident. She said the mairie had it from the mayor, who had been in the square at 06:58 that morning, and that an invoice for fifteen hundred euros would follow.

* * *

Coralie's car was in the yard at seven that evening. She ran the care home in the Port, forty-two beds behind the dyke, and she was at the kitchen table with the staff rota when Lesca came in.

'I had your Port road man in my office at four,' Coralie said. 'Twenty to seven, nine mornings.'

'I have his dates. I rang the company.'

'He'll go to your mother with it.'

'He went to my mother first.'

'She says you left her bread on the table.'

'I ate at nine.'

'She'll want that minuted.'

Coralie filled in a square of the rota. People with something to say to the mairie said it at the home when Arlette's kitchen was shut, and Coralie brought it up the hill with her keys.

'Your mother stopped me by the bins,' she said. 'She tells me something sits on your chest at night.'

'Yes.'

'Since when?'

'Since the flood.'

Coralie put the pencil down. She looked at Lesca for about as long as it had taken Arlette to set down the coffee pot.

'Eleven years I've been across that landing,' she said, 'and I thought it was the shouting.'

'It is the shouting.'

'No. The shouting's the part I get.'

She took the rota upstairs with her. Lesca ate what had been left for her under a plate and washed the plate. A saucer stood by the sink with a tablet on it, put out in advance.

The room across the landing had been the box room, and the boxes were still in it, stacked along the wall under the window. It held a single bed, a lamp clamped to a shelf and eleven years of the home's rotas in lever-arch files. Coralie had gone across with her pillow on a night in February 2015, after the fourth shout in a week, to get five hours before an early shift. The pillow had not come back. Since then she had left the landing light on at night, so as to find the other door without breaking her neck.

At eleven Lesca was sitting on the edge of her bed with the exercise book open on the blanket beside her, on the side where nobody had slept since that February. She laid the pen in the fold.

Coralie came to the doorway in her dressing gown and stood with one hand on the frame.

'What's the exercise book?'

'Minutes.'

'Of what?'

'Of the night.'

'Madame le maire minutes her nights now.' Coralie looked at the book, and at the pen. 'Your mother says you're to lie on your side.'

'I've lain on my side for twelve years.'

'And?'

'I wake on my back.'

'There's a tablet on the saucer.'

'I know where the saucer is.'

The tube of tablets had been replaced twice in eleven years, each time because it had passed its date.

Coralie stayed a little longer. Then she went back across the landing. She left the light on and, for the first time since 2015, her own door open.

Lesca had amended her procedure in two respects. She would lie on her back from the start, and not be turned onto it. And whatever the night brought up she would write down before she moved, and before she ate.

She counted her fingers, both hands, and made ten. The clock radio read 23:07. She had three hours and forty-four minutes in hand. She lay down in the middle of the bed, on her back, with her hands flat on the blanket, and did not turn onto her side.
