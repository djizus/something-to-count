# 5

On Friday she woke with water.

The weight had come at nine minutes to three and she had let it lie, as she had the two nights before. She could not lift a hand to count it, so she had tried the other thing Kévin did. She read the red figures on the clock, turned her eyes to the lampshade and read the figures again. They said 02:51 both times. She was awake under it, then, in her own bed, and it bore down at its thirty-five kilos until she went.

At six the alarm went, and the low meadows were under water in her head, beside the stone and the sash.

She had them from above, from higher than the dyke. Grey water lay from the foot of the bank to the far trees. It was flat, and the wind was putting a grain on it. No grass stood out of it and no fence posts. The hump of Jeannot Castagnet's tonne was gone, and so were her mother's ponies. A flood on the Adour came down brown, with branches in it, and went out through the gate inside a week. This water had dropped its mud long ago. It had been lying there for years.

She counted her left hand on the blanket before she moved, thumb first, and made five. Coralie was on earlies and had left. It was raining on the tiles.

* * *

The landowners' association met once a year, on the last Friday of November, and its statutes of 1962 required it to view the works. At nine it was on the dyke at the sluice gate, eleven members in seven vehicles, parked nose to tail along the crown of the bank with their wipers going.

Guy Larrère presided from his driver's seat, with the window lowered a few centimetres. The others got out. They stood along the rail of the platform in caps and waxed coats and looked down at the gate, and Maylis stood at the winch in her yellow waterproof with the handle in her left hand, because she had been told to bring it. She had been there since eight. She had greased the shaft and the pawl and wiped the planks, and the rain had put the wet back on them before the first car came.

Below the platform the low meadows were as they had been on Wednesday. The grass stood in mud, with a skin of rain on it. The ponies were at the far fence, eleven of them, and the tonne showed its hump. She looked at them for some time. There was grey water over all of it in her head, and it would not come off.

'The employee has a report,' Larrère said through the gap.

'It stuck on Monday at the nineteenth turn,' she said. 'I put my shoulder under it and it gave, and I broke a rib on that rail. I wrote to the council in 2011, 2012 and 2013 that it would seize. If it seizes with the gate down and the river up, there's no letting the water out.'

Nobody answered her. A man in a green cap came along the platform. He farmed sixty hectares of the high ground, next to Larrère's, and he had been at school with her father. He took the handle out of her hand.

'It's how you stand to it,' he said. 'Your father stood square.'

He fitted the handle to the shaft and stood square. The pawl clicked over. He counted aloud for the others, and they counted with him under their breath, and the gate began to come up out of the drain a centimetre at a turn. At the nineteenth the handle stopped. He leaned on it. He took a second grip, lower, and put his back into it, and his face went dark under the cap. The handle did not move. He stood off and looked at it, and then he pulled it from the shaft and handed it back to her.

'It wants grease,' he said.

'It's had grease since 2013.'

She fitted it again herself, because the gate could not be left as it was. She got her left shoulder under the handle this time and pushed with her legs. For the length of that push she could not breathe at all. Then it gave, and she caught the rail with her left hand and not with her ribs, and turned the gate back down. Eleven men watched her do it.

It came to her on the way down, with the pawl clicking. A winch that stopped at nineteen with the gate half up was a nuisance. A winch that stopped with the gate shut and the meadows full would keep the water in for good. It would lie there until it turned grey.

'The association takes note of the winch,' Larrère said. 'It may also note that the question passes from us. From the first of January the works are Hontaube's to maintain, under the merger, and Hontaube's two men will hold the key.' He looked at her through the gap for the first time. 'That's forty euros a month you'll not have the trouble of. You're not to worry about it.'

'Hontaube's eleven kilometres from this gate.'

'They have a van.'

'At three in the morning, in December.'

'They have a van,' he said again, and put it to the vote that the association had viewed the works. Eleven hands went up along the rail. The man in the green cap raised his last, and wiped it on his coat first. The association's secretary had its minute book open inside his coat, out of the rain, and he wrote in it standing up.

They drove off along the dyke in the order they had parked. She stayed on the platform with the handle until the last of them had turned down towards the Port. Then she took her ten breaths, an hour late, with her forearm against her side.

* * *

The parcel came at twenty to twelve. She was raking the gratings by the church when a white van with a courier's name on it stopped in front of the mairie, under the dark tree. The mairie was shut until two. The driver got out, tried the door, read the card of its hours and looked round the square.

'I've a parcel for a Monsieur Lesca, at the mairie,' he called. 'Is the man from the commune about?'

'That's me.'

He looked at the label and then at her. The label said M. Lesca. He brought the box across, a carton the size of a bale, and held out a pad of slips with a pen tied to it by a string.

'Sign at the bottom. Yellow copy's yours.'

She took the pen in her right hand. She had signed M. Lesca with her father's tall L on every delivery note since 2008, and she watched for the L. The pen made it. Then, without stopping, it ran back under the whole name from right to left in one line, turned at the M and came up through it in a loop, and finished with a short stroke like a full stop. It was done fast, by a hand that signed forty times a day.

She had never made that line. She stood with the pen lifted off the paper.

'That's fine,' the driver said. He tore off the yellow copy, gave it to her and took his pad back. Rain was falling on the slip. She folded it once and put it inside her waterproof, against her shirt.

She carried the box to the shed. A spike stood on the bench there with the commune's delivery notes on it, hers down to 2008 and her father's under them. She lifted off a handful and went through them with her thumb. Each of hers said M. Lesca, with the tall L, and stopped at the a.

String number nine was twenty metres of green cable with two hundred lamps on it. Kévin Labat's van was not in the square, and she did not telephone him. At half past one she brought the tractor to the third tree and raised the bucket to the height of the wire loops. She left the engine running and climbed out along the loader arm with the string over her left shoulder, as her father had. It took her forty minutes. Each clip was above her head, and she lifted her right arm to each one, and by the last she was taking her breath in thirds.

Larrère parked under it at two. He did not look up. She threw the switch at half past five, and nine trees came on round the square.

* * *

She went into the café at six because her hands were shaking and she did not want to drive with them.

Jeannot Castagnet was at the counter in his waders with a glass of white in front of him. Four men were playing cards at the table by the stove. She asked for a coffee and stood at the far end of the zinc. The church clock struck. She laid her left hand flat on the counter and counted it with her right forefinger, thumb first, and then turned the right hand over and looked at that. She looked at it for a while. It was the hand that had signed.

'Is there one missing?' Jeannot said.

'No.'

'Then what are you counting them for?'

'To see I'm awake.'

'And are you?'

'Five.'

'She's counting what they left her with in the box,' one of the card players said, and the table laughed. Jeannot looked round at them and then back at her hand. He did not laugh. He moved his glass a little way along the counter towards her.

'I was on the dyke this morning,' he said. 'At the end, in the van. Hontaube's men are to have the key.'

'From January.'

'Two lads who've never seen a tide. I'll have to begin again at number one.' He drank. 'They'll have the gate down when it should be up and up when it should be down, and they'll not come out for it at night.'

'Jeannot. Has the low ground ever stayed under? From the bank to the far trees, and not gone out?'

He put the glass down. It was his subject, and he had sent the mairie twenty-six letters on it.

'In fifty-two. My father had it. The river came over in February with the gate down, and the old winch sheared, and they couldn't raise it. It lay till Easter. He said it went grey as a slate once the mud had settled, and you couldn't see where his tonne was but for the stovepipe.' He looked at her. 'Best duck there ever was. Why?'

'No reason.'

'You've a reason. You've done a rib and you're counting your fingers.' He picked up the glass again. 'If that winch goes with the gate down, I want it in writing that I said so.'

'Put it in forty-two.'

He nearly laughed. She paid for the coffee and left it half drunk.

* * *

Coralie had the soup on when she came in. She had put a line through Thursday on the shopping pad, and a line through Friday. Under them she had written Monday, and drawn a box.

Maylis sat down at the table in her waterproof. She turned the pad over. The back of it was grey card, and she pulled it towards her and wrote on it with the pencil that hung from the dresser.

She wrote Tuesday, and beside it the stone, and where it stood, and that a line was cut above her name which she had not read. She wrote Wednesday, and the sash, and the square full of people waiting. She wrote Friday, and the water, grey, to the far trees. Under the three she wrote The winch went with the gate down, and under that, after a time, I had told them, and they gave me the mairie.

'Is that the shopping?' Coralie said from the stove.

'It's a list.'

'Of what?'

'Things to keep hold of.'

'Put the doctor on it.' Coralie brought two bowls to the table. She looked at the pad lying face down, and at the pencil, and she did not turn it over. 'What do you want with a list, Maylis?'

'I don't know yet.'

Coralie set a bowl in front of her. Then she laid two fingers under Maylis's right arm, through the waterproof, and Maylis's breath stopped short.

'You've had that arm up.'

'I hung the string.'

'On your own.'

'It's up. There are nine.'

Coralie kept her fingers there until the breath came back, and counted it, and went for the bread.

Maylis unzipped the waterproof and took out the yellow slip. It had dried stiff. She unfolded it beside the pad, under the lamp. It said one parcel, 2.4 kilos, Commune de Peyrebarthe, M. Lesca, received 27 November at 11:42. Under that was the name with the tall L, and the line running back beneath it, and the loop through the M.

Kévin had said that writing would not hold in a dream. She looked away, at the window and the rain on it, and counted the fingers of her left hand. Then she read the slip again from the top, and it said the same.
