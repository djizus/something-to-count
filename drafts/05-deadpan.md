# 5

The landowners' association met on Friday at nine, on the dyke at the sluice gate, in rain. Seven of its thirty-one members were present. The keyholder attended. She was not a member and had no vote.

The association owned the gate and the winch that raised it. The commune employed the keyholder. Maylis had written to the council about the winch in 2011, 2012 and 2013, and the council had taken note, the winch not being the council's to mend. Nobody had written to the association. Its president was Guy Larrère, who as mayor had received all three letters, and who held that a thing received by the mayor had not thereby been received by the president.

The members stood on the dyke under four umbrellas. She went up the steps to the platform and unlocked the box where the handle was kept.

From the platform she had the low meadows in front of her as far as the trees on the far bank. They were green and wet. Eleven ponies stood on them with their tails to the river, and Jeannot Castagnet's tonne showed out in the middle, in its mud. She counted the ponies and looked at the grass for some time, to be sure of it.

In the night it had been under water.

The weight had come at 02:51 for the fourth night running. She had let it lie, and gone under, and woken at six with a third thing in her head. It was water, grey and flat, from the foot of the dyke to the far trees. No pony stood in it and no tonne stood out of it. She had seen the river over these meadows in most Decembers of her life, and that water was brown and moved and carried branches. This water was still. It had an edge to it, like a pond's. She had been looking down on it from higher than the dyke, with a thing in her right hand that was rough and cold and about the weight of a roof tile. It had come one winter and had not been let out.

Water stays on a meadow when the gate that drains it cannot be raised. She put the handle on the shaft.

On the pier of the gate a gauge was bolted, with a red line her father had painted across it. The river stood a metre forty below the line.

The first item was the winch. Larrère read out the keyholder's report of Monday, which was a line in his own diary. It said winch stiff, L. hurt side.

'There's nothing wrong with that winch,' said a member from the high meadows, who grew sixty hectares of maize behind the gate and had spoken first at each meeting since 1994. 'It's how it's turned.'

He came up the steps, took the handle out of her hand without asking for it and set his feet apart.

'You get your weight over it,' he said. 'Like this. You don't pull at it.'

He got his weight over it. The handle did not move. He shifted his feet and got his weight over it a second time, and the six members on the dyke watched him do it. Then he straightened up and gave the handle back to her.

'It's seized,' he said.

'It seized on Monday.'

'And if it seizes with the river over the line?' Maylis said.

The member from the high meadows said that it had not seized with the river over the line. Larrère said that the river had not been over the line since 2009, and that the keyholder's remark would be noted. It was the fourth note taken of the winch and the first by the association.

The association resolved, by seven votes, to seek an estimate. It then voted to have the gate raised a further twenty centimetres before the tide turned. Maylis backed the handle a quarter turn to lift the pawl, which was her father's knack, and brought the gate up left-handed with her right elbow against her side. It took nine minutes. Nobody offered to take a turn, the handle having been tried.

Under any other business Larrère said he would be brief, and on this item he was. From 1 January the commune's duties at the gate passed to Hontaube. Hontaube's two men would hold the key and turn the winch as part of their work, without the forty euros. The forty euros had been voted for her father in 1990 and had not been revised. She would be asked to show the two men the gate in December.

'Do they know it sticks at nineteen turns?'

'You'll tell them that,' Larrère said. 'That's what the showing is for.'

He closed the meeting at twenty to ten. The members went back along the dyke to their cars. She took the handle off the shaft and locked it in its box, and put the key back on her belt, where her father had carried it for thirty years and she for eighteen.

She stayed on the platform after the cars had gone. The ponies had moved down towards the tonne. She tried to lay the grey water over them, from the dyke to the trees, and could not make it stay. At ten she drove up to the church for the buckets.

* * *

The mairie opened at two on Fridays. At twenty to two a courier's van stopped in front of it, and the driver put his window down.

'Parcel for a Monsieur Lesca, care of the mairie. Would that be the man from the commune?'

'That's me.'

He looked at the label, and at her.

'It says M. Lesca.'

'M for Maylis.'

He considered that and found nothing in his instructions against it. He passed out a carton the length of a forearm and a delivery slip in three leaves, white over pink over yellow, with a pen.

She put the carton under her left arm and signed in the box.

She had signed M. Lesca with a tall L since 2008, on each delivery note and acknowledgement card that had come to the mairie while it was shut. That made some four thousand times. On the four thousand and first the pen left out the M. It wrote Lesca in one run, without lifting, and then went on. It came back under the whole name from right to left in a single stroke, crossed itself, and stopped.

It had taken about a second. It was the signature of a hand that signed all day and had no time for the letters. She had never made it. She had watched her own fingers make it. She gave the pen back, looked at the hand that had held it and counted the fingers, and made five.

The driver tore off the white leaf, said good afternoon and drove away.

She stood the carton on the bonnet of the commune's van and turned the yellow leaf over. On the back she signed again, to see. It came out M. Lesca, with the tall L. She signed a third time, fast, looking at the church. M. Lesca, tall L. She tried a fourth time for the other and could not find where it began.

The pink leaf was the mairie's. A slip for anything delivered to the mairie went into the mayor's tray, and the procedure made no exception for goods the commune had not ordered. When the door was unlocked at two she went in and laid the pink leaf in the tray, on top of the week's post. The carbon had taken the name faintly and the stroke under it well.

She folded the yellow leaf with the first signature inside and buttoned it into the chest pocket of her waterproof.

Kévin Labat came out of his van at three and went up in the bucket with the new string, and by four the third tree had been clipped along her father's wire. At half past five she threw the switch on the mairie wall. Nine plane trees came on round the square. Larrère drove through at twenty to six, slowed at the third tree and went on without lowering his window.

* * *

She went into the café at ten to six, which she had not done that week. Jeannot Castagnet's Trafic was outside it with its wheels on the pavement. He was at the counter in his waders with a glass in front of him, dressed for the night.

'Nine,' he said, without turning round. 'I counted them from the van. Give her a coffee. The commune owes me for it.'

The owner gave her a coffee.

The church clock struck six. It was an hour, and she had kept the hours since Wednesday. She laid her left hand on the zinc and touched each finger of it with her right forefinger. Then she began on the ten breaths.

Jeannot watched the hand.

'Is one missing?'

'Five.'

'She's counting them,' Jeannot told the café. 'Twelve years out of the box, and she's checking it gave them all back.'

The café laughed, which was four men and the owner. Peyrebarthe had told the box joke in one form since 2014, and this was the first thing anyone had added to it.

'Little Lesca,' Jeannot said. 'You gave it another twenty this morning.'

'I was told to.'

'Forty-two.' He drank. 'And they tell me the key's going.'

'To Hontaube. From January.'

'Who says so?'

'Larrère. Under any other business.'

Jeannot put the glass down. 'Two men from Hontaube. They'll never have seen that gate with the river up. They'll open it when it wants shutting, and they won't know my tonne from a molehill. Your father knew that gate. You know it near enough.' He picked the glass up again. 'That goes in forty-two as well. It's getting long.'

She finished the ten breaths. The rib let her have the last one whole.

'Jeannot. Has the low meadow ever stayed under?'

'It's under every winter.'

'Not for a month. For good. Years.'

He looked at her as he had looked at her on Tuesday, when the rib stopped her laughing.

'It can't,' he said. 'That's what the gate's for. It comes in at Christmas and you let it out in January. If you didn't, my tonne would be at the bottom of it, and me writing to the mairie from a boat.' He laughed at that, with his head back, and the four men laughed with him. 'Why?'

'No reason.'

'Then go home and count your toes,' Jeannot said.

* * *

Coralie came in from the late shift at half past nine. The shopping pad lay on the kitchen table where it had lain since Wednesday. It said Doctor in Dax, and Thursday, and Friday, and neither box was ticked. She read it standing, with her coat on. 'You didn't go on Thursday.'

'I had the cemetery.'

'Or today.'

'I had the association. And the tree.'

Coralie took the pencil, wrote Monday under Friday and drew a third box.

'Monday you've nothing before the buckets. I've looked at the commune's sheet.' She put the pencil down on the pad. 'I felt you again at three.'

'I slept after.'

'You went under after. I know the difference, I work nights.' She laid two fingers under Maylis's right arm, on the place, and left them there for the length of a breath. 'This is mending. That's all I can tell you about by pressing on it.'

She hung up her coat and went up.

When the stairs were quiet Maylis turned the pad over onto its face. She opened it from the cardboard back, at the last page, which Coralie's lists would not reach before the spring, and wrote on it with Coralie's pencil.

*Tuesday. A stone in the square, in the mayor's space. My name on it. A line above that I didn't read.*

*Wednesday. Me in the mayor's sash at the mairie door. The square full. Black shoes, not mine.*

*Friday. The low meadows under water. Grey, still. No ponies. No tonne.*

She read the three down. Under them she wrote what she made of them, which took one line.

*A flood that never went down. Me in the sash after it. The stone is for that.*

She read the line again. Guy Larrère did not come into it anywhere.

Then she wrote a fourth.

*Friday. Signed for the lights. Not my signature. See slip.*

She unbuttoned the chest pocket of the waterproof, which hung on the back of the door, and laid the yellow leaf on the table beside the pad with the first signature upwards. Kévin Labat had said that writing would not hold in a dream. She read the slip from the supplier's name to the foot. It said one string, two hundred lamps, warm white, twenty metres, received in good order, and in the box it said Lesca, with a line under it that crossed itself. She looked away at the clock on the cooker until the minute changed. Then she read the slip again, and it said the same both times.
