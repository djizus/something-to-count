# 6

On the third night of lying on her back, Lesca turned her head.

She had been in that chair most nights for twelve years, before the weight or after it. It was the chair in the steel room the university people had built inside the tobacco barn in June 2014, bolted to the floor, with the panel in front of it and the two lamps on the panel. In the barn one lamp had come on, once. In the chair at night both were lit and stayed lit. She had sat facing them for twelve years with her head held to the front, as it had been held in the barn by nothing except being told.

On Saturday at nine minutes to three the weight came, and she left it alone and did not go under. She lay beneath it with her eyes open for longer than she had managed on Thursday or Friday. Then the ceiling was the steel ceiling, and she was in the chair, and the smell was the same smell, cold steel and old tobacco, with no weight in it.

Her hands lay on her knees. She looked down at them and counted, the left and then the right, and made ten. So she was back, by her grandmother's rule, and she was in the chair. She had both at once.

She turned her head to the left. It went. She saw the wall, grey, with its line of rivets, and then the door, which had a rubber seal round it and no handle on the inside. She turned to the right and saw a steel shelf at the height of her elbow with a pencil on it and an envelope. The arms of the chair were warm under her forearms. They were warmer than she had made them.

She put out her right hand towards the shelf. The hand was lying on a blanket, and the red figures read 06:00.

She was on her back in her own bed. Coralie's door stood open across the landing, and the lamp was on in the box room.

She wrote before she moved.

*Night of Friday 27 to Saturday 28 November. Before breakfast. Woke 02:51. Did not push. Did not go under until after 03:00.*

*1. The room in the barn. Both lamps lit. Counted, ten. Turned my head. Left, the door. Right, a shelf, a pencil, an envelope.*

*2. The counter of the café. A left hand flat on the zinc, counted with the forefinger, five. J. Castagnet beside it in waders with a glass of white. He asked if there was one missing.*

*3. Amend procedure. The count to be made on each hour by day, as well as on waking.*

She read it over and it said the same. In the second entry he was fifty-eight, and he stood at a counter on a Friday evening with a glass in front of him and a Saturday to come. She had not seen him older than forty-six.

Coralie's feet crossed the landing and stopped at the door.

'You're minuting.'

'Yes.'

'Did you shout?'

'You'd know.'

'I'd know,' Coralie said. 'You didn't.' She looked at the notebook for about as long as she looked at a chart, and went down to put the coffee on.

Lesca was at the mairie by seven. The safe stood in the mayor's office behind the door, a green steel cabinet that had come with the building. It held the commune's seal, the cash box for the hire of the hall, the keys to the civil registers and the sash, folded in tissue. She put the notebook under the sash and locked the safe. The key was on her own ring, and there was no other.

* * *

Saturday was dry, with an east wind that came across the lake and over the bank.

The commemoration committee met at ten at the end of the Port, nine members and the secretary, to place the marquee. They walked up onto the dyke in single file, by the ramp, past the care home. Its forty-two beds lay behind the bank, on the dry side, and two residents in dressing gowns watched the committee go up from a first-floor window. The bank was eleven years old and four metres high, grassed, with a gravel path along its crown. The State's engineers had drawn its line in 2016, forty metres back from the old dyke, which could not be raised on its footing. The line crossed the last four houses of the Port. Lesca had put it to the council with the figures. Four houses were to be bought at the State's valuation and taken down, so that a bank could stand in front of thirty-six and the care home. It carried by nine votes to five, and she signed. The four owners had five hundred and twelve thousand euros between them. The café said that week that she had always been hard. In the same week it called Larrère a character, for voting against a bank he had asked the prefecture for in 2008.

The four plots could still be told apart. The bank lay across them, and on either side of it the garden walls ran up to the grass and stopped. A fig tree stood on the second. On the fourth, which had been the last house in the Port, a gatepost still carried the number 40 on an enamel plate, and a hand pump stood in the grass on the lake side. That house had been Adjudant Sarthou's. He was the village's gendarme and Jeannot Castagnet's cousin. He had signed the sale last of the four, in her office, in uniform, and had asked that the gatepost be left standing. Then he had served out his time from a room over the café and gone to a flat in Dax in 2022.

A bench stood on the crown above his plot, facing the water. The prefecture had sent it for the opening. A brass plate was screwed to its back rail. THE COMMUNE OF PEYREBARTHE TO THE STATE, IN GRATITUDE, it said, and the year.

Maïté Castagnet sat down on it, in her black coat, with her handbag on her knees. She did not look at the plate. She looked out over the lake, towards a place somewhere short of the dredger.

'Will it have its back to the water?' she said. 'As last year?'

'As last year.'

'Then they'll not have to look at it while they drink.'

'The marquee is forty metres by fifteen,' Lesca said. 'It stood last year at the foot of the bank on the third and fourth plots, with its back to the water. The bar ran along the bank side. The bull came down the crown from the ramp. I propose the same.'

She went down the bank with the measuring wheel and the commune's can of marking paint and walked it out on the grass, forty metres, and sprayed a white cross at each corner. The fourth cross fell beside Sarthou's gatepost. When she came back up the committee was standing in the wind with its collars turned up, and Marie-Jo Labat was looking at the sky above her head.

'Through the chair,' Marie-Jo said. 'Until 2018 the marquee was the fête's. It stood on the fairground by the hall for forty Julys. The council lent it to December for one year.'

'The council reallocated it.'

'By one vote. The chair's.'

'And the first deputy resigned the same evening. It's minuted.'

'It is. I wrote the letter on the council's own paper.' Marie-Jo brought her eyes down as far as the four white crosses. 'Through the chair. Six thousand people will stand on four families' floors with a glass in their hand. I move that the marquee go on the fairground, where nobody's kitchen was.'

'The fairground parks two hundred cars. The dyke road parks nine hundred. Last year the bar took eleven thousand euros on that grass.'

'I know what it took. I counted it.'

She had counted Lesca's votes too, in the hall in April 2015, twice, and read the figure out herself. For three years after that she had sat at the mayor's right hand and called her by her first name. Lesca had not been inside her house since the night of the letter.

Guy Larrère had said nothing. He stood with his back to the wind and his hands in his coat pockets, watching the two of them as he might have watched a gate working.

Lesca put Marie-Jo's motion first, as the rule required, and it had three hands, Marie-Jo's and Larrère's and one more. She put her own. Hands went up along the crown of the bank. Maïté Castagnet, on the bench, kept both of hers on her bag. Lesca's watch showed eleven.

She had amended her procedure that morning, and she kept to it. She held her left hand in front of her, palm up, and touched off its fingers with her right forefinger, thumb first. The seam lay across the palm. She was on a grass bank in a dry wind, eleven years after she had signed for it. Jeannot Castagnet was not at any counter. She had five fingers.

'Five,' she said.

It was also the number of hands, with her own. The secretary wrote it.

'Madame le maire.' Larrère had taken one hand out of his pocket. 'I ask that the manner of the count be minuted.'

'The count is five to three, with one abstention.'

'The manner of it. The chair counted the fingers of her left hand, aloud, and made five. I saw it and so did the committee. A member may ask that a fact of the sitting be recorded.' He turned to the others. 'In 2014 the commune put her in a box for an afternoon. Twelve years on she is seeing what came out with her.'

Two of them laughed, into their collars. Marie-Jo did not. She looked at Lesca's hand, which was still held out, and then for a second at her face, and it was the look she had given her on Tuesday over a figure.

The secretary had stopped writing. She was waiting on the chair.

'He's within the rule,' Lesca said. 'Record it.'

The secretary wrote that the mayor had counted the fingers of her left hand and found five. Lesca read it upside down as it went into the register, under the vote. It would be read out for approval at the next sitting, and she would sign it.

She closed the sitting at ten past eleven. The committee went down the ramp to its cars. At the foot of it one of the two who had laughed held up a hand to the other and counted it off with a finger, and they laughed again before they got in. Larrère stopped beside the bench on his way and read the plate, as he did each year, with his head tilted.

* * *

Père Honoré had left his Clio at the foot of the ramp. He came up it with his coat buttoned to the neck and a folder under his arm. He was from Togo and served fourteen churches. The one at Peyrebarthe had a new roof, paid for in 2017 out of the flood money, and nine people under it most Sundays. On the twelfth of December it was full.

He had come to see where the marquee stood, since the procession left the church for it after the Mass. He looked at the four white crosses and wrote something in the folder. Then he stood beside her on the crown, facing the same way she was.

'Madame le maire. I ask each year, and I ask early, so that you have the time. I will be in the confessional at Peyrebarthe on the two Saturdays before the twelfth, from five o'clock. Will you come?'

She had gone in January 2015. He had been in the parish four months and the roof was still letting in water, and a bucket had stood in the aisle outside the confessional and dripped while she spoke. It had taken eleven minutes. No one else had heard it. He was bound not to repeat a word of it, and he had not, and he had gone on shaking her hand at the church door for eleven years as he shook everyone's.

'You've asked eleven times, Father.'

'This is the twelfth.'

'And what do I say?'

'You say the mairie will be represented at the Mass.'

'The mairie put a roof on your church.'

'The commune put a roof on the commune's church, and I thanked the commune from the pulpit. I am not asking the mairie.' He was looking along the bank. Maïté Castagnet was still on Sarthou's bench with her bag on her knees and her face to the water. He looked at her for some time. Then he looked at his shoes. 'It is not a thing one does once, Madame le maire. One does not say it once and have done.'

'I said all of it.'

'You said it to me.'

The wind came over the bank and moved the pages in his folder. On the bench Maïté put up a hand and held her collar shut.

'I went once,' Lesca said.
