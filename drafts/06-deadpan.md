# 6

By Saturday the mayor of Peyrebarthe had been back in the box twice, and had minuted both visits.

The box was the village's word for it. In June 2014 the council had let the old tobacco-drying barn to a university team for a fortnight, and had placed its outdoor employee at the team's disposal for one afternoon. The team built a steel room inside the barn, the size of a garden shed, with a chair bolted to the floor in front of a panel with two lamps. One lamp came on, once. She wrote on a card which it had been, sealed the card in an envelope, pressed a buzzer and sat for three hours until they opened the door. They paid her a hundred and eighty euros. Since then anyone late in Peyrebarthe had been still in the box. The prefect had said it of himself in 2015, in the square, having arrived forty minutes late to pin a medal on her.

She had dreamed the room a few times a year since, as people dream of the school they went to. Those dreams had no smell.

On Thursday night she lay on her back from eleven, by her amended procedure. The weight came at 02:51. She did not push it, and she did not let it put her under. She kept her eyes open and gave it nothing to press against, which was harder than either, since it took all the attention she had to do nothing with her chest. The red figures read 02:52. Then she no longer had the clock.

She had the panel. It was a forearm's length from her face, grey steel, with two lamps on it side by side, and both were lit. The chair was under her, bolted down, and narrower than she remembered. She could not turn her head. She had the panel and the two lamps and the smell, which was cold steel and old tobacco. It had sat on her breastbone for twelve years, and here it was only the smell of the place. Nothing was on her chest.

In 2014 she had sat in that chair for three hours and been bored. She was not bored now. The door was behind her left shoulder, where she could not see whether it was shut. The team had unbolted the room twelve years ago and driven it away in a hired lorry, and she was awake in it. She put the visit at two minutes. Then the alarm went, and she wrote four lines before she moved her legs.

On Friday she counted her fingers at each hour. Her mother's rule said on waking. Lesca had extended it to the hours, on the ground that she could no longer be certain, at any given one, which she was doing. She counted seventeen times between six in the morning and eleven at night, and made five on each hand each time. At four the secretary asked whether she had hurt her hand.

On Friday night, in the box, she counted them again. To count them she had to look down, and her head went down. Her hands lay on her knees. She touched off ten, and the seam was across the right palm where it belonged. When she looked up both lamps were still lit. In 2014 only one had been, and she did not need a card to tell her which. Then she turned her head to the left, since it would go. The wall was steel plate, riveted, twelve rivets to a row. The door was in it, shut, with no handle on the inside. She turned to the right. A shelf ran under the panel with the buzzer on it, and beside the buzzer an envelope lay open with a card drawn half out. There was writing on the card. She had begun to lean towards it when the alarm went.

She wrote that down on her back, with the notebook held over her face and nothing moved but the one arm.

*Night of Friday 27 to Saturday 28 November. Before moving. Woke 02:51. Did not push. Did not go under.*

*1. The box. Both lamps lit.*

*2. Counted fingers. Ten.*

*3. The head turns. Left, the door, no handle. Right, a shelf, the buzzer, an envelope open, a card.*

*4. Did not read the card.*

On Thursday Coralie had stood in the bedroom doorway and looked at the notebook, and then at the pen. At eight on Saturday Lesca drove to the mairie, which was shut, and opened the safe. It held the commune's seal, the petty cash, the mayor's sash in its box, the prefect's medal in its case and a deputy's sash in a carrier bag. She laid the notebook on the carrier bag and shut the door. The combination was known to the mayor and, in a sealed envelope, to the prefecture. From now on the notebook would spend its days there and its nights on the blanket.

* * *

The commemoration committee walked the dyke at half past nine, in frost, to place the marquee.

The dyke was ten years old. In 2016 the State had offered to rebuild the Port's defences at its own cost, on its own line, sixty metres back from the old bank. The line passed through four houses. It protected thirty-six, and the care home. Lesca had put it to the council as thirty-six against four, and it had carried by ten votes to five. The four were bought at the State's valuation and taken down the following spring. From the crest the committee had the thirty-six below it on one side, with the care home and its minibus among them, and on the other side the lake.

The bank ran across what had been their plots. On the land side each plot kept whatever the contractor had been told to leave, which was a doorstep, a length of garden wall and, on the second, a fig tree. The downstream plot had been the last house of the Port. It had belonged to a gendarme named Sarthou. The State had set a bench on it, with a brass plate on the back that gave the years of the works and stated that the commune of Peyrebarthe thanked the State. The plate had come from the prefecture with the bench, already engraved. Sarthou had signed last of the four, on the final day allowed, and had left his key in the door. He had retired to a flat in Dax in 2022 and was not known to have sat on the bench.

The marquee measured forty metres by fifteen, and the four plots were the only flat ground of that size in the Port. The secretary carried the plan and the register. Lesca carried the stakes and the mallet. She had driven the commune's stakes for seven years, and saw no reason to send for the outdoor man on a Saturday at time and a half.

She put the first stake in beside the doorstep of the first plot.

'Through the chair,' said Marie-Jo Labat. There was no wall on a dyke for her to look at, and she addressed the far shore of the lake. 'The sixty tables are in the hall, up on the terrace. Last year the fête committee carried them down here and carried them back. I'd ask the chair who carries them this year.'

'The commune's man. With the trailer.'

'Through the chair, the commune's man has twenty-eight hours a week and nine strings of lights to take down.'

'Then the fête committee. As last year.'

'For six hundred euros,' Marie-Jo told the far shore.

She had been first deputy for three years. In June 2018 Lesca had proposed moving the festivities line from July to December, where the people were, and the council had carried it by nine votes to five. Marie-Jo handed in her deputy's sash the same evening, across the council table, in a carrier bag. The bag went into the safe that night. Her successor had bought his own.

'Through the chair,' Marie-Jo said. 'I move the marquee goes up on the terrace, beside the hall. The tables are there. The fête is held there in July, and nobody has drowned at it.'

The secretary stopped writing. Maïté Castagnet, in her black coat, held her handbag in front of her in both hands and looked at the water. She was looking at a place on it to the left of the dredger. No one else on the committee looked that way.

'There's a second site moved,' Lesca said. 'We'll place the bull and then vote.'

The firework bull was to be lit sixty metres from canvas, by the pyrotechnician's sheet. She paced the sixty along the crest from Sarthou's bench and drove a stake.

'That stake is in the association's concrete,' Larrère said. He was president of the landowners' association, and the gates stood forty metres further on.

'It's in the commune's dyke. Your concrete starts at the rail.'

'I'll be brief,' Larrère said, and he had got as far as 1962 when Marie-Jo took the white kitchen timer out of her coat pocket and held it up. He looked at it, and at her, and did not go on. The rule made no distinction between a council room and a dyke, and she had applied it to him in both.

'He couldn't abide fireworks,' Maïté said, to the stake. 'They put the duck off for a week.'

Lesca came back along the crest with the mallet and put the two sites to the vote from Sarthou's doorstep. The plots were taken first. Six hands went up, and hers was one. The clock on the terrace began to strike ten.

It was an hour. She brought her raised hand down in front of her, with the mallet under her other arm, and touched each finger of it with the forefinger of the left, thumb first. She made five. The seam pulled. Then she did the left hand, and the clock finished.

'I ask that that be minuted,' Guy Larrère said.

'That what be minuted?' said the secretary.

'The mayor's count. I had her at five.' He looked round the committee. 'So the box gave them all back.'

Two members laughed. Marie-Jo did not. For the first time that morning she looked at Lesca and not at the lake, and what she looked at was the hands.

Lesca had not meant to do it in front of nine people. By Saturday it was done at each hour before she had meant anything.

'Minute it,' Lesca said.

The secretary looked at her, and wrote. The terrace was then put, and fell by two votes to six. Maïté kept both hands on her bag for it, as she had for the plots.

The minutes of that meeting record six votes for the plots, two against, which were Marie-Jo's and Larrère's, and one abstention. They also record that during the vote the mayor counted her fingers and found five.

* * *

Père Honoré arrived at twenty past ten, when the stakes were in. He had come from a funeral at Hontaube in his Clio, and he walked up the bank holding his coat shut at the neck.

'We thought you were still in the box, Father,' Marie-Jo said.

It was said to him as it was said to anyone. Lesca had heard it some hundreds of times in twelve years, at the café, in council, and once from a prefect. She had not before heard it on a morning when she had been there at three.

The committee went down to its cars. Maïté went last. Père Honoré stayed on the crest to see where he would stand, since on the twelfth he blessed the dyke before the bull was lit. He chose the bench, and sat down on it with his back to the plate. He patted the slat beside him. Lesca stayed standing.

'Nine on Sunday,' he said. 'Under a roof that does not let in one drop. On the twelfth I will have four hundred, and I will know none of them.'

'It's a good roof.'

'It is the flood's roof. I say so each year, when I thank you for it.' He looked at the stakes. 'You are putting the bar on a man's kitchen.'

'He was paid the valuation.'

'I know what he was paid. He told me.' Père Honoré folded his hands. 'Madame Castagnet comes to me on the first Friday of each month. I tell you only that she comes.'

Lesca looked along the crest. Maïté had reached the bottom of the bank and was walking towards the houses with her bag held in front of her.

'Madame le maire. I ask each year.'

'You do.'

'I am at Peyrebarthe on Thursday the tenth, from five. The church is warm now. Will you come to confession before the twelfth?' He waited. 'I have asked eleven times. I do not ask because I have forgotten anything.'

She stood the mallet on its head on the frozen grass. She had her right hand shut on the handle, over the seam.

'I went once,' she said. 'You heard it.'
