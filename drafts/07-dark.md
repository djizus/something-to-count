# 7

Maylis had the bar from eight, and the loto began at half past.

The bar was a trestle across the kitchen hatch, with the coffee urn on it and a price list in Marie-Jo's capitals. The hall smelled of floor wax and of the urn. Twenty-four tables stood in three rows under the strip lights, one tube of which had flickered over the third row since 2019. The tables were as she had set them on Tuesday, each with its paper cloth and its saucers of maize kernels for marking the cards. On the stage the prizes stood on a table of their own, the two ducks, the strimmer in its box and the ham in a cloth. Beside them was a chair for the caller and a bag of ninety numbered counters.

She had carried eleven crates in from the van by herself, on her left side, one at a time. Her yellow waterproof hung dripping from the nail behind the hatch, with COMMUNE DE PEYREBARTHE turned to the wall.

People came in from the rain in ones and twos and paid Marie-Jo at the door, two euros a card. Marie-Jo had printed two hundred. By twenty past eight she could see how many there would be, and she had the empty chairs lifted out from the tables and stacked against the wall, so that the rows closed up and the hall looked full. She left one. It was at the end of the third row, nearest the bar, and she laid a card in front of it and a saucer of kernels.

'That's yours,' she said. 'It's paid. Someone can mind the hatch for the ham.'

'I'm on the bar till the end. You put me on it.'

'I put you on it because nobody else can work that urn. Sit when you can.'

The care home's minibus backed up to the door at twenty-five past. Coralie got down from the driver's seat and brought twelve residents in, two on her arms and the others on frames, and sat them along one table in the second row. She took their coats, spread the cards and put the kernels where each hand could reach. Then she came to the hatch.

'Eight coffees, four with nothing in. And a chair for me, if she's left one.'

'There's one. It has my card on it.'

'Then I'll stand.' Coralie looked at the crates stacked behind the trestle and counted them. 'You carried those.'

'One at a time.'

'I'd have come at seven. You had only to say you wanted it.' She took the tray and went back to her table.

Maylis's mother was at the end of the first row with three cards. She did not look round at the bar. She squared the cards with the side of her hand and tipped her kernels out onto the cloth to count them.

* * *

Kévin Labat came in at half past, with rain on his cap, and bought one card.

She had had him in her head since six that morning. The weight had come at its minute and she had let it lie, and what the night left her was Kévin on the commune's mower, the ride-on, cutting the grass round the war memorial on a dry day. He was wearing her waterproof. She had it from above and through glass, from the first-floor window of the mayor's office, where she went twice a week to lay the post in the tray. She had been standing at that window with a pen in her hand. The yellow back went down the grass and came up it, and the letters across it were hers, with the E of COMMUNE worn half away where the strimmer harness rubbed. He had lifted a hand to the window, and she had not lifted hers.

She had written it on the last page of the shopping pad before she moved. *Saturday. Kévin L. on the mower in my jacket. Me at the mayor's window, watching.*

He came to the hatch for a beer.

'Nine,' he said. 'It looks right. Did you get somebody for the bucket?'

'No.'

'You went out on the arm. With that rib.' He waited. 'You could have rung me.'

'I didn't want you.'

She had not meant it to come out as it did. He took his change and looked at her, and then past her at the crates.

'I'll shift those for you at the interval.'

'Leave them where they are.'

'What have I done?'

'Nothing.'

'You're looking at me as if I'd had something off you,' he said. He picked up the beer and went to sit by the door, where he could leave for the tide.

* * *

Père Honoré had driven over from a baptism at Hontaube with his coat over his alb. He hung the coat on the back of the caller's chair and came to the hatch for a glass of water before he began. He stood with it and counted the hall, by rows, and then counted it again.

'Ninety-one,' he said. 'I had nine on Sunday. This is the most I will have in front of me this year.'

'It's the ham, Father.'

'I know what it is. I am glad of them all the same.'

He went up onto the stage, sat down and took the bag on his knees. Marie-Jo gave him the microphone and told the hall the order of the prizes, a line for each duck, two lines for the strimmer and the full card for the ham. The hall went quiet. He put his hand in the bag.

'Forty-seven,' he said. He let a breath go by, and said it again, lower. 'Forty-seven.'

He called as he said Mass, to the back wall, and he gave each number twice. His voice dropped on the second, as it did at the end of a prayer. Marie-Jo had not asked him for that. Between the numbers there was the sound of ninety-one people moving a kernel or not moving one, and the urn ticking, and the rain. Her mother pushed each kernel into its square with one finger and kept the finger on it for a moment. Coralie leaned along her table and marked a card for a woman whose hands shook.

'Twelve. Twelve.'

The first duck went at ten to nine to a woman from Hontaube, and the second to one of the residents, and Coralie held the card up for her while Marie-Jo checked it.

At nine the church clock struck through the rain. Maylis took her ten breaths behind the urn. Then she laid her left hand on the trestle and touched off the fingers. By the door Kévin's phone buzzed on the table, and he had his own hand up in front of his face. A man waiting for a pastis watched her finish.

'All back?' he said.

'Five.'

'They'll be glad to hear it at the barn.' He took his glass and went to tell his table.

* * *

Adjudant Sarthou had the strimmer at twenty-five past nine. He stood up at the fourth table in the first row with his card held out, in a pullover, and said 'Here,' in the voice he used at the roadside. Marie-Jo read his two lines back against the counters and gave him the box.

He was the gendarme for Peyrebarthe and two communes more, and he was Jeannot Castagnet's cousin. He lived in the last house of the Port, the one that stood hard against the dyke, behind a privet hedge he kept at chest height. He had been due his pension since 2022.

He did not sit down. He put the box under one arm and held out his hand for the microphone, and Père Honoré gave it to him.

'While I have you all in the one room. There was to be a drink at the brigade on the nineteenth of December, for my going. It's put off. That is the fourth time.' He turned his head and found the table he wanted. 'A complaint was laid on Thursday at ten past four, against persons unknown, for the lowering of water on the common grazing. I took it down myself. A man can't be let go with a file open in his name, and I've not had one shut since the spring but the same hand has opened another.'

Jeannot Castagnet was at the sixth table in his waders, with six cards spread in front of him and his elbows either side of them.

'It's my right,' he said, without looking up.

'It's your right. And it's my pension.'

'Then find who drained me, cousin, and shut it.'

Some of the hall laughed. Sarthou did not, and neither did Jeannot. Sarthou's eyes went along the tables to the hatch and rested on Maylis for as long as it takes to read a number plate. He knew who had raised that gate on Monday, and on whose telephone call. Then he gave the microphone back and sat down with the box on his knees.

She found that she was holding the edge of the trestle. The key was on her belt, under her pullover.

* * *

At the interval the bar was three deep. She served it left-handed and took the money with her right.

Jeannot came for a glass of white and put the coins down one by one.

'It's not against you,' he said. 'Persons unknown. I had him write it that way.'

'I'm the person. I turned it.'

'You're the hand. I know whose telephone it was.' He drank half the glass standing. 'He'll not shut that file by asking you. Let him ask the mairie.'

Coralie came after him with the tray of cups.

'Twelve of them, and a duck,' she said. 'The one who won it asked me if there'd be a loto next year. I told her there would.'

'There will.'

'There'll be a loto,' Coralie said. She did not say where the twelve would be. She set the cups down in the sink herself, so that Maylis would not have to reach.

When the hall had sat down again Marie-Jo came round behind the trestle with the cash box, tipped it out on the draining board and began to build the coins into columns of ten.

'Three hundred and six on the bar,' she said. 'A hundred and eighty-two at the door. The bull didn't light in July and I've the firm's invoice all the same. I have to make it here.' She squared a column with her thumbnail. 'The committee's writing to the council for a new urn. Forty euros. There's nobody on that council who'll lift a hand for it.'

'Write to Hontaube.'

'You'd be on it. If you'd answered me in 2020 you'd be sitting there now, with a hand to lift. For the urn, and for your winch.'

'I'd have had to hand in the keys. An employee can't sit.'

'I know the rule. I read it to you in my kitchen.' Marie-Jo did not look up from the coins. 'I kept you the eleventh place. The list went in to the prefecture on the Thursday at six. I rang you at four and at five. You'd said you'd think.'

'I was thinking.'

'You were on the mower. I could hear it.' She swept a column into a paper sleeve and folded the end over. 'And you're handing the keys in anyhow. In January, to two lads from Hontaube, and nothing to show for them. That's all I'm saying. I'll say no more about it.'

She wrote the two figures on her clipboard and carried the box back to the door.

Maylis looked at the waterproof on its nail. On the pad she had written that they gave her the mairie. Nobody was given a mairie. A woman put her name on a list by a Thursday at six, and before that she handed in the keys, and the jacket with them. Then somebody else had the jacket. On the far side of the glass it was a Labat, and he had waved to her.

She would have neither by January. The chair at the end of the third row stood empty, with its card and its saucer.

'Sixty-eight,' said Père Honoré. 'Sixty-eight.'

At ten the clock struck. She took her ten breaths. The man with the pastis was watching the hatch from his table, and two others with him, so she kept her hands down. Then she counted the left one under the trestle, by touch, against her thigh, and made five where nobody could see it.

The ham went on the full card, and it took a long time. He drew and called and let the breath go by, and the hall bent to its cards and did not speak. By the door Kévin's chair was empty and his glass was on the table. Coralie had three cards going at once along the residents' table and a hand on someone's shoulder. Her mother had one square left to fill on the middle card, and kept a kernel ready between finger and thumb.

'Nineteen,' said Père Honoré.

Maylis's breath stopped short, on its own.

'Nineteen.'

Jeannot Castagnet came up off his chair with a card over his head and shouted.
