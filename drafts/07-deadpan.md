# 7

The loto of Peyrebarthe opened at eight on Saturday evening with ninety-one players, a priest at the microphone and the commune's outdoor employee behind the bar, where Marie-Jo Labat had put her on Tuesday.

The bar was a trestle table across the back of the hall, with the urn on it, two crates of beer underneath and a slate. Coffee was a euro, beer two, and wine one fifty the glass. Maylis read the slate twice when she had chalked it. It said the same both times.

The twenty-four tables she had carried in on Tuesday stood in three rows under the strip lights, with paper cloth along them. The floor had been waxed on Thursday and the urn had been on since six, and the hall smelled of both. A bowl of maize kernels stood at each table for marking the cards. A card cost two euros. Marie-Jo had printed two hundred and sold a hundred and sixty-three, most players taking two, and Jeannot Castagnet four.

Ninety-one was three more than the year before. Marie-Jo had counted them at the door. Père Honoré had counted them again from the stage, twice. Eleven had come over from Hontaube, and had been heard in the porch to say that the lights in the square were well done.

They sat facing the stage. From the bar Maylis had ninety-one backs. Her mother was at the end of the second table, with her coat on, pushing a kernel onto a card with one finger.

At the end of the third table, nearest the bar, one chair stood empty. A card lay in front of it with six kernels counted out beside it. Marie-Jo had laid it there for the bar, in case the bar was quiet. She had not asked whether the bar wished to play.

Père Honoré stood at the microphone with a cloth bag of numbered wooden counters. He drew one, held it at arm's length and gave it twice, first whole and then in its parts, with a pause between, as he gave the responses at Mass.

'Sixty-three,' he said. 'Six, three.'

Ninety-one heads went down.

'Eleven. One, one.'

Kernels went onto cards the length of the hall, with a sound like rain starting on a tin roof.

'Forty-one. Four, one.'

'I've sent that one,' Jeannot Castagnet said, and the third table laughed. He sat among his four cards in waders and two jackets, dressed for the night, with the kernels heaped in front of him.

* * *

Coralie had brought twelve residents up from the Port in the home's minibus. They had the first table, by the radiators, with a card each. She marked for three who could not see the numbers and for one who could not hear them, and at a quarter past eight she came to the bar with a tray.

'Twelve coffees. Four with nothing in, eight with two sugars.'

Maylis filled twelve cups from the urn left-handed.

'You've done your ten?'

'At eight.'

'And Monday?'

'It's on the pad.'

'It's on the pad in my writing,' Coralie said. 'I'd like it in your diary.' She squared the cups on the tray. 'My eldest wants to know if the home's table can win the ham, since it's their last loto.'

'It's a loto. I don't decide who wins it.'

'I told her that. She said to ask you anyway.'

The home was to close in June. Coralie carried the tray back down the hall between the tables. Nobody at the first table had mentioned June all evening.

'Seventy-eight. Seven, eight.'

At half past eight Kévin Labat came in by the side door with a crate of beer on each arm and stood them under the bar. He had on a brown leather jacket with a sheepskin collar.

In the night it had been hers.

The weight had come at 02:51, and she had read the clock twice and let it take her. At six she had a fourth thing. Kévin Labat was on the commune's ride-on mower, on the verge of the dyke road, with the blades down. He was wearing the yellow waterproof with COMMUNE DE PEYREBARTHE across the back, and the cuffs were turned back once, as she turned them. He lifted two fingers off the wheel to her as he went by. It was how men in Peyrebarthe greeted Guy Larrère's car. She had been in a car herself, at the wheel, a dark one she did not own, with a folder on the seat beside her.

She had written it at the back of the shopping pad before the coffee. *Saturday. Kévin Labat on the mower, in my jacket.* The first three had each given her something, the stone and the sash and the mairie that went with them. This one took. The commune issued one waterproof, to its one outdoor employee. The waterproof was on a hook by the side door of the hall at that moment, where she had hung it at seven, and she looked across to be sure of it.

Under the line she had written three words more. *My job gone.*

Larrère had told her on Tuesday that there was the question of staff, which was not settled, and that she was not to worry. He had told her on Friday that the key went to Hontaube. She had not been told the rest. She did not see how Kévin Labat came to be Hontaube's man, and she had left that off the pad.

'Kévin. Has Larrère said anything to you about January?'

'To me? About what?'

'Work. The mower. The verges.'

'What would I want with the verges?' He straightened up from the crates. 'I make more in one tide than you do in a fortnight. And my mother wouldn't have me in a commune jacket. She'd say I'd taken it off your back.'

'Twenty-two. Two, two.'

The hall clock reached nine, and a phone buzzed in his jacket. He looked at his hand. She looked at hers, on the bar, and touched off five.

'You're doing it,' he said.

'On the hour.'

'Anything?'

'What would there be?'

'A hand that comes out wrong. Writing that moves.'

'I read a slip twice last night. It held.'

'Then you were awake.'

'I knew I was awake.'

'That's what I'd say asleep,' Kévin said. He took a beer, put two euros on the bar and counted them aloud so that she could not push them back.

* * *

The two ducks went before nine, one to Hontaube and one to the second table. The strimmer was played for at twenty past, on a full line.

'Nineteen,' said Père Honoré. 'One, nine.'

Maylis put her forearm against her side.

'Here,' said a man at the second table, and stood up with his card.

It was Sarthou. He was a gendarme at the brigade, and Jeannot Castagnet's cousin, and he had the last house of the Port, with the dyke for a back fence. Marie-Jo checked his line against the counters and carried the strimmer down from the stage in its box. He held it up to the hall. He said it would do the bank behind his house, which the commune had not cut since August.

'It's on the sheet for December,' Maylis said from the bar.

'Then I'll do it in November, and send the commune the petrol.'

He did not sit down. He walked up to the stage with the strimmer under his arm and held out his hand for the microphone, and Père Honoré, who had been asked for many things at many microphones, gave it to him.

'While you're all sitting down,' Sarthou said. 'The nineteenth of December is off. Those of you who had the invitation can put it with the other three.'

The hall groaned. His retirement had been fixed for June 2025, for December 2025 and for June 2026, and the hall had been booked each time and each time released.

'I retire with my files shut,' he said. 'I've always said so. On Thursday my cousin drove to the brigade and filed a complaint against persons unknown, for the lowering of the water at his tonne on the afternoon of Monday the twenty-third. That's a file. It's mine. It has a number.'

Ninety heads turned to the third table.

'I've a right to file,' Jeannot said.

'You have. And I've a right to a pension.'

'Then find who lowered it.'

Some of the heads turned on, as far as the bar. The person unknown had forty cups to wash and washed two of them.

Sarthou gave the microphone back and came down the hall for a beer. Kévin Labat had moved to the far end of the trestle when the gendarme left the stage, and was reading the slate. Sarthou paid for the beer and stood the strimmer on the bar.

'I'll want a statement from persons unknown,' he said, not loudly. 'Monday.'

'Monday I've a doctor in Dax.'

'Tuesday, then. Bring the rib, it's in the complaint. He says the winch is unsafe and somebody's been hurt on it.'

'He's complaining for me?'

'He's complaining about the water. He's put you in as evidence.' Sarthou drank. 'It's not you he's after. It's Larrère who gave the order. But he won't file against Larrère by name.'

'Why not?'

Sarthou looked into his glass for a time and did not say why not.

'Tuesday,' he said. 'At the house. You know which it is.'

* * *

At the interval the players came to the bar, and for twenty minutes she had their faces.

Père Honoré asked for a glass of water.

'Ninety-one,' he said. 'I had nine on Sunday. It is the most I have had under one roof this year, and the roof does not leak.'

'They've come for a ham, Father.'

'I have preached to people who came for less.'

Jeannot came last, with his four cards under his arm so that no one could look at them.

'I've put your rib in it,' he said. 'Don't thank me.'

'You've filed against me.'

'Against persons unknown. You're known.' He took his glass of white. 'Little Lesca turned it. Somebody told her to. I want it written down who.'

When they had gone back Marie-Jo brought the cash box from the door and set it beside the bar tin. She let nobody else count either.

She counted the door first. A hundred and sixty-three cards at two euros made three hundred and twenty-six. Then she tipped out the bar tin and built the coins into piles of ten along the trestle.

'Ninety-four,' she said. 'Four hundred and twenty. Less the ham, the ducks and the strimmer, the fête has thirty-one euros.'

'Three more players than last year.'

'Three.' Marie-Jo swept the piles into the box one at a time. 'You'd be on the council now, you know. If you'd answered me in 2020.'

'I said I'd think about it.'

'You're still thinking about it. The lists closed on the twenty-seventh of February at six in the evening. I was in the sub-prefecture at Dax at ten to, with fourteen names and a gap at number three.'

'I'd have had to resign the post. An employee can't sit.'

'I know what an employee can't do. I read it to you in your own kitchen.'

'I'd have had to hand in the key.'

'You're handing in the key on the first of January,' Marie-Jo said. 'For nothing.' She shut the lid. 'We lost by thirty-eight votes.'

Maylis rinsed a cup.

'If we'd got in. Who'd have been mayor?'

'I would. Who did you think?' Marie-Jo looked at her, for about as long as it takes to count a chair. 'It's the one thing I ever asked you, Maylis. The rest I've told you, and you've done it. I've wondered since which you minded.'

She took the box under her arm and went back up the hall to the stage.

On the pad it said that they had given her the mairie. Maylis had not written who they were. In 2020 it would have been Marie-Jo's, on thirty-eight votes the other way, with Maylis third on the list and the key handed in.

* * *

The ham was played last, on a full card.

The bar was quiet for it. The chair at the end of the third table stood empty four metres from her, with its card and its six kernels. Maylis stayed where she had been put. The hall clock reached ten, and she counted her left hand on the trestle and took the rib's ten breaths, and across the hall her waterproof hung on its hook with the commune's name on the back.

Kévin stood by the side door in his leather jacket, watching his mother watch the cards.

'Eight. Eight on its own.'

Nobody spoke. Ninety-one heads were down under the strip lights. At the first table Coralie leaned across and put a kernel down for her eldest.

'Fifty-five. Five, five.'

Her mother pushed a kernel onto a card and looked for the next.

'Thirty. Three, nought.'

Père Honoré put his hand into the bag, and held the counter out at arm's length, and read it.

'Forty-two,' he said. 'Four, two.'

Jeannot Castagnet stood up in his waders with a card in each hand and shouted for the ham.
