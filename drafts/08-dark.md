# 8

The entry for Saturday night was short, and Lesca wrote it at six on Sunday with the notebook held over her face.

*Night of Saturday 28 to Sunday 29 November. Before breakfast. Woke 02:51. Did not push. Held eleven minutes. Under by 03:02. Did not reach the room.*

*1. The village hall, set for the loto. Père Honoré on the stage with the bag of numbers. J. Castagnet on his feet, shouting for the ham.*

She counted her fingers and made ten. Across the landing Coralie's door was open and the bed in the box room was already made.

The notebook spent its days in the safe. At twenty to eleven she drove up to the square with it on the seat beside her. Sunday was dry again, with frost in the gutters, and the nine strings hung unlit in the plane trees. She heard the leaf blower before she had the car door open.

Kévin Labat was working round the stone. He was the commune's outdoor man, and he had the blower on its harness and a drift of plane leaves moving ahead of him across the tarmac towards the church wall. He was wearing a yellow waterproof with COMMUNE DE PEYREBARTHE across the back. The E of COMMUNE was worn half away where the harness rubbed. The left cuff was bound with a strip of grey tape.

She had put that tape on in 2013, after the cuff caught in the winch. She had handed the waterproof in with her keys two years later, and the commune had issued it to each of her three successors in turn. On Tuesday night she had seen that cuff on a hand that was turning a winch.

He saw her and cut the motor.

'Madame le maire.'

'You're on the stone early.'

'It's Mass at eleven. He doesn't like it running while they go in.' He pushed the ear defenders down round his neck. 'I wanted to say thank you for the Sunday hours. It's time and a half up to the twelfth. It pays the insurance on the car.'

'It's in the budget. The council voted it.'

'On your proposal. I read the minutes.' He looked at the stone, which had a clean space round it now, and then back at her. 'You always go round me a bit wide. You've no need. I've said it before.'

She had not answered him the other times either. In 2022 the commune had put cameras on its three gates, on her proposal, and she had sent fourteen nights of film to the biodiversity office in Dax. The netting of glass eels stopped that winter. The commune kept a grant of sixty thousand euros a year that it had been about to lose. On the ninth night of the film Kévin Labat was kneeling on the apron of the middle gate at ten past three, with a net, lit by his own head torch. He had served six months at Mont-de-Marsan in 2023. In March 2024 she had proposed him for the outdoor post, and it had carried by eight votes to seven. The café had not settled since which of the two it held against her, the film or the post.

'It was a good picture,' he said. 'They showed it in court. You could count my teeth.'

'That jacket,' she said. 'Has it been off you this week? Lent to anybody?'

He looked down at it. 'It's the only one there is. Why?'

'Monday. Ten to four in the afternoon.'

'On me. I was in the cemetery with the blower. You signed my sheet on Tuesday.' He waited for her to say why, and she did not. 'Is one missing? There's just this.'

'No. There's just that.'

The church clock began to strike eleven, and the bell for Mass started under it. It was an hour, and she kept the hours now. She brought her hands up in front of her and touched off the left and then the right. She made ten. The seam pulled. She was in the square in a frost, and the jacket was on Kévin Labat, three metres from her, with her own tape on the cuff.

He had watched her do it.

'My mother said you did that on the dyke,' he said.

'What else did she say?'

'That she'd have nobody laugh at it in her kitchen.' He pulled the ear defenders up. 'She said not to tell you.'

He started the motor and took the leaves on towards the wall.

* * *

She put the notebook in the safe, on the carrier bag, and stood at the window of the mayor's office while the bell rang.

Nine people crossed the square and went in at the church door. She counted them from above, as she counted hands. Seven were women. Marie-Jo Labat was the sixth, in her dark coat, and she passed the stone without turning her head and passed her son without speaking to him. Maïté Castagnet was the ninth. The door shut. The roof over them was slate, new in 2017, bought with what the State had paid the commune after the flood, and it did not let in a drop.

They came out at ten to twelve. Père Honoré stood in the porch in his alb and shook nine hands. Then he looked across the square and up at the mairie, at the one window with somebody in it. He was from Togo and had fourteen churches, and he had had six thousand people in front of him on the twelfth of last December and would have them again in thirteen days. He lifted his hand to the window. It was his tenth of the morning. She lifted hers.

Marie-Jo had stopped by the stone on her way back. Lesca went down, since she had to pass it to reach her car.

Kévin had the blower off again. His mother was doing up the top press stud of the waterproof for him, under his chin, as if he had been nine.

'Sundays,' Marie-Jo said to him. She did not look round. 'She has you out on a Sunday now.'

'It's time and a half.'

'She's generous with what she took off you.' She finished the stud and patted the yellow chest once, flat-handed, on the commune's name. 'I counted what six months came to. I had the time to count it.'

'Mother.'

'I'm saying it to you. I'm not saying it to the mairie. I've nothing to put to the mairie until the next sitting.'

She walked away along the church wall with her missal under her arm. She had not gone through the chair, because there was no chair in a square. Lesca stood with her car keys in her hand. Kévin looked at his boots.

'She did say that, though,' he said. 'About her kitchen.'

* * *

The envelope was in the top drawer of the chest of drawers, under the clock radio, where it had been since June 2014.

It was a plain white envelope, of the size that takes a card. The university people had given it to her in the steel room with the card and a pencil, and told her to write down which lamp came on, seal it, and press the buzzer once. She had done that. The woman in charge had called her the friend, through the door, and she had taken it kindly. Three hours later they had opened the door and paid her a hundred and eighty euros. They had packed up by the weekend and had not asked for the envelope. She had carried it home in the pocket of the yellow waterproof and put it in the drawer. It had her father's key ring on top of it, and the box for a watch.

In twelve years she had not opened it. There had been no reason to. She knew what was in it.

On Friday night she had seen it lying open on a steel shelf, with the card drawn half out and writing on the card.

She sat down on the edge of the bed with it at four on Sunday afternoon. The gum had dried, and the flap came up under her thumbnail without tearing. She drew the card out. It was white, unlined, and a little yellowed along one edge. In the middle of it, in pencil, in her own hand, it said *right*. The t was crossed low, as she crossed it.

She read it twice. She read nothing once that she was going to put her name to. It said *right* both times.

She laid the card on the chest of drawers, face up, beside the envelope.

Her grandmother had left four rules. Lesca had broken the first on Thursday and the third each morning since, and each breach had brought her further in. She kept the fourth on every hour. That left the second. If it woke you before light, you got up and you stayed up, and you did not lie down again that night.

She took the clock radio in her lap and set the alarm for four.

* * *

Coralie came up at ten. She stopped in the doorway with her hand on the frame and saw the card.

'You've opened it.'

'Yes.'

'Twelve years that's sat in the drawer. I've dusted round it.' She did not come in. 'What does it say?'

'Right.'

'And is it?'

'It's what came on.'

Coralie looked at the clock radio, which was turned on its base towards the pillow. The small red dot of the alarm was lit beside the figures.

'That's set.'

'For four. I'll be up twenty minutes. I'll try not to wake you.'

'You'll wake me. My door's open.' She took her hand off the frame. 'I'm not asking what it's for. I'd like to be told one day, that's all.'

She went back across the landing.

The weight came at 02:51. Lesca left it alone and held for seven minutes, and it put her under, and she got nothing.

At four the alarm went. She was on her feet before she was awake. She counted her fingers on the landing, by the light from Coralie's door, and made ten, and went down in her coat. The kitchen was cold. Her mother's window was dark across the yard and would be for another hour. Frost had drawn a fern on the inside of the pane over the sink. She stood there and drank a glass of water, and her feet ached on the tiles. She did not sit, and she did not eat. At twenty past four by the cooker clock she went back up.

'Twenty minutes,' Coralie said, from the box room, in the dark.

'Yes.'

She lay down on her back in the middle of the bed with her hands outside the blanket. There was no weight. She had the ceiling for a short time, and then she did not have it.

The panel was in front of her, with both lamps lit. The chair was under her, and its arms were warm. The smell was the smell of the place.

She looked down and counted her hands, and made ten.

She turned her head to the right. The shelf was there, and the buzzer, and the envelope lying open with the card drawn half out. She did not put out her hand. She had lost the room on Friday by reaching. She leaned in the chair as far as her head would go, and read.

It was a white card, unlined. In the middle of it, in pencil, in her own hand, it said *left*. The t was crossed low.

She looked away from it, at the left-hand lamp, and held the lamp for a count of five. Then she read the card again. It said *left*.

She had read *right* at four that afternoon, on the edge of her own bed. She had written *right*, in that room, on a Thursday in June, because the right-hand lamp was the one that came on. Nothing she remembered had the other word in it. A hand that crossed its t as hers did had sat in this chair and seen the other lamp.

The arms of the chair were warm. It was the warmth a body leaves, and it did not cool while she sat. She kept her forearms on them and did not move.

At six the alarm went a second time, and she had the bedroom ceiling. She wrote before she moved.

*Night of Sunday 29 to Monday 30 November. Before breakfast. 02:51, under by 02:58, nothing. Up at 04:00 for twenty minutes. No weight on return. In the room by half past.*

*1. Counted, ten. Did not reach.*

*2. The card on the shelf says left. Read twice. My hand.*

*3. Mine says right.*

*4. It is not a thing I remember. Somebody else wrote it.*

She read the four lines twice, as she read anything, and they held.

Then she got up. She took her card off the chest of drawers and slid it back into the envelope, as far as half way, with the word showing. She did not seal the flap. She left the envelope open on the chest of drawers, under the clock.
