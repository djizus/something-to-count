# 8

On Saturday night the mayor of Peyrebarthe held out under the weight for forty minutes and was put under at 03:31 without having reached the room.

She wrote that first on Sunday, on her back, with the notebook over her face. Then she wrote what she had woken with.

It was the village hall, at night, with the strip lights on and three rows of trestle tables down it. The hall was full. Père Honoré stood at a microphone on the stage with a cloth bag, calling numbers, and all down the tables people pushed maize kernels onto cards. Sarthou was there, who lived in Dax, holding a strimmer over his head in its box. Marie-Jo Labat stood beside her at a trestle, counting coins into piles, and spoke to her while she did it. She did not speak through any chair. And at one of the tables Jeannot Castagnet got to his feet in his waders with a card in each hand and shouted for the ham.

*4. J. Castagnet on his feet. He shouted.* Thursday's entry had him laughing and Sunday's had him shouting for a ham. In neither had he the look of a man who had drowned.

*5. Room not reached. Held 40 minutes. The alarm at six is too late and 02:51 is too early.*

Coralie crossed the landing at ten past six and stopped in the doorway.

'Three nights,' she said.

'Three nights what?'

'You haven't shouted since Wednesday. I've started lying awake for it.' She looked at the notebook. 'I'd got used to the hour.'

There had been no saucer by the sink since Thursday. Lesca noticed it when she went down, and Coralie saw her notice.

* * *

Mass at Peyrebarthe was at half past ten on the second and fourth Sundays, and on the fifth when there was one. Lesca did not go to it. She went to the mairie for the notebook's sake, and at twenty past ten she was crossing the square with the safe key in her hand.

The commune's outdoor man was at the stone with a leaf blower. The plane trees had been letting go all week, and leaves lay against the foot of the granite and in the cut of the letters. He had the machine on his back and the nozzle down, and he was wearing the commune's yellow waterproof.

It was hers. She had handed it in with the keys in 2015, and the commune, finding it sound, had not bought another. COMMUNE DE PEYREBARTHE had cracked across the shoulders. The cuffs were turned back once.

On Tuesday she had woken with those cuffs on the handle of a winch. She looked at the hands in them now. They were Kévin Labat's, broad, with a signet ring, and they were not the hands that had counted nineteen turns.

He saw her and cut the engine.

'Madame le maire.'

'Not during Mass, Kévin.'

'It's twenty-five past. They're not in yet.'

Nine people stood in the porch. Père Honoré was in the doorway in his alb, counting them in, and when he had counted them he counted them again. The roof over him was eleven years old and had not let in a drop. It had been paid for out of the flood money, and so had the gutters.

In the night he had had a hall.

'Start again when they're out,' Lesca said.

'I'm on Sunday hours whether it's running or not.'

'Then be paid quietly until half past eleven.'

He unslung the blower and stood it on the kerb. He was Marie-Jo's son. In 2022 the commune had fixed a camera above each of its motorised gates, at four hundred euros a camera, and its mayor had sent eleven nights of footage to the biodiversity office. From November to March glass eels came up the Adour on the night tides, and for as long as anyone could remember men had netted them where the water narrowed. The Natura grant was sixty thousand euros a year and conditional on the netting stopping. It stopped. Lesca had put it to the council as sixty thousand euros against the nets of five men. Four of the five could not be made out under their hoods. Kévin Labat had looked up at the lens on nine of the eleven nights, to see what it was.

He got six months and served them at Mont-de-Marsan. When he came out, the outdoor post was vacant, and she gave it to him at twenty-eight hours a week. The café had spent that month deciding which of the two things it held against her more.

'I wanted to say thank you for the Sundays,' Kévin said. 'Three of them, at double. That's the tyres.'

'The stone has to be clear for the twelfth. It's not a favour.'

'I know what it is. I'm saying thank you for it.' He looked at the church. 'My mother says I'm not to.'

'Your mother's in there.'

'She's one of the nine. She counts them for him, in case he's wrong.'

The church clock struck eleven over their heads. It was an hour. Lesca put the key in her left hand and touched off the right, thumb first, and then changed hands. Kévin watched her do it.

'It's in the minutes,' he said. 'Larrère's told the café. Five.'

'Six voted. I counted five.'

'What's it for?'

'To be sure I'm here.'

He considered the stone, and the mairie, and the leaf blower on the kerb. 'Where else would you be?'

She did not answer that. She asked him instead whether he had hurt his side that week, the right side, on anything. He said he had not, and why. She said the jacket had looked to her as if it had been in a fall.

'It's your jacket,' Kévin said. 'It looked like that when I got it.'

The nine came out at twenty to twelve, and Père Honoré shook each of them by the hand in the porch. Marie-Jo was the fourth. She came across the square in her Sunday coat, stopped beside her son and turned his collar down for him.

'You don't thank her,' she said. 'You work them.'

'I've thanked her.'

'Then it's done.' There was no chair in the square to speak through, and she went through him. 'Tell Madame le maire the fête committee wants the hall keys on the fifth, for the sixty tables. And tell her nine.'

'She says nine,' Kévin said.

'I heard her.'

Marie-Jo looked at Lesca's hands, as she had on the dyke, and then at the leaves in the letters. She went home to put the dinner on. In the night she had stood at Lesca's elbow counting coins, and had called her by her first name without looking up.

Père Honoré came over last, when the porch was empty, with his alb over his arm.

'Nine,' he said. 'I counted twice, and Madame Labat counted after me.'

'It's a dry roof, Father.'

'It is a dry roof over nine people.' He watched his breath go up in the frost. 'Thursday the tenth. From five.'

'You said so yesterday.'

'I say it again. That is what a priest is for.'

Kévin started the blower. The leaves came out of JEAN CASTAGNET first.

* * *

The envelope was in the top drawer of the chest of drawers, under the clock radio. It had been there since June 2014, beneath her passport and her father's watch.

It was brown, with the university's name printed in the corner and nothing written on it. The woman in charge, a Dr Kessler, had called her the friend all that afternoon, and Lesca had taken it kindly. She had handed her the card and the envelope together and told her to write one word, seal it and keep it by her until someone asked. Nobody had asked. The team had left on the Saturday.

She knew what was in it. One lamp had come on, once, and she had seen which.

At three that afternoon she sat on the edge of the bed with the envelope and a kitchen knife. In the room an envelope like it lay open on a shelf, with a card half out and writing on the card, and she had twice lost the room before she could read it. She wanted to know what a card of hers looked like, so as to know another when she saw it.

She slit the flap. The card was white, the size of a postcard, unruled. On it, in biro, in the hand she had written before she had a signature, was one word.

*right*

She read it twice, as she read anything she was to sign. Then she laid it on the chest of drawers and amended her procedure for the third time.

Her grandmother's second rule was that if it woke you before light you got up and stayed up, since if you lay down again it was waiting. The weight came at 02:51, which left her three hours to hold before six, and she could not hold three hours. She would let it put her under at 02:51, as she had for twelve years. She would wake herself at four, get up, stay up for twenty minutes and lie down again. If the rule was sound, it would be waiting.

She set the alarm on the clock radio for 04:00.

Coralie came up at ten and saw the figure from the doorway.

'Four.'

'For twenty minutes. Then I go back to bed.'

'I've forty-two residents. I'm paid to stop them doing that.'

'I'm not a resident.'

'No. They tell me when something's wrong.' Coralie had one hand on the frame. She looked at the card on the chest of drawers and did not ask what it was. 'I'll hear it go.'

'Shut your door.'

'I'll leave it,' Coralie said, and went across the landing.

* * *

The weight came at 02:51, and Lesca let it have her.

The alarm went at four. She got up, by the rule, and stayed up. Coralie's door was open, and the breathing behind it was not that of someone asleep. She went down in her coat and stood in the kitchen without putting the light on. The yard was white. Across it her mother's kitchen was dark, and would be for another hour. She counted her fingers at the window and made ten. She read the clock on the cooker, looked at the frost and read the clock again.

At twenty past four she went back up and lay down in the middle of the bed, on her back, with her hands outside the blanket.

It was waiting. It came down on the bone before she had her breath arranged, cold, with its smell of steel and old tobacco, and she kept her eyes open and gave it nothing. The red figures read 04:26. Then she no longer had the clock.

She had the panel, and both lamps lit. The chair was under her, and its arms were warm.

She looked down and counted ten. She turned her head to the right. The shelf was at her elbow with the buzzer on it, and beside the buzzer the envelope lay open, brown, with the university's name in the corner. The card was drawn half out.

She did not put out her hand. She had lost the room that way on Friday. She leaned, and read.

It was a white card, unruled. On it, in biro, in the hand she had written before she had a signature, was one word.

*left*

She knew the hand. It made the f as she made it, with the bar too low.

She looked away from it. She looked at the left lamp, which was lit, and at the right lamp, which was lit, and she counted the rivets in the top row of the wall and made twelve. Then she read the card again.

*left*

A thing dreamed would have changed. A thing remembered would have said right, since she had never in her life written the other. She had been taking the winch and the forty-one letters and the counter of the café for her own past, come back with the dates wrong. This card had no date to get wrong. It had been written once, in that chair, by her hand, by somebody who had seen the other lamp.

The arms of the chair were warm, and she had not been in it long enough to warm them. She did not turn her head to the left. The door was on that side, and she did not want to learn whether it was still shut.

She was more frightened than she had been at any time since the night of the flood, and she sat in the chair and went on reading the one word until the alarm took the room away at six.

She wrote before she moved. It came to three lines.

*1. Read the card. It says left. Read it twice. It says left.*

*2. My hand. Not my word.*

*3. It is not a memory. Somebody else wrote it.*

Then she got up. Her own card lay on the chest of drawers where she had left it, with its one word upwards. She slid it back into the envelope as far as the middle and no further, as the other lay on its shelf, and stood the envelope against the clock radio, open.
