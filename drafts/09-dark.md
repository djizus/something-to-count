# 9

On Monday at nine Guy Larrère stopped his car beside her in the square and held a pink slip of paper flat against the inside of his window.

Maylis was raking the gratings under the third tree. She leaned the rake on the trunk and came to the glass. Rain ran down it between her and the slip, and his exhaust blew warm against her knees. It was the mairie's leaf of Friday's delivery note, and the carbon had taken the stroke under the name better than the name.

He lowered the window a few centimetres.

'This was in my tray. One string of lamps. The commune ordered no lamps.'

'I ordered them.'

'With whose money?'

'Mine.'

'Then it's a gift to the commune from its employee. The council accepts a gift by deliberation or it comes down. I'll put it under any other business.' He turned the slip to look at it himself and turned it back. 'And this. Who taught you to sign like a prefect?'

'Nobody.'

'You've signed M. Lesca on my post for eighteen years, as your father did. I've four thousand of them in that building. Then on Friday I get this.' He laid a finger on the glass over the stroke. 'A hand like that belongs to somebody with something to sign. Use your own on the commune's paper.'

'It is my own. It came out like that.'

'Then see it doesn't.' He put the slip on the seat beside him. 'Sarthou wants a statement from you tomorrow, about last Monday's water. You raised that gate at the request of the association. The association has thirty-one members.'

'You telephoned at ten to four.'

'The association asked. I can't see that you'd remember a telephone.' He looked at her through the gap. 'They tell me you count your fingers at the café now. I'd not do that in front of a gendarme. You're not to worry about any of it.'

The window went up. He drove round to his space, under the ninth string, and went in with the slip in his hand.

She took the rake off the tree. Her right hand was shut on the handle, and she opened it and looked at it. The church clock struck nine behind her, and she counted.

* * *

Kévin Labat's van was behind the hall at noon, out of sight of the road, with the pump running in the back. He was asleep across the front seats in the leather jacket. She knocked on the glass with the key to the shed.

He sat up and looked at her before he wound the window down.

'I was wrong on Saturday,' she said. 'I thought you were getting my job.'

'Who told you that?'

'Nobody. I dreamed it. You were on the mower in my jacket.'

He rubbed his face with both hands. 'And you took it out on me awake.'

'Yes.'

He thought about that and then he leaned across and opened the passenger door. She got in out of the rain. The cab smelled of river and of coffee.

'You asked me on Wednesday if it was your own hand, in one,' he said. 'I've been thinking about it since. Why did you ask?'

'There's a room I've dreamed for twelve years. The box, in the barn. A few times a year, and I'm only ever sitting in it. Since the rib it's close every night, and I go under before I'm in. I want to be in it with my head on.'

'What for?'

'To look round.'

He did not laugh. He took his phone off the dashboard and turned it over in his fingers.

'There's a way in. It's what I do between tides. You set a clock for four in the morning. You get up, and you stay on your feet twenty minutes. No phone. Nothing to eat. Walk about. Then you lie down flat and you don't move a finger, and you go straight in with your head still on. That's how I got to the gutter.'

'Four.'

'Four or five. When you've had most of your sleep.' He put the phone back. 'And when you're in, count. Then read something, look away, read it again. If it's a dream, it'll have changed.'

'And if it hasn't?'

'It always has. If it didn't, I'd not know what to call it.' He looked at her. 'Is there anything to read, in the box?'

'There was a card. I wrote on it which lamp came on. They never asked for it.'

'You've still got it?'

'In the dresser. I never opened it.'

'Twelve years.' He shook his head. 'I'd have had that open in the car park.'

'I know what it says.'

'Then you'll know if it's changed,' Kévin said.

* * *

Coralie had not waited for the third box. She had rung the surgery in Dax herself on Saturday and written half past three across it, and at half past two she was in the yard with her car running.

The doctor put the film up on a lit screen. It was the eighth rib on the right, cracked through and not displaced. He said six weeks and no lifting, and asked how she slept. She said on her back. He said that was correct, and to go on with the breathing. Then he turned a form towards her and held out a pen.

She took it and watched the nib. It wrote M. Lesca, with the tall L, and stopped.

'What?' Coralie said, in the corridor.

'Nothing.'

'You looked at that pen as if it might go off.'

Coralie drove. The wipers went. At the Hontaube turning she said, without taking her eyes off the road, that she would not draw any more boxes, since it came to the same thing in the end and she had the telephone. Maylis said that the rib was hers. Coralie said that the appointment was hers, then, and she would keep the next one as well.

* * *

The envelope was in the kitchen dresser, in the folder with the guarantees for the cooker and the washing machine. It was white and plain, and the gum of the flap had gone brown. She had brought it home from the barn in the pocket of the yellow waterproof on a Thursday in June 2014 and put it in the folder that evening. It had not been asked for since.

Coralie was on earlies and had gone up at nine. Maylis sat at the table under the lamp and got her thumbnail beneath the flap. It lifted without tearing. Inside was a white card, unlined. In the middle of it, in pencil, in her own hand, it said *left*.

She looked at the window and counted her left hand. Then she read the card again. It said *left*.

That was the lamp that had come on. She had sat in a steel room with the door shut for three hours, and one lamp had come on, once, and she had written down which. She put the card back in the envelope and stood the envelope against the clock radio in the bedroom, where she would have it in the morning. She set the alarm for four.

'What's that for?' Coralie said, from her side, into the wall.

'I've to be up at four for twenty minutes.'

'For the commune?'

'No.'

Coralie turned over and looked at her in the red light of the figures, and at the envelope. 'For you, then,' she said. She sounded as though she would have liked to be glad of it. She turned back to the wall.

The weight came at 02:51. Maylis read the clock twice and let it take her, and it left her nothing.

At four the alarm went. She had her boots and the waterproof on over her T-shirt before she was awake, and she went out into the yard. It was raining. Her mother's kitchen was dark. She walked the yard from the gate to the shed door and back, with her forearm against her side, and counted the lengths. At the tenth she counted her fingers under the yard lamp. At twenty past by the church clock she went in, hung the waterproof up wet and lay down on her back with her hands outside the blanket.

No weight came. She had the ceiling, and the sound of Coralie breathing, and then she had neither.

A steel panel was in front of her at arm's length, with two lamps on it, and both were lit. She was sitting in a chair that was bolted to the floor. The smell was cold steel and old tobacco, and nothing lay on her chest.

She looked down. Her hands were on her knees. She touched off the left and then the right, and made ten, and they stayed ten.

The arms of the chair were warm. She had not been in it long enough to warm them.

She turned her head to the right. A shelf ran under the panel, with the buzzer on it, and beside the buzzer an envelope lay open with a card drawn half out. She leaned, and read. In the middle of the card, in pencil, in her own hand, it said *right*.

She looked away, at the left-hand lamp, and counted five. She read the card again. It said *right*.

It had not changed. It was her hand, and it was the wrong word, and it held. She had read the other on her own kitchen table seven hours before. Kévin had said that it always changed. He had said that if it did not he had no name for it.

The back of the chair was warm as well, the whole height of her spine.

She sat still. Then she began to remember things, one after another, as she might have remembered a week's work.

She remembered the end of the Port. She was walking up a ramp onto a grass bank four metres high, in frost, with a mallet in her hand. The bank ran where the last four houses should have been. Sarthou's hedge was not there, nor his house. His gatepost stood by itself at the foot of the bank with its number on it, and on the crest above was a bench with a brass plate.

She remembered Maïté Castagnet in a black coat, with a wreath on a stand beside her. She had bent and kissed Maïté on the cheek, in front of a great many people. Maïté had held her face still for it. When it was done she had taken a glove off and wiped the cheek with the bare hand, twice, looking at the stone.

She remembered the inside of Jeannot's tonne, by torchlight. Everything in it was coated in dried mud to above the door. Two gendarmes had the duckboards up, and under the duckboards were nets, fine as curtain, on four hoops, and a cool box with its lid taped down. She had been standing at the hatch with a folder, and one of the gendarmes had looked up and said, 'Madame le maire.'

The alarm went at six. It was Coralie's.

* * *

Maylis wrote before she moved, at the back of the shopping pad, with the pad against her knee.

*Tuesday. Up at four, walked the yard, lay down again. In the box. Both lamps on. The card there says right. Read twice. My writing.*

*Mine says left.*

*A bank where Sarthou's house is. Maïté C. in black, wiping my kiss off. Nets under the boards in Jeannot's tonne. They called me Madame le maire.*

She looked at what she had. The stone had her name on it. The sash had been on her. The pen on Friday had made a mayor's mark with her fingers round it. And a hand that wrote as hers did had sat in that chair and seen the other lamp.

She wrote one line more.

*There are two of us. She is the mayor.*

On Wednesday the square had stood full and waited for somebody to begin. It had not been waiting for the woman who raked it.

Coralie had left for the home. Maylis took her own card out of its envelope, read it once more and put it in the chest pocket of the waterproof with the yellow slip. She was in the van by half past seven with the wipers going.

She did not go up to the square. She drove down through the Port in the dark, past the home with its lights on, to where the road ended at the dyke. She stopped short of the end with the engine running and the headlamps on what was there. There was no ramp and no bench. The dyke was the old dyke, with the spring's patch of clay still bare on its face. The last house stood in front of it, hard against the bank, with a lamp lit over its door and the number 40 on the gatepost. The privet ran along the front at chest height. Sarthou was out in the rain in a cape with a pair of shears, clipping the top of it level.
