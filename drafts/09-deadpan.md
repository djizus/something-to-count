# 9

On Monday at nine Guy Larrère stopped his car beside her in the square, lowered the window a few centimetres and held a pink slip of paper up in the gap by one corner.

Maylis was sweeping, as on every Monday, left-handed. It was raining. She leaned the broom on her shoulder and read the slip through the gap. It was the mairie's leaf of Friday's delivery note.

'What's this?'

'A string of lights. Two hundred lamps. It's on the third tree.'

'The commune ordered no lights.'

'I did. On my card. It came to the mairie because that's where it hangs.'

'Then it's no paper of the commune's, and it's been in my tray since Friday on top of the prefecture.' He turned the slip round and looked at it himself. 'That's not what I'm asking you. I'm asking you this.' He put his thumbnail under the signature. 'Who taught you to sign like a prefect?'

'Nobody.'

'I've had your name on every chit in that mairie since 2008. M, full stop, Lesca, with your father's L on it. I could draw it for you. This one has an office and a secretary.' He held it nearer the light. 'It's good. I've been mayor eighteen years and mine isn't as good.'

'It came out like that.'

'They don't come out. They're practised. Somebody sat down with a sheet of paper and did that forty times.' He looked at her through the gap, and then at the broom. 'So there's the Lesca who rakes my parking space, and there's this one. Which of them did the university send back?'

The wipers went across twice.

'I'll tell you why I'm asking,' he said. 'Hontaube takes over the staff file on the first of January. They'll want a specimen signature for each name on it. I can't send them one employee with two hands. They'll think Peyrebarthe has been paying a ghost.'

'I'll sign the old way.'

'See that you do.' He pushed the slip out through the gap, and she took it. 'And keep that. It's yours, not the commune's. You're not to put in for the forty-seven ninety either.'

'I hadn't.'

'Then we understand each other.'

The window went up. He had been six minutes, and for once had not said he would be brief.

She folded the pink leaf and put it in the chest pocket of her waterproof with the yellow one. Then she finished the square, and emptied the seven buckets in the nave, and at eleven she was in Dax. The doctor sent her across the road for a picture. It showed a line through the seventh rib on the right. He charged thirty euros to tell her six weeks, on her back, and ten breaths an hour.

* * *

The envelope was in the top drawer of the chest of drawers, under the clock radio. It had been there since June 2014, beneath her passport and her father's watch.

It was brown, with the university's name printed in the corner. The woman in charge had given it to her with a card and told her to write one word, seal it and keep it until she was asked. Nobody had asked. The team had given her a hundred and eighty euros and a ham sandwich, and had left on the Saturday. Nobody in Peyrebarthe had asked her anything about that afternoon in twelve years, except whether she was still in there.

She knew what the card said. One lamp had come on, once, and she had seen which. She had not opened the envelope, because there was nothing in it to learn.

At two she sat on the bed with it and a kitchen knife. Larrère had asked which of them the university had sent back. This was the one thing that had come out of the box with her, and it had a hand on it from before any of this began.

She slit the flap. The card was white and unruled, the size of a postcard. On it was one word, in biro.

*left*

It was her own hand, the ordinary one, with the bar of the f too low. She read it twice. She took the two slips out of her pocket and laid them beside it on the blanket, the yellow and the pink, with the name that crossed itself. The same fingers had made all three. Only one of the three was hers.

She put the card back in the envelope and the envelope on the chest of drawers.

* * *

Kévin Labat's van was by the church at four, with the windows steamed. She knocked on the glass. He slept by day in eel season, and he wound the window down a hand's width without sitting up.

'You said you didn't want me.'

'I shouldn't have said it.'

'What had I done?'

'Nothing. I'd had a bad night, and you were in it. That's all it was.'

He thought about that. Then he sat up and wound the window the rest of the way.

'In it how?'

'On the mower. In my jacket.'

'I told you. I wouldn't be seen in it.' But he was pleased, she could tell, to have been in somebody's night. 'What do you want?'

'You said once you know you're in one, you can do what you like. How do you get in? On purpose. Not waiting for it.'

'What for?'

'There's something I want to read.'

He did not laugh. He counted it off for her on the hand he used for counting.

'You sleep till four. You set the phone for four, on buzz, under the pillow. You get up, right up, out of the room. Twenty minutes. No screen, no light if you can help it. Walk about. Then you go back and lie down on your back and you keep one thing in your head, the thing you want. You go straight in.' He shrugged. 'Four times in ten. It's the best there is.'

'And if you lie down and you can't move?'

'With something sat on you?'

She said nothing.

'That's the door,' Kévin said. 'People on the forum would pay for that. You don't push at it and you don't drop off. You keep your eyes on one thing and you wait. I've had it twice. I panicked both times.' He looked at her with some respect. 'How long have you had it?'

'A week.'

'Since the rib. You're on your back.' He nodded, as at a tide table. 'Then you're halfway in already.'

* * *

Coralie came in from the early shift and found the doctor's paper on the kitchen table. She read it. Then she took the pencil and ticked the Monday box on the shopping pad herself, since nobody else was going to.

'He says what I said.'

'He charged thirty euros for it.'

'I'd have taken twenty.' She saw the phone on the table with the alarm showing. 'What's at four?'

'I get up for twenty minutes. Then I come back.'

'On whose advice?'

'Kévin Labat's.'

'The doctor says rest, and you go to Kévin Labat for how to sleep.' Coralie put the pencil down. 'What do you want at four in the morning, Maylis?'

'To read something.'

'There's a lamp by the bed.'

'It isn't here.'

Coralie looked at her for some time. Then she looked at the envelope on the chest of drawers, which had been in a drawer for as long as she had known the drawer, and was now slit along the top.

'I'll feel you go,' she said. 'I always do.'

* * *

The weight came at 02:51 and Maylis let it take her.

The phone buzzed under the pillow at four. She got out of bed by stages, with her forearm against her side, and Coralie turned over and did not speak. She went down in her waterproof and walked the yard in the rain, from the lean-to to her mother's gate and back, for twenty minutes by the church clock. Her mother's kitchen was dark. A lamp came on behind the bedroom shutter while she was at the gate, and went off again. She kept one thing in her head, which was a card.

At twenty past she lay down again on her back, with her hands outside the blanket.

It was on her before she had her breath. It came down on the bone at its thirty-five kilos, a hand's width from the rib, with its cold and its smell of steel and old tobacco. She did not push. She did not let go either. She fixed her eyes on the red figures and took her breath in thirds, under the rib, where the weight could not reach it. The figures read 04:27. Then she had no clock to look at.

She had a steel panel a forearm's length from her face, with two lamps on it side by side. Both were lit.

She knew where she was. She had sat here for three hours in June 2014, for a hundred and eighty euros, and had been asked ever since whether she was still in it. The chair was bolted to the floor and narrower than it had been. The smell was the weight's smell, and nothing was on her chest. For the first time in a week the rib did not hurt.

She looked down, and her head went down. Her hands lay on her knees. She counted them, thumb first, and made five and five.

The door was on her left, shut, with a rubber seal round it and no handle on her side. In 2014 that had not troubled her. They had told her three hours and had opened it at three hours. She did not know who had the opening of it now.

Then she turned her head to the right, since that was where they had put the shelf. It was at her elbow. The buzzer was on it. Beside the buzzer lay a brown envelope with the university's name in the corner, open, and a white card drawn half out of it.

She read the card.

*right*

The bar of the f would have told her, but there was no f. The g told her. It had her tail on it, the one she had been marked down for at school.

Writing would not hold in a dream. She looked away at the lamps, the left one and the right one, both lit. In 2014 the left one had come on, alone, and she had written it down and had the card at home to prove it. She looked back.

*right*

It held. She was not dreaming it, then, and she had not written it. Somebody had sat in this chair with her hand and her schooling and had seen the other lamp. The arms of the chair were warm, and she had been in it two minutes.

She sat with her hands on her knees and her heart going, and she did the sum. There was a hand that wrote right and signed with a stroke that crossed itself. Larrère had said it was a hand with an office. There was a sash, and a square full of people waiting, and a stone with her name on it in the mayor's space. They had not given the mairie to her. They had given it to the one who wrote this card. That one had a village that turned her way and waited for her to begin. Maylis had a broom, and a key until January.

She was still looking at the word when the room went.

* * *

At six she wrote on the shopping pad before she moved, from the back, in the dark.

*Tuesday. In the box. The card says right. Read twice. My writing.*

*There are two of us. The other one is the mayor.*

She had three more things, and she wrote them under it in the order they came.

*The end of the Port. Sarthou's house is not there. A grass bank across the plot, a bench on top. His gatepost at the bottom with the 40 on it.*

*Maïté Castagnet in a black coat, by the stone. I kiss her on the cheek. She takes it. Then she wipes it off with her glove.*

*Jeannot's tonne, inside, by torch. The duckboards are up. Nets under them, green, fine as stocking, folded wet.*

She knew what the nets were for. Everybody at the gates knew that mesh.

Coralie was awake beside her and had been for some time.

'Did you read it?'

'Yes.'

'And?'

'It said what I thought it wouldn't.'

Coralie waited for more. When none came she got up and went down to put the coffee on.

It was Tuesday, and Sarthou wanted his statement at the house. On Saturday he had told her she knew which it was. In the night she had stood on a bench where its roof should have been. Maylis drove down to the Port at half past eight, past the care home and the shuttered fronts, to where the road ended against the dyke. Number 40 was on the gatepost, on its enamel plate. Behind the gatepost stood the house, with its shutters open and smoke coming from the chimney, and along the front of it Sarthou was clipping his privet in the rain.
