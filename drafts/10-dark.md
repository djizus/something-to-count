# 10

The council sat on the first Tuesday of the month at half past eight, fifteen members round the long table, and under the first deliberation Lesca had signed it rose at half past ten.

On Tuesday 1 December the fourth item was the prefecture's request for a protocol. Six thousand people would stand in the Port on the night of the twelfth, behind the dyke, and the prefect wished to know in writing how the gates would be worked while they stood there. Lesca had circulated a page. The gates would stay on their motors, and nobody would hold a key.

Guy Larrère asked for the floor. He had a ledger in front of him, bound in black cloth, with a label on the cover in his father's hand. The secretary set the timer to three and stood it beside the water jug.

'I'll be brief,' he said.

He opened the ledger at a ribbon. It was the association's book of manoeuvres, he said, kept since 1962. Each time a gate of the association was raised or lowered, the keyholder or the president wrote the date, the hour, the height of the river against the red line and by whose order. He turned it round on the table so that it faced her, and he read from it upside down, with his finger under the lines.

'March 1978. River forty below the line and falling. Raised sixty centimetres, to drain. Order of the president. February 1996. River at the line and rising. Lowered and pinned. Order of the president.' He turned a block of pages. 'There are four hundred and some. I have read them all. In each one the gate is raised on a falling river and shut on a rising one, since that is what a gate is. It is a door in a bank. It lets a meadow out and it keeps a river out.'

He had come to the last written page. Half of it was white.

'Night of the twelfth of December 2014. River ninety over the line and rising. Order of the president, by telephone. Keep shut.' He took his finger away. 'There is no line under that. The book has no opening in it for that night. Thirty-six houses are dry by a manoeuvre that nobody wrote down, and one man is not, and Madame le maire has the medal for it in her safe. Since 2016 there are motors, and floats, and no hand writes anything. I only wish this council to know what the motors are doing when they shut, which is what I ordered done by hand, and to understand that on the night of the'

The timer rang. He stopped on the word and closed the ledger on its ribbon.

In eleven Decembers he had explained the gates to her eleven times, and each time she had answered him. She had the answer by heart. It was thirty-six houses and forty-two beds against three hundred hectares of grass, and it took her under a minute.

She did not give it. She was looking at her own hands on the table, and what she had in them was a chain.

* * *

On Monday at six she had written *My hand. Not my word*, and had lain for a time without moving.

Somebody else sat in that chair. Whoever it was wrote as she did and had seen the other lamp. Lesca went through the notebook from the first page. It showed six entries received. A rib had come to her on the night after a Monday. A letter and a van had come after a Tuesday, a counter after a Friday, a hall after a Saturday. Each was a day, with rain in it, and each had reached her within a night or two of being lived. The register showed nothing sent.

She did not believe that a thing came one way only. If the other one's days arrived on her pillow, hers went somewhere. Hers on Monday would be nine weighbridge tickets and an invoice for fifteen hundred euros.

She decided at ten past six what she would take in with her instead, and wrote it under the entry as an order of ceremony.

*To be carried in tonight. Saturday 17 January 2015.*

*1. The prefect's car at the church, eleven o'clock. He gets out on the square side.*

*2. The television van by the pump, with its mast up. Two cameras.*

*3. The village in the square, to the back wall. G. Larrère beside the prefect, in the sash.*

*4. His speech. He says they tell him that in Peyrebarthe a person who is late is still in the box, and that on the night of the twelfth Mademoiselle Lesca was not late.*

*5. The medal. Red, white and blue ribbon. He pins it through the lapel and pricks his thumb.*

*6. The applause. The cameras come in.*

She began it at the car door. She did not begin it any earlier. Behind that Saturday was the night it was given for, and in the night there was a van. She left the van where it was and wrote nothing about it.

She rehearsed the six points all Monday. She went through them at her desk, between signatures, and on the telephone to the gravel company while the site manager looked for his tickets. At the hours she counted her fingers and then went through them again. At four she opened the safe and took out the case. The medal was bronze and heavier than a two-euro piece, and its ribbon had faded along the fold. She held it in her right hand, on the seam, until she could have drawn it. By the evening she could go from the car door to the cameras in four minutes without losing a point, and she had done it thirty times.

'You've been saying something all through supper,' Coralie said. 'Your lips are going.'

'A speech.'

'For the twelfth?'

'For tonight.'

Coralie put down her fork. She did not ask, and it cost her something not to.

* * *

The weight came at 02:51, and she let it have her. The alarm went at four. She stood in the kitchen for twenty minutes in her coat, with the frost on the yard, and went through the six points twice. At twenty past she lay down on her back in the middle of the bed.

It was waiting, as it had been on Sunday. It came down on the bone. On Sunday she had held her eyes open against it until the clock went, and there had been a gap, and then the panel. She meant to have no gap.

She began at the car door. The prefect got out on the square side. She kept her breath where it was and let the weight settle to its full thirty-five kilos, and she went on to the van by the pump. Her feet left her first. They did not go numb. They went as a thing goes that has been put down. Her hands went next, on the blanket, and then her arms to the shoulder. She had the village in the square, to the back wall. The red figures read 04:31, and she could read them. Her chest rose and fell under the weight by itself, slowly, as a sleeper's does, and she was not asleep. She had the speech. The ceiling was plaster, with the mark of the lampshade on it. Then it was plaster with a seam across it. Then the seam had rivets. She did not lose it at any point. She watched it become steel, and she knew her own name and the date the whole way.

The panel was in front of her, with both lamps lit. The weight had stayed behind on the bed.

She counted ten. She did not look at the card. She sat in the chair with her forearms on its warm arms and went through the six points, from the car door to the cameras, and she had every one. The thumb bled a little on the ribbon. She had forgotten that since 2015, and it came now with the rest.

When it was done she sat still. Then her hands were cold, and wet, and there was a chain in them.

She remembered it. It was dawn, with enough light to see the links. She was crouched in mud at the back of a white Trafic that had gone down to its axles on the track out to the tonne. The chain was the commune's, the heavy one from the shed, and she was working its hook through the towing eye with fingers she could hardly feel. The other end went to the drawbar of the commune's tractor, which stood behind her on the firm ground with its engine running. Jeannot Castagnet was standing at the driver's door in his waders. He was telling her that her father would have hooked it from the other side.

She pulled the chain tight with both hands. Her palm ran along the links and closed on them, and nothing in it tore.

The alarm went at six.

* * *

She wrote it before she moved. It came to nine lines, and she took longest over the last three.

*7. A chain, at dawn. The track to his tonne. His van down to the axles. My hands hooking it to the tractor. He stood by and talked.*

*8. She went down the track.*

*9. Over there somebody went to the end of it and got him out.*

She lay with the notebook on her chest. In twelve years she had been down that track many times with her eyes shut. She had walked it for the prefecture's inquiry and for the gendarmerie's, on a plan, with a ruler, and each time she had come to the end of it after the water. She had not once had the van in her hands and a chain on it, and him alive beside it giving instructions.

Coralie was in the doorway. She had been there for some time.

'I came in at half past four,' she said. 'Your eyes were open.'

'I was awake.'

'You weren't. I said your name.' She stopped, and Lesca waited to hear which name, and did not hear. 'I held my hand in front of your mouth to see that you were breathing. I do it for the residents. I've not had to do it in my own house.'

'I was breathing.'

'Six to the minute. I counted.' Coralie looked at the notebook. 'You were smiling at the ceiling. In eleven years I've not seen that either. I don't know which of the two I minded.'

She went down, and Lesca heard the tap run.

The nine weighbridge tickets came to the mairie by courier at eleven, addressed to Monsieur le Maire. All nine were stamped before seven o'clock. Lesca raised the invoice herself and signed it. She rehearsed nothing that day. At the hours she counted her fingers, and twice between hours she found her right hand lying half shut on the desk, as it would have lain on a chain.

* * *

'Thank you, Monsieur Larrère,' Lesca said.

The council waited for the answer. Several of its members had the figures ready on their own lips, as a congregation has a response.

'The protocol is as circulated. Those in favour.'

Eleven hands went up. Larrère's was not one of them. He had his on the ledger, and he was looking at her across it as he had not looked at her in some years, with the face of a man whose opponent has failed to turn up.

'You've no reply,' he said. He had no time left to say it in, and the secretary's hand moved towards the timer and stopped.

'Not tonight.'

'It's in the book whether you reply or not.'

'I know what's in your book. I know what isn't.' She turned the page of her agenda. 'Item five.'

Item five was Hontaube's request to open talks on joining Peyrebarthe, and it was referred to a working group of three in under four minutes. Item six was the invoice to the gravel company, which carried. The church clock struck ten through the shutters. She counted her fingers below the edge of the table, left and then right, and made ten, and no member asked for it to be minuted.

Under any other business there was nothing. In eleven years there had always been something, because she had put it there.

She looked at the clock over the door. Then she looked round the table, at fourteen faces, and none of them was his. He was in a box file in the cupboard next door, with twelve envelopes, and he was on the stone. And somewhere he was standing at the door of a white Trafic in the first light, telling a woman with a chain that she had it on the wrong side.

'The agenda is exhausted,' she said. 'The sitting is closed.'

Nobody stood up. The council of Peyrebarthe had risen at half past ten on the first Tuesday of each month since May 2015, and its members had arranged their evenings by it. The secretary looked at the clock, and at the mayor, and wrote in the register that the sitting was closed at eleven minutes past ten.
