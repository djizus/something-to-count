# 10

On Monday the mayor of Peyrebarthe drew up what she would have in her head that night, as she would have drawn up an agenda.

She had the principle from her own minutes. Everything that had come to her in a week had been in somebody's day first. A letter had been signed for in the rain, a hand counted on a café counter, a ham shouted for. Somebody had carried those things about until bedtime, and they had arrived in Lesca's notebook by morning. That somebody wrote *left* in Lesca's hand. It followed that whatever Lesca carried about until bedtime went the other way, and for a week the other one had been getting it unsorted.

Lesca did not send out papers unsorted. She chose one item and gave it the day.

The item was the morning of Saturday 7 February 2015, when the prefect of the Landes had pinned a medal on her in the square.

She took it in order, beginning at the church clock. The ceremony was called for eleven. At eleven the square held four hundred people, a platform borrowed from Hontaube and a television van with its mast up, and no prefect. He came at twenty to twelve in a grey car. He got out, shook her hand and told the microphone that he begged the commune's pardon, he had been still in the box. The square laughed. He had been in the department three weeks and somebody had briefed him.

Then he read the citation. It ran to four lines, and she had it by heart. On the night of 12 to 13 December 2014, acting alone and on her own initiative, the commune's employee had opened the sluice gate of the low meadows and so preserved forty dwellings, a care home and the lives of those within them. It did not say against whose order. Guy Larrère stood at the prefect's left in the mayor's sash and clapped with the tips of his fingers.

The pin went through the lapel of a coat that was Coralie's. The prefect said one sentence about Jean Castagnet, with his head lowered, and the square lowered its head with him. Then they played the anthem off a loudspeaker on the mairie steps, and a woman with a furred microphone asked her how it felt. She had said that the houses were dry. It was broadcast that evening and on six evenings after.

That was the item, and it took eleven minutes to go through from the clock to the microphone. She went through it at each hour, after the fingers. She went through it at the mairie with the medal out of the safe and open in its case on the blotter, to be sure of the ribbon, which was red, white and blue in seven stripes.

There was no van in it.

That needed no arranging as far as the ceremony went. Nobody in the square that morning had said the word. It was the rest of the day that wanted watching, the ten minutes between one run and the next, when she was signing something or looking out at the back of the stone. Twice on Monday afternoon she found the track in her head, in the wet, and what stood at the end of it. Each time she put down her pen, counted ten fingers and began again at the church clock.

By six she had been through it eleven times, and she had kept it clean.

* * *

Coralie found the case on the kitchen table at seven, with the lid up.

'What's that doing out of the mairie?'

'I'm going over the day.'

'Which day?'

'The seventh of February. The prefect.'

Coralie took her coat off and hung it up. It was not the same coat. 'You wore mine. Yours had the commune's name across the back, and they wanted you in something plain for the cameras.' She turned the case round on the oilcloth with one finger and looked at the medal. 'He put the pin through the lining. I mended it.'

'I'd forgotten the lining.'

'You've forgotten the week, then. It was the week I took my pillow across the landing.' She shut the lid. 'On the Monday you shouted four times. On the Saturday they gave you this. I stood in the second row and clapped, and I'd had nine hours' sleep in five nights.'

'I didn't know it was the same week.'

'You weren't counting. I was.' Coralie pushed the case back across the table. 'Why are you going over it?'

'So as to have it right.'

'For the twelfth?'

'For tonight.'

Coralie looked at her as she looked at a resident who had given the wrong year. She did not ask. She had made a habit of not asking about that winter, and Lesca had always put it down to tact.

'There's soup,' she said.

* * *

Lesca set the alarm for four, by her third amendment, and at 02:51 the weight came and she let it have her.

At four she got up and stood in the dark kitchen for twenty minutes. She went through the item once more at the window, from the clock to the microphone. Then she went up and lay down on her back.

Until that night she had gone into the room as a thing is carried in. The weight had come down, she had held on under it, and at some point the clock had been taken from her. This time she did it in order. She kept the square in front of her, with four hundred people in it, and let the rest of herself go a part at a time. Her feet went first. She told them nothing and they stopped answering. Then her legs went, and her hands on the blanket. She was awake above a body that was asleep, and when the weight came it did not drop. It settled on the bone as a load settles on a trailer when the straps are let off. She was not afraid of it. She had made room.

The red figures read 04:31. She shut her eyes, which she had not dared do on any other night, and when she opened them she had the panel and both lamps.

She had come through with her head clear and the item whole. She counted ten. The card on the shelf said *left*, and she read it twice, out of procedure. Then she sat in the warm chair and went through the seventh of February from the church clock to the furred microphone, without leaving anything out and without letting anything in. It was like reading the minutes of the last sitting to a council that could not be seen. When she had finished she began again.

She had been through it three times when the alarm took the room at six.

* * *

She wrote before she moved, with the notebook over her face.

*Night of Monday 30 November to Tuesday 1 December. Before moving. Went in awake, 04:31. Item delivered three times, complete.*

Then she wrote what she had woken with, and she was some time over it.

It was dawn, and cold enough for her breath to show. She was on the track that crossed the low meadows to the tonnes, at the far end, where it gave out in the mud. A white Trafic stood there, sunk to its axles, with its nose towards the tonne. The commune's tractor stood behind it with the engine running. She had a chain in her hands. They were her own hands, in the commune's work gloves, and they passed the hook through the towing eye under the Trafic's rear bumper and dropped the pin, and tried it twice. Jeannot Castagnet stood beside the van in his waders with his hands in his pockets, telling her which gear to pull in. Then she was up on the tractor and the chain came taut, and the van came backwards out of the mud a wheel at a time.

*1. The track, far end. Dawn. His Trafic to the axles. I chain it and tow it out with the tractor. He is there, standing, giving instructions.*

She knew the place. She knew that van at the end of that track as she knew her own front door. She had left it out of the item eleven times on Monday, and it had come back to her from the other side with a chain on it.

She lay with the notebook on her chest and worked it through as she would have worked through an estimate.

The other one had the same hand. She had sat in the same chair on the same afternoon. She had been the commune's employee, with the key, on the night of the flood. And at the end of that night, at first light, she was at the far end of the track with the tractor, pulling his van out, and he was standing up beside it telling her she was doing it wrong.

She had gone down the track, then. She had knocked.

That was why he had gone on to write twenty-nine more letters. That was why he had been laughing at a van window in the rain and shouting for a ham on Saturday. It was why there was a parking space in front of that mairie and no stone.

Lesca lay still. The Port stood, forty houses and a home, and she had a medal for it in a case on the kitchen table. Somebody with her hands had something else. She had a man standing in the mud at dawn, telling her which gear.

She did not ask where the water was. The ground in the picture was mud to the axles and no more than mud, and she wrote *mud* in the minute and went no further into it.

*2. She went down the track and knocked. He is alive there because of it.*

She read the two lines over. Her mother's light came on across the yard and showed on the ceiling. She found that she was crying, flat on her back, without any sound. It ran into her ears. She had not cried since the funeral, where she had not been entitled to.

* * *

On Tuesday she went through the item eleven times more, since nothing in her minutes told her how long a thing took to cross. The van stayed out of it. It was easier to keep out now that it had a chain on it.

The council of Peyrebarthe sat on the first Tuesday of the month at half past six. Under its present mayor it had risen at half past eight for eleven years, to the minute, with or without its agenda.

Fourteen members were present on 1 December. At seven Lesca counted her fingers on the open register. Nobody asked for it to be minuted, it having been minuted once. The fourth item was the maintenance contract for the motors on the three gates, four thousand two hundred euros a year, for renewal. Guy Larrère asked for the floor. Marie-Jo was not on the council and had sent no timer, and the secretary set the one from the cupboard to three minutes and stood it on the table.

'I'll be brief,' Larrère said. 'Before the council votes money for the gates, it might help the chair to have it clear what a gate is.'

He drew the water jug towards him, and two glasses. He set the glasses a hand apart and stood his agenda on its edge between them.

'That's the river,' he said, of the left-hand glass. 'That's the meadows. That's your gate.' He poured into the river. 'Now. The river comes up. The gate's shut. The meadows are dry, and whatever's in them is dry.' He filled the glass to the brim. 'What counts is which side the water's on when you lift it, and who's had notice. A gate is not a tap, Madame le maire. You don't turn it on because the sink's full.'

He lifted the agenda. Nothing happened, the glasses being glasses. He poured from the one into the other to show what he meant, and some went on the register.

In eleven years she had sat through this in six forms. She had given him each time the look she kept for the prefecture's circulars. This time she watched the water go from one glass to the other, and she thought that somewhere he had explained it to a woman who had no need of it explained. That one had left the gate alone and walked.

The timer rang. He was holding the jug.

'Thank you, Guy,' Lesca said.

The secretary looked up. Larrère put the jug down. He had not been thanked in that room since April 2015, and he looked round the table to see who had heard it.

The contract carried by thirteen votes to one. Two items remained, and any other business.

'They'll keep till January,' Lesca said. 'The sitting is closed.'

Nobody moved. Thirteen councillors sat on with their agendas open at the fifth item, which was the school boiler. The clock on the council room wall said eleven minutes past eight. The secretary looked at it, and then at the mayor, and then she wrote in the register that the sitting had risen at 20:11.
