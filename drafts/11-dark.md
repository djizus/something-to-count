# 11

On Wednesday Maylis went into the café at noon, when Jeannot Castagnet would be at the counter and the card table not yet full.

He was there, in his day jacket, with a glass of white. Two men sat by the stove. She stood beside him at the zinc and did not ask for anything.

'By telephone,' Jeannot said, without turning his head. 'That's what you gave my cousin yesterday. He read it out to me. Asked by whom, the keyholder states by telephone. A telephone hasn't a name, little Lesca.'

'It has. I didn't give it.'

'No. You were told not to.' He drank. 'So the file stays open, and your rib stays in it, and he clips his hedge.'

She had the words ready. She had gone over them on the mower all morning, and they came out lower than she had meant, and steady.

'There are nets under the duckboards in your tonne. Green, fine as stocking. You fold them wet. And what comes out of them goes to Irun in Kévin Labat's van, in two blue boxes, on the night tides.'

He put the glass down. He did not look at the men by the stove. He looked at the shelf of bottles behind the counter and then at her hands, which were flat on the zinc and still.

'Who's been down my hatch?'

'Nobody.'

'Kévin's told you.'

'Kévin's told me nothing. He doesn't know I know.'

'Then how?'

'I saw the boards up.'

'When? There's a padlock on that hatch, and I'm in it from dusk.' He waited, and she had no answer that she could say in a café. 'And what do you want for it?'

'The complaint at the brigade. Take it back. And the one in the mayor's tray.'

'Or?'

'The number of the men in green is on a poster in the mairie porch. I walk past it with the broom.'

He was quiet for as long as it took one of the men by the stove to shuffle and deal. She had known him since she was a child at the fence with her father. He had sent the mairie forty-one letters, and she had signed for most of them, and in eighteen years he had not once looked at her as he looked now. It was how he looked at Larrère's car.

'Four winters you've held that key and looked the other way,' he said. 'For Kévin, for me, for the others. For nothing. I've said so at this counter. I said the Lesca girl is the one straight thing on that dyke.'

'I never had a file with my rib in it.'

'I put the rib in for you.'

'I didn't ask you to.'

'No. Nobody asks you, do they?' He took coins out of his pocket and laid them by the glass, which was half full. 'Friday. When the mairie's open. I'll take back the forty-first, and I'll go and see my cousin.' He did not move the glass along the counter. He pushed it away from both of them, towards the owner. 'Your father stopped four turns short for me and never said a word about it.'

'He never got a word for it either. Not in forty letters.'

Jeannot buttoned his jacket. At the door he turned round.

'Good day to you, Maylis,' he said.

The owner took the half glass off the counter and tipped it into the sink.

'He's not left one before,' he said.

Jeannot had not used her name before either. He had called her little Lesca since she was six. She stood at the counter until his van had gone, and then she counted her fingers, though it was not the hour, and they were five and five. It was the first thing in Peyrebarthe that had been done because she said so. She had done it with something that was not hers.

* * *

The rain stopped at one, for the first time in nine days, and she got the ride-on out for the last cut of the year.

She had been up at four on three nights running. On Tuesday she had walked the yard and lain down, and it had put her under before she had the room. On Wednesday the same. She had slept five hours in each of the three nights and none of them whole.

The grass round the war memorial was long and wet. Four stone bollards stood at its corners with a chain slung between them. She took the mower round the outside, then in along the church wall, with the engine at half throttle and the warmth of it coming up through the seat. At two the clock struck. She took the ten breaths and counted her hands on the wheel.

After that she had the wall going by, and the cut line in the grass, and then she did not have them.

Something hit her under the right arm. It was the wheel. The mower had stopped dead, and the engine was labouring, and a hand that was not hers came past her and turned the key.

'You were asleep,' Kévin Labat said. He was out of breath. 'I watched you come thirty metres across that grass with your chin on your chest. I shouted.'

The front of the mower was against the nearest bollard. The bollard had gone over at the base and lay in the grass with the chain pulled tight across it. Behind it were the thirty-one names. She sat and held her side with her forearm until she could get air to the bottom.

'That's the fours,' he said. 'I should have told you. I sleep in the day. You don't.'

He got his arms round the bollard and stood it up. It would not stay, and he laid it down again.

'Castagnet came to my van at one,' he said. He was not out of breath now. 'He says you know what's under his boards. He says you know who drives it. You told me you'd never looked.'

'I haven't.'

'Then how?'

'I saw them up. In the box, on Tuesday. I read the card first, twice. It didn't change.'

He stared at her.

'You saw his nets in there. And you came out and used them on him.'

'Yes.'

'And me.'

'I used your name.'

'It's the same thing.'

'I know it is.'

He walked a few steps off, towards his van, and came back. He was thirty, and nobody had frightened him at the gates in four winters.

'A dream doesn't know where a man keeps his nets,' he said. 'I told you I'd no name for it. I don't want to know what else you've got in there about me. Don't tell me.' He looked at the bollard. 'What did it get you?'

'He's taking the complaint back.'

'So it works.' He said it with dislike, and with something else. 'If you go again tonight, don't let it take you first. Stay on one thing the whole way down. Go in with your eyes open, like I said, and see what's there when you haven't been asleep.'

He did not offer to help her load the bollard. She did it herself, with the chain, left-handed.

* * *

Coralie found her asleep at the kitchen table at eight, with her head on the shopping pad. Two boxes on it were still empty.

'There's a bollard in the back of the van.'

'I hit it.'

'With the mower. Marie-Jo rang me at the home.' Coralie stood over her. 'You fell asleep on a machine with blades.'

'For thirty metres.'

'Don't give me the measurement.' She put her hand flat between Maylis's shoulders and left it there. 'I've not asked what the four o'clock is. I said it was yours and I was glad. I'm not glad of it today.'

'One more night.'

'That's what the residents say. About the stairs.'

She took her hand away and lit the gas under the soup.

* * *

The weight came at 02:51, and Maylis let it take her. The phone buzzed at four. She walked the yard in the rain, which had come back, and at twenty past she lay down on her back.

It was on her before she had her breath. She did what Kévin had said. She fixed on the red figures and went on reading them, and she did not let go and did not push. Her feet went from her, and then her hands on the blanket. Her chest went on rising under the thirty-five kilos by itself. The figures read 04:29. She kept them. They went from four figures to two red points, side by side, and she watched them do it, and they were the lamps. She had not been away at all.

She counted five and five. The card on the shelf still said *right*. The chair was warm along the whole of her back.

Then it came, and it came in order.

A black car drew up at the church and a prefect got out on the square side, in his cap and his white gloves. A television van stood by the pump with a mast run up from its roof. The square was full to the back wall, in winter coats. Guy Larrère stood beside the prefect in the sash, with his hands behind him, and he was not the one they were looking at. The prefect spoke. He said they told him that in Peyrebarthe a person who came late was said to be still in the box, and that on the night of the twelfth Mademoiselle Lesca had not been late. The square laughed, and it was not at her. He took a medal from a case, bronze, on a ribbon of blue, white and red, and pinned it through her lapel, and pricked his thumb doing it. There was a spot of blood on the white glove. The square clapped. Two cameras came in close, and behind the cameras her mother was standing with her bag held in front of her.

It ran from the car door to the cameras without a break, as nothing else had. The stone and the sash and the water had come in pieces. This had been put in order, by somebody, to be seen.

She sat in the chair with the medal's weight still on her lapel. In eighteen years the commune had given her a waterproof and a key. In that square no car had stopped beside her to say how her father would have stood. She would have sat there a long time.

Something came in behind it that had not been put in order.

It was night, and raining hard. Two headlamps lit the rain and, at the end of them, at the far end of a track, the back of a white van. Low on its left-hand door was a reflector, and it was burning red in the lights. It was round. It was the kind that belongs on a trailer, and it had been screwed on crooked. She saw nothing else of the van. Her right hand was on a winch handle, wet, and it was turning.

The alarm went at six.

* * *

She wrote at the back of the pad, with the pad on her knee.

*Thursday. Went in awake, no gap. The prefect, a medal, television. The whole square. He said I was not late. It was all in order, as if she had laid it out for me.*

*After it, not laid out. A reflector on the back of a van, in headlamps, rain, the end of a track. Round, crooked. My hand on the winch.*

She read the two down. Then she turned back a page, to the bank and the kiss and the nets. Maïté Castagnet had been in black. Two gendarmes had had the boards up in his tonne, and he had not been there to stop them. A line was cut on the stone above her name.

*He is dead there. It was his van in her lights. She drove into him.*

She could make nothing of the winch, and left it.

Coralie was watching her from the pillow.

'You want something,' Coralie said. 'I can see it on you. I've waited sixteen years to see that.'

'Yes.'

'What is it?'

'Nothing I can have.'

She was in the commune's van by seven, in the dark, with the wipers going. She drove down through the Port and along the dyke to the gate, and stopped there with her engine running and her headlamps on the mouth of the track. The river stood a metre below the red line.

She counted her hands on the wheel by the light of the dashboard, and made ten.

Lights came up the track from the low meadows, slowly, throwing mud. It was the white Trafic, coming off its night. It climbed onto the dyke road and passed her at the width of a hand. Jeannot was at the wheel. He did not lift a finger from it.

Then the back of it was in her headlamps, going away towards the Port. Low on the left-hand door was a reflector. It was round, and screwed on crooked, and it burned red until the rain took it.
