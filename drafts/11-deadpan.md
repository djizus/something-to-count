# 11

On Wednesday she went into the box with her eyes shut.

The weight had come at 02:51 and she had let it take her. The phone buzzed at four. She walked the yard for twenty minutes in the rain and lay down again on her back, and this time she did not wait to be flattened. She let herself go a part at a time, as Kévin had said, with her head still on. Her feet went, and then her legs, and then the hands on the blanket. She was awake over a body that was asleep, and when the weight came it had nothing to startle. It settled on the bone and stayed there. The rib let her breathe under it. She read the red figures at 04:29, read them again, and shut her eyes on them.

When she opened them she had the panel and both lamps.

She counted five and five. The card on the shelf said *right*. The chair was warm up the whole of her back. She had come to look round, and she had not finished turning her head when the square arrived.

It came as the bank and the kiss had come on Tuesday, but those had been scraps. This was entire. It began with the church clock at eleven and it did not skip.

She was standing on a platform in front of the mairie, in a dark coat that was too narrow across the shoulders. The square was full. A television van was parked by the church with a mast up from its roof. For forty minutes nothing happened, and four hundred people waited without going home. Then a grey car drew up and a man got out of it in a uniform with oak leaves on the cap. He shook her hand. He told the microphone that he begged the commune's pardon, he had been still in the box.

The square laughed. It was the joke she had been told to her face for twelve years, and it was pointed the other way. They were laughing at a prefect for keeping her waiting. Nobody in that square had a car window between himself and her.

He read from a sheet. She had her name out of it, twice, and the word courage, and the rest went by as a sermon does. Then he took a medal out of a case, on a ribbon of red, white and blue, and pinned it through her lapel, and he was some time finding the cloth. Guy Larrère was in the front row, clapping. They played the anthem off a loudspeaker. A woman with a furred microphone asked her how it felt.

It was eleven minutes long, and she had all of it. Somebody had gone over it until it was smooth.

There was something behind it that had not been gone over.

It came through after the anthem, with nothing leading to it. It was night, and rain on a windscreen, and wipers. Headlights lay along a track. At the end of the track, low down, a red reflector stood in the lights. It was the size of a playing card and it did not move. Then there was a hand on a winch handle in the dark, a right hand, hers, wet, turning fast.

That was all there was. It was gone before she could look at it twice, and the square came round again from the church clock.

At six she wrote on the pad before she moved.

*Wednesday. Went in awake, 04:29. The prefect gave her a medal. The square full, the television there. He made the box joke about himself and they laughed.*

*After it, a van's reflector in headlights, end of a track, raining. Then my hand on a winch.*

She looked at the first entry for some time. Then she wrote three words under it, and they were the first of that kind on the pad.

*I want that.*

* * *

On Tuesday she had sat at Sarthou's kitchen table, with the dyke filling his window, and given her statement. She had said that the order to raise the gate came by telephone at ten to four from the president of the association, who was also the mayor. Sarthou had written it down, read it back to her and remarked that it made his file a page longer.

Jeannot Castagnet had it by Wednesday morning. He was at the counter of the café at eight, in from the tonne, with mud to the knees of his waders and a coffee in front of him. Two men were at the table by the stove.

'Little Lesca,' he said. 'You gave him Larrère. By name, and the time of the call. I'd not have thought it of you.'

'It's what happened.'

'It's what happened for eighteen years, and nobody's said it to a gendarme.' He pushed the sugar towards her. 'Have a coffee. The commune owes me two now.'

'Withdraw it, Jeannot.'

'Withdraw what?'

'The complaint. And forty-one.'

He laughed, with his head back. 'I've only now got his name in it.'

'It's a file with my rib in it and your cousin's pension. Larrère will get a letter from the association, signed by himself. I'll get asked which hand I sign with. Withdraw it.'

'No.'

The church clock struck eight. She counted her left hand against her thigh, where the stove table could not see it, and took her time over the breaths. Then she said it, low, with her eyes on the coffee machine.

'There are nets under the duckboards of your tonne. Green, fine as stocking. You fold them wet. And I know whose van takes what's in them to Irun.'

Jeannot did not move. He had the cup halfway up, and it stayed there.

'Who's been in my tonne?'

'Nobody.'

'Those boards have a padlock under the third one. Who's been in it?'

'I've told nobody. Not the men in green, not Sarthou. I'd sooner not.'

He put the cup down in its saucer without a sound.

'Your father would never have said that to me.'

'My father stopped four turns short for you. I've looked the other way at that gate for four winters and had nothing for it. This is the first thing I've asked.'

He looked at her. He had looked at her in that café since she was tall enough to see over the counter, and had called her little Lesca since before that. He turned on his stool to do it now, the whole of him, as he would have turned for a man.

'That's not like you, Lesca,' he said.

He had left the little off. She heard it go.

'Will you withdraw it?'

'I'll think about it.'

It was what she had said to Marie-Jo in 2020. He put two euros on the counter for his own coffee and none for hers, and went out to the Trafic in his waders. The two men by the stove watched him go, and then looked at her.

She had not been in his tonne. Somebody had, with a torch, and had the boards up. She sat over the coffee and considered that. A man's duckboards are not taken up by torchlight while he is sitting on them. Maïté had been in black, and had wiped her cheek.

* * *

The last cut of the year was on the commune's sheet for the first Wednesday of December, weather allowing. At two the rain held off, and she took the ride-on mower round the lawn in front of the mairie.

She had been up at four on three mornings. The lawn was forty metres by twelve, and the mower did it in strips. On the ninth strip she was in the square with four hundred people and a prefect looking for the cloth of her lapel, and on the tenth she was nowhere.

The mower found the bollard without her. It was one of the two that marked the mayor's parking space, cast iron, set in concrete in 1996. The deck rode up it and the engine stalled. She woke with the wheel in her chest, on the left, and the bollard lying over at the angle of a man leaning on a bar.

Kévin Labat got to her before anyone came out of the café. He had been asleep behind the hall, and he had his boots on unlaced.

'Are you hurt?'

'No.'

'You were asleep. I saw your head go.' He looked at the bollard. 'That's the alarm. I said twenty minutes at four. I didn't say three days of it on a machine with blades.'

They got the deck off between them, she with her left arm. He stood the bollard up as far as it would go, and it leaned.

'Jeannot's been to see me,' he said. He did not look at her. 'He says you know who drives to Irun.'

'I didn't give him a name.'

'He didn't need one. There's one van.' Kévin wiped his hands down his jacket. 'Four winters you never looked. You told me so on Wednesday. So who looked for you?'

'Nobody. I've not been near his tonne.'

'Then how do you know what's under his boards? I don't know what's under his boards. He brings them up to the track in a bucket. I've never been let past the hatch.'

'It was in the box,' she said. 'On Tuesday. I saw them.'

He did not laugh at that either. He stood with the mower between them and worked it out, and she watched him get to the end of it.

'It was true, though,' he said. 'He went white. You saw a true thing in there that you'd no way to know.' He looked at his own hand, and did not count it. 'That's not what I've got. I got as high as a gutter. What have you got, Maylis?'

'I don't know yet.'

'Does it know about me?'

'It had you on a mower.'

He looked at the mower, and at the bollard.

'I'd go back to bed,' he said. 'I mean it. In the daytime, with the light on.'

She entered it on the sheet as one bollard, struck, and the lawn as half cut.

* * *

Coralie had it at the home by four. A resident's daughter had telephoned it in.

'You went to sleep on the mower,' she said, with her coat still on. 'In the square. In front of the café.'

'For one strip.'

'I've been asking you for sixteen years what you want. I'd have settled for anything. A holiday. A dog.' She put the phone down on the table between them, with its alarm showing. 'Now you want something. I can see you wanting it. It gets you up at four and it's got you writing on the back of my shopping, and I don't know what it is.'

'I can't say it yet.'

'Is it here?'

Maylis looked at the pad. It lay face up, with the two boxes of Monday still empty on it.

'No,' she said.

Coralie took her coat off then, and hung it up, and did not ask where.

* * *

She had not gone down to the gate after dark since December 2022. At a quarter past five she took the commune's van down through the Port and out along the dyke road, in the rain, with her lights on.

She wanted the end of the track by headlight. She had seen it that way at half past four in the morning, from a seat she had never sat in, and she meant to look at it from her own.

She was a hundred metres short of the gate when lights came up behind her. They sat on her bumper, and then the horn went once, and she pulled in against the bank. The white Trafic went by with mud up to its door handles and Jeannot at the wheel, dressed for the night. He did not lift a finger from it. Then he was in front of her, and her headlights were on the back of his van.

It had a red reflector low on each side. The left one was the size of a playing card, with the bottom corner broken off and the screw showing.

She read it. She looked away at the gauge on the gate pier, where the river stood a metre below her father's line, and counted five against the wheel. She looked back and read it again. It was the same reflector, and it had the same corner off it.

She had the rest on the pad. Maïté was in black there and would not keep her kiss. His tonne had been gone through with a torch, which is not done while a man is using it. And somebody with her hands had come down this track at night, in rain, with her lights full on the back of his van. A driver does not carry a reflector about in her head unless she was coming at it fast.

They had given the other one a medal. Maylis did not know what for. She knew now what had come behind it, and that it had been left out on purpose. The winch she could not place.

The Trafic turned down the track. She did not follow it. She sat on the dyke with the engine running and watched the reflector go out along her lights to the end, where it stopped, and stood, and did not move.
