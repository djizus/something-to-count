# 12

On Thursday the mayor of Peyrebarthe fell asleep in the chair during the budget, and the finance committee, after some discussion, went on without her.

The committee met on the first Thursday of December, at half past nine, to go through the draft for the year to come. It had five members, and the mayor presided. The secretary read the draft aloud by chapter and line. The operating section for 2027 stood at one million one hundred and forty thousand euros, of which the lake brought in four hundred thousand. Lesca had the pages in front of her with her own figures in the margin, in pencil. She had been up at four on five mornings running.

She followed the secretary through staff costs. She followed her through insurance and into general expenses. At water she was still there. At telephone she was not.

The night had been a full one.

She had gone in awake at 04:28, by the method of Tuesday, with her feet first and no gap. She had counted ten and read the card. She had brought nothing to deliver. The medal had been given three times on Tuesday and three more on Wednesday, and she judged it sent. She sat in the warm chair with her head clear and waited to be given something, and she was.

It was her own kitchen, the one across the yard from her mother's, with the oilcloth and the shopping pad. Marie-Jo Labat sat on the other side of the table with her coat on and the electoral code open in front of her at a paper marker. She was reading an article aloud. A person employed by a commune may not be elected to its council. She shut the book on the marker. She said that there were fourteen names and that she wanted a fifteenth, at number three, and that it would mean handing in the key. She said Maylis. She said it twice in one sentence, as she had used to.

Then it was an afternoon. Lesca was on the commune's mower, on the long verge by the cemetery, and a telephone was going in the chest pocket of her waterproof. She felt it against her through the cloth. She did not take it out. She turned at the end of the verge and came back, and it rang again on the next strip, and the church clock struck six over the noise of the blades. That was all that happened. A woman mowed a verge until a clock had struck.

After that there was the dashboard of the commune's van, with rain on the windscreen and ten twenty-euro notes laid on it in a fan. Kévin Labat's hand was drawing back from them. Her own hand came into the picture, the right one, and pushed the notes across the plastic to his side with the flat of two fingers. There was no seam on it. She saw the back of the hand and knew.

The alarm had taken the room at six, and she had written three entries before she moved.

She had read them over in the mairie at eight, with the safe open. The first she knew from the inside. Marie-Jo had sat in that kitchen in March 2015 with the same book and the same marker, and read her the same article, and Lesca had said yes before the book was shut. She had handed in the waterproof and the keys that week. Marie-Jo had counted her votes twice and sat at her right hand for three years.

The second she could date. The lists for the municipal elections of 2020 had closed at the sub-prefecture on Thursday 27 February, at six in the evening. Lesca had filed her own that morning, with fifteen names on it. The other one had been asked the same thing in the same kitchen, five years later, and had gone out and mown a verge.

The third had a price on it and no date. She did not yet know what the two hundred euros had been for.

That had cost her a seat. It had left her something, and Lesca had seen it on Sunday without understanding it. In the hall with the strip lights Marie-Jo had stood at the other one's elbow counting coins, and spoken to her without a chair between them, and called her by her name.

Lesca had got as far as that when the secretary reached telephone.

* * *

She woke on the word carried.

The clock on the council room wall said twenty-four minutes to eleven. It had said fourteen minutes past ten at water. Her pencil lay on the floor. Five members and the secretary were looking at the papers in front of them with close attention.

'Carried what?' Lesca said.

The secretary read it out. The code provided for a mayor who was absent and for a mayor who was prevented, and in either case the first deputy took the chair. It made no provision for a mayor who was present and asleep. The committee had waited four minutes. Then Guy Larrère had observed that the chair was plainly prevented, in the sense of the article, and had asked the first deputy to proceed, which the first deputy, after looking at the mayor for some time, had done.

Larrère had then moved an amendment to line 6232, fêtes and ceremonies. The fête of July went from six hundred euros to three thousand. The bull of December stayed at nine thousand. The difference came off the mayor's line for receptions. He had said he would be brief, and with nobody awake to time him he had taken forty seconds.

It had carried by four votes to none. The minute recorded that the chair had not taken part.

'We didn't like to wake you,' Larrère said. 'They tell me Madame le maire keeps late hours now, with her fingers.'

'I can have it put again.'

'You can. You're awake.' He folded his hands on his copy of the draft. 'I'd be interested to hear you speak against a village fête, the week before six thousand people come to yours.'

She looked at the line. In 2018 she had moved it herself, from July to December, by nine votes to five, and it had cost her a first deputy. She had the figures for that in her head still. Two hundred and ten people in July, six thousand in December, eleven thousand euros back from the bar.

She had other figures that morning. There were fourteen names and a gap at number three.

'A vote taken is a vote taken,' she said. 'Go on from telephone.'

The secretary went on from telephone. Larrère looked at her for the rest of the sitting as he would have looked at a gate that had opened by itself.

* * *

She went to the café at eleven for coffee, which she did perhaps four times a year. She could hear it from the pavement.

'Four thousand two hundred a year,' a man was saying, 'to take pictures of water.'

'It's sixty thousand a year coming in.'

'It was a lad's six months going out. I'd like to know what that's entered under.'

'I'm stood here,' said Kévin Labat. 'You can say it to me.'

She pushed the door. The talk stopped. It was the gates contract of Tuesday, which had come round the village in two days and brought the cameras with it, as it did each December. There were four men at the table by the stove, and the owner, and Kévin at the counter in her old waterproof with a coffee. Larrère was at the stove table with his coat on. He had got there before her.

'Madame le maire is up,' he said.

She asked for a coffee, and drank it standing. Since half past ten she had not trusted a chair. The talk started again, lower, and it was about the weather.

The church clock struck eleven. She put her hands on the counter and counted them, left and then right, and made ten. Nobody laughed. One of the men at the stove began to, and Kévin turned his head and looked at him, and he found something in his glass.

'They do it every time I come in,' Kévin said to her. 'The cameras. It's not me that starts it.'

'I know who starts it.'

'I'd as soon they stopped. I looked up. That's all there is to say about it.'

She stirred the coffee. He stood a metre from her, as he had on Sunday, not going wide. In the night his hand had been drawing back from ten notes.

'Kévin. Before the cameras. When the gate still had a hand winch and somebody kept the key.'

'That was you.'

'Say it had still been me, in 2022. What would you have done?'

He did not have to think. 'Come and seen you. In the van, where nobody'd hear.'

'With how much?'

'Two hundred a night.' He looked at her. 'It's what a night was worth, with the platform left unlocked. Everybody knew the figure. Why?'

'And if I'd pushed it back at you, and said nothing to anyone. Not the mayor, not the gendarmes. And gone on keeping the key.'

Kévin put his cup down. The stove table had gone quiet again.

'Then I'd have thought you were waiting for more,' he said. 'And after a winter I'd have thought I didn't know what you wanted. And I'd still be netting.' He turned the cup a little in its saucer. 'That's not one I was ever in, Madame le maire. You didn't push anything back. You sent them eleven nights of me with my mouth open.'

'Sixty thousand euros a year.'

'I know the sum. You gave it me in court.' He was not angry. He had not been angry at any time, and she would have found it easier. 'And then you gave me the job. I've never got to the bottom of you. My mother says not to try.'

'What else does your mother say?'

'That you were asleep in the chair this morning, and nobody's to laugh at that either.' He finished his coffee. 'She had it before I did. Larrère rang her.'

Larrère was looking at the stove.

Somewhere a woman with Lesca's hands had pushed two hundred euros back across a dashboard and gone on turning a winch. She had sent nobody anything. She had taken nothing and filmed nothing, and Kévin Labat had gone on netting, and his mother spoke to her.

Lesca paid for the coffee and left the change.

* * *

A subsidy to an association required the signature of its president on a form in two copies. The form for the fête committee could have gone up with Kévin at noon. It could have waited for Friday, two till six, in the tray marked for collection. At half past five Lesca put it in a file, with the extract of the morning's minute, and walked it up to the terrace herself.

Marie-Jo lived in the second house past the hall. There was a light in the kitchen and a smell of onions. Lesca knocked, and stood on the step, which was a single slab with a boot scraper let into it.

Marie-Jo opened the door with a cloth in her hand. She looked at Lesca, and then at the file. She did not stand back from the door.

'Three thousand for July,' Lesca said. 'It was carried this morning. It wants your signature by the fifteenth.'

There was no chair on the step and no son. Marie-Jo spoke to the file.

'I've heard. Larrère moved it and you slept through it.'

'Yes.'

'Then it's Larrère's three thousand.'

'It's the commune's. I could have had it put again when I woke. I didn't.'

'Why not?'

Lesca had the answer in a notebook in a safe. She gave another, which was also true.

'You came to my kitchen in March 2015, with the code. You read me the article. You said there was a gap on the list.'

'At number one,' Marie-Jo said. 'Nobody else would head it.'

'I said yes before you'd shut the book.'

'You did.' Marie-Jo looked up from the file then, at her, for the first time on that step. Her eyes went to Lesca's hands and stayed on them. 'I've had eight years to think about that.'

'If I'd said I'd think about it. And let the date go by.'

'Then you'd be on the mower still, and I'd be telling you where to put my trestles.' She folded the cloth in two. 'And I'd not have had to find a carrier bag.'

The church clock began to strike six down in the square. It was an hour. Lesca kept her hands where they were, on the file, and did not count them.

Marie-Jo saw that as well.

'Go home and sleep, Madame le maire,' she said. 'You'll do it at the stone next, in front of the prefect.'

She put out her hand. Lesca gave her the file, and with it the keys of the hall on their wooden tag, which the fête committee had asked for through Kévin on Sunday, for the fifth.

'For the sixty tables,' Lesca said.

'I know what they're for.' Marie-Jo took the file in against her chest with the cloth and the keys, stepped back, and shut the door.
