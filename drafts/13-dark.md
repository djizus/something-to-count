# 13

On Friday Maylis woke with her right hand shut, and it hurt to open.

It was six, and Coralie's alarm was going. She had gone in awake at half past four, by the red figures, with no gap. She lay now with the hand on the blanket and unclosed it a finger at a time. A weal ran across the palm, from the heel to the root of the first finger. It was as wide as a pencil and raised, dark red, and at the heel end the skin had gone and the place was wet. She had not had it when she walked the yard. She had counted that hand under the yard lamp at ten past four.

It was the mark a handle leaves when it turns in the grip under load. Her father had come home with it. On Thursday she had written that she could not place the winch. It was placed now. The other one's hand had been on it, turning fast, and Maylis had the mark.

She took the pencil in her left hand and wrote at the back of the pad before she moved anything else. It came out large and slow.

*Friday. In awake. The water isn't a flood. It's a lake. She signed for it.*

It had come whole, as the medal had, from the first paper to the last. There was a lease, thirty years, to a gravel company, of the common grazing on the low meadows. There was the figure, four hundred thousand euros a year to the commune, and what the figure bought, which was a third class at the school and a doctor's surgery. She had put it to a council and it had carried. She had signed the last page with the stroke that crossed itself. Then a dredger was moored in the middle of forty hectares of grey water, with a conveyor running to the far shore, and loaded lorries were coming up through the square past the stone, one after another.

Before the dredger there was a lorry of a different kind. It backed into her mother's yard on a morning in June with its ramp down. Her mother stood at the foot of the ramp and counted the ponies up it, aloud, and signed the driver's sheet and went indoors. Maylis had given her the figures the night before, across the oilcloth. Her mother had said that she could count as well.

She wrote it down in six lines. Then she wrote two more, since they had come behind it.

*Maïté C.'s roof. I'm up it with a tile. She holds the ladder for me.*

*She asks me to see to the gutter while I'm there.*

Coralie was on lates. She turned over and saw the hand.

'What have you done?'

'I woke with it.'

Coralie sat up. She took the hand by the wrist and turned it to the lamp, and she looked at it for longer than she had looked at the rib.

'That's a friction burn. A rope, or a bar that's turned on you with your weight on it.' She pressed beside it with her thumb. 'That's hours old. Not days. What did you take hold of at four?'

'Nothing. I walked the yard.'

'You came back to bed with that?'

'I came back without it.'

Coralie looked at her. Then she went down for the box from the home that she kept under the sink. She cleaned the place with saline, laid a square of greased gauze along the weal and bound the hand across the palm, twice round, leaving the fingers free. She did it as she did it for the residents, quickly, with her mouth shut.

'Six weeks for a rib and now a hand,' she said. 'On the same side. You'll fork nothing and turn nothing till I've looked at it again.'

* * *

The mairie was shut on Friday mornings. Maylis swept the square with the broom held in her left hand and the handle laid along her forearm. The bollard leaned beside the mayor's space. The nine strings were off.

She had read the pad down twice before she left the house. On the Friday before, she had written that the winch went with the gate down and that she had told them, and they had given her the mairie. None of that had happened. No winch had seized over there. The other one had taken three hundred hectares of common that had been grazed since before the maize, and let it for thirty years to a company with a dredger, by a vote, and it had cost her mother eleven ponies. It was done on paper, in a council room, with a pen.

Maylis had held the key to that grazing for eighteen years. In that time she had been asked for one decision about it, and she had said she would think.

She went home at noon to eat. The pad lay on the kitchen table. She had left it face up, with the two empty boxes showing. It was face down now, open from the cardboard back, at the first of the pages in her own hand.

Coralie was in her coat by the door, with her bag on her shoulder and the car keys in her fingers. She had been waiting.

'I went to put gauze on the list,' she said. 'I've never read a thing of yours. Not a letter. It was on the back of my shopping.'

'It isn't what you think.'

'I don't know what half of it is. There's a stone, and a box, and two of you. I read that and I thought, she's not well, and I'll ring Dax.' Coralie's voice was level. 'Then I read the rest. Her name's on four pages, Maylis. You kiss her in front of the village. She's a widow in it. You've got him dead. And this morning you're up on her roof, and she's holding the ladder, and she wants her gutter seen to.'

'It isn't me. It's the other one.'

'It's your handwriting.' She turned the pad a page with one finger and laid the finger under three words. 'Yesterday. I want that. I asked you on Wednesday if it was here, and you said no. I've spent sixteen years waiting for you to want something. I never thought to ask who.'

'It's not Maïté Castagnet I want.'

'Then say what it is.'

Maylis looked at the pad. It was a square full of people, and a prefect kept waiting, and a signature. It was a lake. She had no way of saying any of it in that kitchen that would not sound worse than a woman.

'I can't yet.'

'No,' Coralie said. 'You said that as well.'

She went out to her car. Maylis went upstairs with her bound hand on the rail. On the bed there was one pillow. The door of the box room across the landing stood open, and the other pillow was on the camp bed in there, among her father's cartons, with Coralie's alarm clock beside it.

The church clock had struck twelve while Coralie was speaking. Maylis stood on the landing between the two doors and took the ten breaths she owed, and counted the hand that was not bound.

* * *

The mairie opened at two. The secretary had the key in the door at five to, and her coat off and the date stamp turned to the fourth by the hour. She came on Tuesdays and Fridays and had done so under three mayors.

Jeannot Castagnet came in at ten past, with his cap in his hand. Maylis was at the mayor's tray with the week's post. He saw her, and saw the bandage, and looked away at the counter.

'I've come to take a letter back,' he said to the secretary. 'The registered one, of the twenty-fourth. Number forty-one.'

The secretary opened the register of incoming post and found the line with her finger.

'It was received, Monsieur Castagnet. It's entered. It has the stamp.'

'I know it was received. I watched it signed for. I'm withdrawing it.'

'There's no procedure for that.'

'I wrote it. I can unwrite it.'

'You sent it. Once it's sent and entered it's the mayor's post, and it's for the mayor to answer within two months.' She turned the register towards him so that he could see the line. 'I can't make it not have come. There's no column.'

'Then give me the envelope.'

'It's addressed to Monsieur le maire. I can't hand the mayor's post across the counter.'

He stood with the cap in both hands. He was not a patient man, and he was being patient, since the person he was doing it for was six paces off with a letter knife.

'What can you do?'

'You could write to the mairie to say that you withdraw your letter of the twenty-fourth. I'd enter that.'

'That's another letter.'

'It would be, yes.'

'I'm not writing a forty-second to take back the forty-first.'

The secretary considered it. Then she went to the mayor's tray herself, took out the envelope with No. 41 in the corner, and wrote along its top edge in pencil that the sender had called on 4 December and wished it withdrawn. She initialled that, and put the envelope back in the tray, on top.

'He'll see that on Tuesday,' she said.

'And then?'

'Then it's for him.'

Jeannot put his cap on. He came the six paces along the counter.

'That's what it's worth,' he said. 'I was at the brigade at nine. My cousin wrote down that I take it back. He says a file's shut when Dax says it's shut, and Dax hasn't said.' He looked at the bandage again, and did not ask what she had done to herself. 'You have what you asked for, Lesca.'

He went out. She had written in pencil that he was dead, and it was in her kitchen, face down. He crossed the square in front of the window and got into the Trafic, and she watched him do it as she would have watched a man walk on a frozen pond.

'What did he mean by that?' the secretary said. 'What did you ask for?'

'The winch,' Maylis said.

She waited until the stamp had come down on the afternoon's post. Then she asked for the register of deliberations, which any inhabitant might consult during opening hours.

'You've the key to this building,' the secretary said. 'You could read it any night.'

'I'm asking for it.'

The secretary fetched it. Maylis turned the pages left-handed. The estimate for the school roof was in March 2024, from a firm at Dax, fifty-two thousand euros. The council had taken note of it and had not entered it in the budget. It was there again in March 2025, at fifty-five thousand, and in March 2026, at fifty-nine. The junior class had been taught round two buckets since the first of those winters.

She worked it on the edge of the blotter. One year of the lake was six school roofs at the last price, and the church's, with five thousand over.

The other one had not asked for a register. She had had the figure in front of her and a council waiting, and she had put the question and counted the hands.

'What do you want with the school?' the secretary said.

'I wanted the figure.'

* * *

At half past four her mother was in the passenger seat of the commune's van with two bales in the back, and they went down through the Port and along the dyke.

The low meadows were green and standing in water to the fetlock. The ponies had heard the van. They were at the fence before she had the doors open, eleven of them, with the old mare in front.

In the night she had watched them go up a ramp. She had seen the paper that sent them, and the name at the foot of it. She knew whose yard the lorry had backed into. She would have signed.

She cut the twine with the knife held in her left hand. She took the fork in the left hand too, short, with the shaft under her arm, and kept the bandaged one against her chest. It was the only way the rib and the palm would both allow. The hay went over the wire a little at a time. The mare took the first of it off the tines.

Her mother had the window down. She did not count the herd at once. She was watching the fork, and then the hand that was not on it.

'Give me that here,' Arlette said.

Maylis came to the window and held the hand out. Her mother did not touch the bandage. She looked at where it ran, across the palm from the heel to the first finger, and at the brown that had come through at the heel.

'Your father had that every December. I bound it for him for thirty years. It's the handle does it, when it's stiff and a man won't let go.' She looked up. 'I'm not asking how you came by it. I'm asking you one thing. What have you been turning?'
