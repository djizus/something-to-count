# 13

On Friday she woke with her right hand shut, and when she opened it there was a weal across the palm.

It ran from the base of the forefinger to the heel of the hand, as wide as a pencil and raised. It was red at the edges and paler along the middle, like a thing some days old. It was hot. It had not been there at midnight, when she had set the phone for four with that hand.

She counted the fingers above it, since she had woken, and they were five. Then she tried to take up the pencil for the pad and could not close on it.

She wrote left-handed, slowly, in letters a child would have been marked down for.

*Friday. Went in awake. Not a flood. A lake.*

The weight had come at 02:51 and she had let it have her. At four she had walked the yard. At twenty past she had lain down and kept the red figures the whole way, and they had become the lamps without a gap. She had counted, and read the card. Then a file had been put in front of her.

It came as the medal had come, entire, and in order. It was not a ceremony. It was a decision, with its papers.

She was at the head of the council table, in the mayor's chair, with fourteen people down the two sides. In front of her lay a lease of thirty-eight pages. The commune of Peyrebarthe let its common grazing on the low meadows to a gravel company for thirty years, to 2049, for four hundred thousand euros a year. She knew the clauses as she knew the commune's sheet. Under clause 4 the common was to be cleared of livestock at the company's expense. Under clause 9 the lorries ran from seven. She had put it to the table as a third class at the school and a doctor two days a week, and it had carried. Then she signed the foot of each page with the stroke that crossed itself, thirty-eight times, and did not slow down.

After the signature came what it bought. A dredger was moored in the middle of forty hectares of grey water, with a conveyor running to heaps of gravel on the far shore. Lorries came up through the square. Twenty-two children sat in a classroom that the school did not have.

Then it was a morning in June. A lorry stood backed up to her mother's paddock gate with its ramp down. Her mother stood at the foot of the ramp and counted the ponies up it, aloud, as far as eleven. She signed the driver's sheet on the gatepost. She went into the house without looking across the yard, and shut the kitchen door.

Maylis had sat in the chair with all of it. She had been wrong about the water. Nobody's winch had failed and nothing had been left to lie. The other one had looked at three hundred hectares of grass, done a sum and dug a lake.

It had taken her one sitting. Maylis had written to the council about a winch in 2011, 2012 and 2013.

*She leased the low meadows for gravel. 400,000 a year. Mother's ponies went in a lorry. She signed it.*

Under that she wrote *Maïté again*, since at the end there had been a ladder against a wall, and Maïté Castagnet in her black coat with a foot on the bottom rung, looking up.

She did not write that she would have signed it. The pencil would not stay in her left hand that long.

* * *

Coralie's alarm went at six. She turned over, saw the hand lying open on the blanket, and was out of bed before she had spoken.

She took it by the wrist and turned it to the lamp. She pressed beside the weal with two fingers, above it and below, and watched the colour go and come back.

'That's a friction burn. Off a rope, or a handle. It's been healing a week.' She looked up. 'It wasn't there last night. You laid the table with that hand. I watched you cut bread.'

'I woke with it.'

'You don't wake with a week-old burn.' She fetched the box she kept from the home and dressed it, with cream and a square of gauze and a turn of bandage, and she did it as she would have done a resident's, without hurrying. 'What did you have hold of?'

'Nothing. I was on my back.'

Coralie tied off the bandage. The pad was lying on the blanket where Maylis had dropped it, open at the back. She picked it up to move it. Then she saw that the writing on it was not a shopping list and was not in any hand she knew, and she read it.

She read the left-handed page. She turned back one and read that, and then turned back again. She read standing, in her nightdress, with the box from the home under her arm. Maylis watched her go through the week from the wrong end.

'Maïté Castagnet,' Coralie said. 'In a black coat. *I kiss her on the cheek. She takes it. Then she wipes it off.*' She turned a page. '*Maïté is in black for him. She knows it was me.*' She turned another. '*Go and see Maïté?* With a question mark. And this morning, *Maïté again.*'

'It isn't what it looks like.'

'I don't know what it looks like. I'm reading it.' She went back to the Thursday page and put her finger on it. '*I want that.* I asked you on Wednesday if what you wanted was here. You said no.'

'That's not about Maïté. That's a medal.'

'A medal.'

'There are two of me, Coralie. One of them's the mayor. She was given a medal. Maïté's the widow there.'

Coralie looked at her for some time. She had been told a great many things at six in the morning, at the home, by people who believed them.

'I've nursed people who had two of themselves,' she said. 'They didn't write it down this neatly.' She shut the pad and laid it on the chest of drawers, beside the slit envelope. 'She's on four pages. I counted. Kévin Labat's on two. Your mother's on one, with her ponies. There's a prefect.' She squared the pad against the edge. 'I'm not on any of them.'

'You're here. I don't have to write you down.'

'No. You've never had to.'

She took her pillow off the bed. She did it with the hand that was free, and held it against her with the box.

'I'm on earlies all next week. You're up at four and I'm up at six, and I feel you go every time. I'll take the box room.' She stopped in the doorway. 'It's for the sleep. I'm telling you that so that you know what I've said.'

The box room was across the landing. It had the boxes in it and a single bed. Maylis heard the pillow go down, and the door left open, and Coralie in the bathroom running the tap.

On the shopping pad's front page, when she went down, Monday's two boxes were still empty, and nothing had been added under them.

* * *

The mairie opened at two. Maylis was at the counter at five past, with her bandaged hand in her pocket, asking the secretary for the register of deliberations.

'You want the register.'

'It's open to the public in opening hours.'

'I know what it is. Nobody's asked for it since the notary in 2019.' The secretary brought it from the cupboard and laid it on the counter. 'What year?'

'The school roof.'

It took her ten minutes, turning the pages with her left hand. The estimate was from a roofer in Dax, for thirty-eight thousand four hundred euros, to strip and re-tile the school over both classrooms. The council had had it before it in March 2024, in March 2025 and in March 2026. Each time the minute said the same. The council recognised the need, took note of the estimate, and adjourned the work for want of resources.

She did the division in her head, standing at the counter. At four hundred thousand a year, the other one's lake paid for that roof every thirty-five days.

The door opened behind her. Jeannot Castagnet came in, in his day jacket, with his cap in his hand. He did not look at her. He went to the far end of the counter and spoke to the secretary.

'I've come to take back a letter.'

'To take back what?'

'A letter. Number forty-one. Registered, the twenty-fourth of November. It's in his tray, or it's been answered, or it's in the file with the forty others. I want it out.'

The secretary took off her glasses. She had been at that counter since 2003.

'I can't give you a letter out of the mayor's post.'

'It's my letter. I wrote it.'

'You sent it. It was signed for. It's been entered in the incoming register with a number.' She found the line and turned the book towards him. 'Once it's entered, it's the commune's. I can answer it, or file it, or forward it. There's nothing here that lets me un-receive it.'

'Then put down that I've withdrawn it.'

'In a letter?'

'Not in a letter. If I write to you it's forty-two, and forty-two is spoken for.' He turned his cap round in his hands. 'I've been to the brigade this morning. My cousin had a form in his drawer for taking back a complaint. He'd had it ready since Saturday. He stamped it in front of me. A gendarme has a form, and the mairie hasn't.'

'The mairie has never been asked.'

'It's being asked now.'

The secretary considered the incoming register, and then the day book, in which she entered callers. She took up her pen.

'I can note it,' she said. 'Monsieur Castagnet attended at twenty past two and stated that his letter number forty-one was to be regarded as withdrawn. The mairie takes note.'

'And the letter?'

'Stays in the file. A letter withdrawn is still a letter received.'

'So I've taken it back and you've kept it.'

'Yes.'

'That's the mairie,' Jeannot said. He put his cap on. On his way to the door he passed behind Maylis, and he spoke to the back of her waterproof, where the commune's name was.

'Both done, Lesca. You've what you asked for.'

He had not said what it had cost him, and she did not turn round. When the door had shut the secretary was looking at her over the day book.

'What did he mean, what you asked for?'

'The winch,' Maylis said. 'It was about the winch.'

She closed the register on the school roof and pushed it back across the counter.

* * *

It rained until four and then stopped. At half past she took two bales down to the low meadows in the commune's van, with her mother in the passenger seat.

The ponies had heard the van. They came across the grass with their heads up and stopped along the wire, eleven of them, small and dark. Beyond them Jeannot's tonne stood in its mud. Beyond that the meadows ran green to the far trees, three hundred hectares, with no water on them and nothing moored.

She cut the twine with the knife in her left hand. She held the fork short in the same hand and kept the right against her chest, in its bandage, and each forkful went over the wire at half the size of last week's.

'That's two things wrong with one side of you,' Arlette said through the window.

'It's nothing.'

'I'm not asking. I've stopped.' She watched four forkfuls go over. 'Marie-Jo says you had Castagnet in the mairie today, taking back a letter. She says nobody's seen that done.'

'He changed his mind.'

'Castagnet's not changed his mind since his first communion.'

The old mare took hay off the fork. Maylis looked along the wire, and counted them, and made eleven. That morning she had watched them go up a ramp.

'What would you do,' she said, 'if the commune took the grazing? For money. A great deal of it. For the school.'

'Who's been saying that?'

'Nobody. If it did.'

Arlette did not have to think about it. 'I'd not set foot in that mairie again,' she said. 'Not for a stamp. And I'd tell whoever signed it that I could count as well as she could.'

She had said she. Maylis put the fork down in the bale.

Her mother was not looking at the ponies. She was looking at the bandage, which had worked loose with the forking and come away from the heel of the hand. The end of the weal showed under it.

'Give me that.'

Maylis held the hand in through the window. Arlette took it in both of hers, pushed the gauze back with her thumb and turned the palm up to the light that was left.

'Your father had that,' she said. 'The same hand. The winter the pawl let go and the handle ran back through his fist. It took the skin off in one line, from there to there.' She laid her thumb along it, without pressing. 'That's a winch has done that, and not last Friday, and not a week since. So I am asking. What have you been turning, Maylis?'
