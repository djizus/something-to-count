# 14

On Saturday Lesca stayed in the room after the alarm had gone, which she had not known could be done.

She had gone in awake at 04:27, feet first, with no gap, and had brought nothing to deliver. She counted ten and read the card. She sat in the warm chair and waited, and what came was a road she drove twice a week.

It was the road down through the Port, in rain, behind the wipers of the commune's old van. The care home was lit. Past it the house fronts were shuttered, one in two. She looked at the walls as she went by, since she had had them replastered. In 2016 the State had fixed a blue enamel plate to each front in the Port, at a metre sixty from the pavement, with ADOUR 13 XII 2014 on it and a line. It was the height the river had stood at against the dyke that night. There were thirty-six plates, and the home had two.

These walls were bare. They were stained with damp to the height of a boot and no higher. There was not a plate on them, nor the four holes of one.

The road ended against a dyke. It was the old dyke, low, with a patch of new clay on its face. A house stood hard against it with smoke at the chimney and the number 40 on the gatepost, and a man in a cape was clipping a privet hedge level in the rain. It was Sarthou. He had lived in Dax for four years.

The alarm began then. She heard it from a long way off, as she might have heard the church clock from the dyke. The panel in front of her lost its grey. The two lamps drew together and thinned, and were starting to be red figures on a chest of drawers.

She did not want to go. She had not seen why the walls were bare.

She did what her hands did at the council table. She laid them palm to palm in her lap and rubbed them, hard, with the seam between them. She picked the left-hand lamp, since it was the other one's, and looked at that and at nothing else. She looked at the rim of it and the wire behind the glass. The alarm went on, further off. The lamp came back to its place and was a lamp. The steel came back round it.

She held it. As long as she had the lamp and her hands were moving, the rest came, and it came faster than before.

She was on the platform of the hand winch, at the pier of the gate. A gauge was bolted to the pier, with a red line across it that her father had painted. The river stood a metre under the line. A car was stopped on the dyke with its window down by the width of three fingers, and Guy Larrère's voice came out of the gap. It said that the river had not been over the line since 2009.

Then she had an envelope in her hand, at the door of the mairie, in an ordinary rain. It was one of his. In the top left corner it said No. 17. It complained of ruts on the track out to the tonnes, made by the commune's tractor in pulling his van out of the mud, on a morning in January 2017. With it she had a pay slip for that month, and on the pay slip, two lines from the bottom, eleven litres of diesel were taken off.

She let go of the lamp. The room went at once.

* * *

The alarm was off. Coralie was standing over the bed in her dressing gown with her hand on the clock radio.

'Six minutes,' Coralie said. 'It rang for six minutes, and you lay there with your eyes open and your hands going. I shook you. I've not shaken anybody since my training.'

'I heard it. I was busy.'

'You were talking. You said, it's a tow. You said it twice.' She took her hand off the clock. 'You said it as you'd say somebody had died.'

Lesca had the notebook over her face. She wrote.

*Night of Friday 4 to Saturday 5 December. Went in awake 04:27. Held six minutes past the alarm by fixing on the left lamp and rubbing the hands. To be repeated.*

*1. The Port. No plates on any wall. Old dyke. Sarthou's house standing, and him in front of it.*

*2. The gauge at the gate. River a metre under the line. Not over it since 2009.*

*3. His letter No. 17. January 2017. The commune's tractor pulled his van out of the mud. The diesel was stopped from her pay.*

She read it down. The fourth line she wrote as she would have written a finding.

*4. No water came there on 12 December 2014. The chain was a tow, on a dry track, two years after. Nobody went down to knock, since there was nothing to knock about. He is alive there because it did not rain.*

On Tuesday she had lain in this bed and cried into her ears. She had believed for four days in a woman with her hands who had been given the order and had walked instead. There was no such woman. There was one who had never been given the order at all.

She did not cry. She turned back through the notebook from the first page, looking for one word, and found it in each entry that had weather in it. A rib was broken in rain. A letter was signed for in rain, and the L spread. He laughed in rain. A hall was full, and they had come in out of the rain. She had written on the first morning that she had heard rain all night in a week without any.

She went to the dresser for the prefecture's report on the flood, which she had by heart, and looked all the same. In 2014 it had rained in the Adour basin on twenty-three days out of twenty-four before the twelfth.

*5. It has rained there every day since at least 23 November. Old dyke, patched. A hand winch that sticks at nineteen. The key goes to Hontaube on 1 January. Their river is a metre under the line today.*

*6. It did not come there in 2014. It is coming now.*

Coralie was still in the doorway.

'I'm going to Dax,' Lesca said.

'To a doctor?'

'To Sarthou. The committee wants a flag.'

'You lay there for six minutes, and you're going to Dax about a flag.' Coralie pulled her dressing gown shut at the neck. 'I'll say this once. I run a home. When one of them starts being somewhere else with their eyes open, I ring the family. I don't know who I ring for you.'

* * *

Sarthou had a flat on the second floor of a block near the baths, with the Adour under the window. It was the same water. It would pass the gates of Peyrebarthe by night.

He let her in without a word and went ahead of her into a room with a table, two chairs and a television. He was sixty-three. He had been the gendarme for Peyrebarthe and two communes more for nineteen years, and he was Jeannot Castagnet's cousin. On the balcony a box hedge grew in a trough a metre long. It was clipped level.

'Madame le maire,' he said. He did not ask her to sit, and she did not trust a chair.

'The committee's flag bearer has had his hip done. He can't stand for an hour. The prefect comes on the twelfth, and there has to be a flag at the stone.'

'There's the fire brigade.'

'I'm asking you.'

'You've not asked me for anything since you asked for my house.'

'The State asked for your house. It was four against thirty-six.'

'You put it to the vote. I was in the room. I counted the hands myself, ten and five, and went home and oiled the hinges on the gate. A man does odd things.' He stood by the sideboard with one hand on it. 'Why me?'

'You were the gendarme that night. You're his cousin. You can wear the uniform to a ceremony.'

'I've carried him already. I was at the left front corner.'

She had seen him at it. She had stood in the third row at that funeral with the medal not yet struck. In the night she had watched him clip a hedge in front of a house that the commune's bank lay across, on a morning when he should have been taking down a statement. She could not say which of the two had put her in the car.

A biscuit tin stood on the sideboard under his hand. It was square, with a lighthouse on the lid, and a thick rubber band went round it twice. He saw her look.

'That's the notebooks,' he said. 'The brigade keeps its registers. A man keeps his notebook. One a year for thirty-one years, and the last few are in there. I've not had the band off since the removal.' He moved his hand on the lid and left it there. 'There's nothing in them that isn't in my reports.'

She had not asked whether there was.

'I'll telephone,' he said. 'I don't say yes on a doorstep. I learned that from signing things.'

He saw her out to the landing, and she took the stairs.

* * *

It caught her on the Bayonne road, at the lights by the tyre depot on the edge of Dax.

The light was red. She stopped behind a bread van with her hands on the wheel. It was nine minutes to twelve by the clock on the dashboard, in full daylight, with frost still white on the verge.

The weight came down on her breastbone.

It was the same weight, at its thirty-five kilos, cold through her coat. The car filled with the smell of cold steel and old tobacco. She told her right foot to stay on the brake, and found that it would do that and nothing else. Her hands were on the wheel at ten to two and would not close on it. Her eyes moved. She could see the light.

It went green. The bread van pulled away. She told her foot to lift, and it stayed. A horn sounded behind her, once, and then for longer. She did not push against the weight. She had not pushed in twelve years, and she could not let go to it either, at a junction, with the engine running. She did nothing, with all her attention, and watched the light.

It went red. A man got out of the car behind and came up along her window and looked in. He saw a woman sitting upright with her eyes open and her hands on the wheel. He rapped on the glass. She could not turn her head to him.

It went green a second time. He was saying something through the glass. The little finger of her right hand bent on the wheel.

The rest came back at once. She got the window down and said that she had been taken ill and was better, and he said he should hope so, and went back to his car. She drove through on the amber. Four hundred metres on she pulled into a lay-by and counted her fingers, and made ten, and could not have said for a minute which road it was.

It had come at 02:51 for twelve years, to a woman lying down. She had gone to meet it on purpose on five mornings. It had now found her sitting up, at noon, in gear.

* * *

She did not go to the mairie. She sat in her own kitchen with her coat on and the notebook open at the sixth line.

On the night it came there, the other one would be at the gate. She had the key until the first of January. She had the winch. The order would come by telephone, as it had come to Lesca, in the same voice, and it would say the same two words. And the other one would look down the track with her headlamps on, and see what was there to be seen at the end of it.

Lesca knew what was at the end of it. She had kept it out of the item eleven times in a day.

Nothing she said in that chair crossed. She had tried on Wednesday, aloud, and had heard her own voice stop at the steel. What crossed was what she carried about. The medal had taken two days of going over at every hour, and it had been eleven minutes long.

She had seven days. She would need something that could be carried in the mouth.

She wrote on the blotter, not in the notebook, and struck out as she went. *He is in the tonne.* That was a fact, and the other one would have it already, from what stood on the track. *Knock before you open.* A woman who had turned that winch for eighteen years would open first and mean to knock after. *Walk down the track.* It did not say why. She worked at it as she had worked at the two names for the stone, for most of an hour, with the pencil.

What she had at the end of the hour was five words.

*Go to the tonne first.*

She read it twice, and it held. She said it aloud in the kitchen, once, and then she counted her fingers and said it again. At the hour she would say it after the count, and between hours whenever her hand lay shut. She had not chosen the medal, this time. She was sending the van.

The telephone rang at ten past six, in the dark. It was a Dax number.

'I'll carry it,' Sarthou said. 'For my cousin. Not for the commune. You can put that in your minutes or leave it out.'

'I'll put it in.'

'There's one thing. That bench the prefecture sent, with the plate on it. I'm told it stands on my plot.' He stopped. She could hear the river through his open window, or thought she could. 'I've never been back to look. When they put the bank through. Which side of it was my kitchen?'
