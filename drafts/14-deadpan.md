# 14

On Saturday at half past four the mayor of Peyrebarthe sat in the chair with nothing to deliver, and was driven through the Port in the rain.

It was the commune's van, with the wipers going. She had the wheel. The road went down from the terrace as it had always gone, past the care home, and the care home had its minibus in the yard and no plate on its wall.

She saw the plate first, by its absence. In 2015 the commune had fixed eleven cast plates in the Port, as the law required of a place that had been under threat. Each carried a line, at the height the river had stood on the far side of the bank, and the date. The wall where the first should have been was bare render. So was the next.

Every third house had its shutters closed. Two had a notary's board. In her own Port the shutters were open and four of the fronts had been repainted in the last year.

The road ended against the dyke. It was the old dyke, sixty metres nearer than it should have been and a metre lower, with a patch of bare clay on its face. There was no ramp and no bench. Four houses stood under it in a row, and the last had smoke coming from the chimney and the number 40 on its gatepost. Along the front a man in a gendarme's trousers was clipping a privet hedge level, in the rain, with shears.

Then she was in his kitchen, at the oilcloth. The dyke filled the window. Sarthou sat opposite with a form and wrote on it in capitals, and she read it upside down as she read anything across a desk. Statement of the keyholder. Complaint of J. Castagnet concerning the level of the water at his tonne. It was dated Tuesday 1 December 2026.

After the kitchen she had the gate. It had the hand winch on it, the one she had sold for sixty-two euros, with its handle locked in a box. On the pier was the gauge, and her father's red line, and the river a metre under the line and brown.

And she knew a thing, as on the first morning she had known forty letters. She knew it as a figure. January 2017. Letter 17, about ruts. The ruts had been made by the commune's tractor on the track to the tonnes, while it pulled a white Trafic out of the mud, and the diesel had been stopped out of somebody's pay.

She was reading the gauge a second time when the alarm took the room.

* * *

She wrote it before she moved, in nine lines. Then she lay with the notebook on her chest and went through it as she went through a file that had come back from the prefecture refused.

The chain was a tow. It had been a January, in 2017, and a van in the mud, and a man complaining of the ruts. It had not been the morning after any night.

There had been no night. The plates were not on the walls because nothing had been there to mark. The old dyke stood because no river had tried it. The four houses stood, and Sarthou clipped his hedge and took statements about a hand's depth of water, because in that village the twelfth of December 2014 had been a Friday with a Saturday after it. The rain had not fallen there. It was why that mairie had a parking space where hers had a stone. Nobody's name had been cut, because nobody had drowned and nobody had opened anything.

So the other one had not gone down the track and knocked. She had not had to. Nobody had telephoned her in the night. She had the same hands and the key, and she had never been asked the question.

On Tuesday Lesca had cried on her back for a village where she had done the other thing. She took the notebook up again, turned to Tuesday and ruled out the two lines under the tow, with the edge of the envelope for a ruler, as an entry is struck from a register. She initialled the correction.

Coralie was in the doorway.

'You're not smiling at it today.'

'No.'

'I'd got used to that as well,' Coralie said, and went down.

Lesca stayed where she was. It was a quarter past six, and she was still looking at the nine lines when she saw what else was in them.

It was raining there. It had rained in each thing she had been given for twelve days. There had been rain on an acknowledgement card and on a van window, and rain on the roof of a hall. Here it had been dry, with frost, since the middle of November.

She got up then, and did not wait for breakfast. In the mairie she took down the flood file of 2014 from the cupboard behind the secretary's desk. The prefecture had asked for the readings afterwards, day by day, and she had copied them herself from the association's book. It had begun to rain in Peyrebarthe on 24 November 2014 and had not stopped. On 4 December the river had stood a metre under the red line. On the twelfth it had stood ninety centimetres over.

In the chair the river had been a metre under, and brown, on what she took to be Thursday or Friday.

She sat with the file open. That village had not been spared December 2014. It had not had it yet. It had an old dyke with a clay patch on it, a hand winch that stopped at the nineteenth turn and a key that went to Hontaube in January. It had Guy Larrère in the mairie with a telephone. It had forty houses and a care home. And on any night between now and the twelfth it had Jeannot Castagnet in the cellar of his tonne, with his waders on, alive.

She counted her fingers, though it was not the hour, and made ten.

* * *

The flag of the commune had been carried at each commemoration by a veteran of Algeria, who was eighty-seven and had been in hospital at Dax since October with his hip. Lesca had meant to give it to the first deputy. On Saturday morning she drove to Dax and asked for Sarthou.

He lived on the third floor of a block near the arena. There was a balcony a metre deep, and on it a box bush in a tub, clipped square. He let her in without surprise and put coffee in front of her at a table for two.

'Four years,' he said. 'I wondered which of you would come. I had it down as the priest.'

'The flag wants carrying on the twelfth. I'd like it to be you.'

'Why me?'

'You were on duty that night. You had the last house. And you've not been back.'

'I've been back. I park at the bottom of the bank and look at the bench.' He turned his cup in its saucer. 'I've never gone up to read it.'

'It thanks the State.'

'So I'm told.'

He was looking past her at the sideboard. A square biscuit tin stood on it, with a Breton girl on the lid and a rubber band round it both ways. He looked at the tin, and then at her, as he would have looked at a number plate.

'You drove down the dyke road that night,' he said. 'To the gate.'

'Yes.'

'There's the one road.'

'There is.'

'I was on it myself. Before you.' He let that stand for as long as it took the coffee to stop turning. 'I retired with every file shut. I'd always said I would, and I did. I kept one thing from that night. It's in there. I've not had the band off it since I moved.'

She did not ask what it was. In eleven years she had not asked a gendarme anything about that night that she did not already know the answer to, and she saw no cause to begin on a Saturday in his flat. He did not offer to open it.

'I'll telephone,' Sarthou said. 'I'll not say yes with you sitting there.'

'The order of ceremony goes to the printer on Monday.'

'Then you'll have it before Monday.'

At the door he held out his hand, and she counted hers before she gave it, since the cathedral was striking eleven. He watched her do it. He had been told about that too.

* * *

The road back crossed the river at the works on the Hontaube bridge, where a temporary light let one line through at a time. It was red. She stopped, put the handbrake on, as she always did, and laid her hands on the wheel.

The weight came down on her breastbone through her coat.

It was a quarter to twelve in the morning, in sunlight, with the engine running. She tried her right hand and nothing went down the arm. She tried her foot. She had her eyes. The car smelled of cold steel and old tobacco, and it had smelled of nothing when she got into it.

The light went green. She did not go.

Behind her a lorry of the gravel company sounded its horn, and then leaned on it. It was loaded. It was worth fifty-seven euros to the commune and she could not get out of its way.

The windscreen had begun to go grey at the edges, and flat, and she knew what it was turning into. She had gone into the room awake on four nights by letting everything go. Now it was coming for her at a set of lights, and she did not mean to be found by a lorry driver with her eyes open and nobody behind them.

The light went red again. She took hold of it. She fixed on the one red lamp and gave it everything she had left, as she would have held a rail, and did not let her eyes off it for the grey. The grey stopped where it was. The lamp stayed a lamp. She was in a car on a bridge, and she could count the bolts on the bracket.

It went green a second time. Her little finger moved on the wheel, and the hand after it.

The lorry driver was at her window. She wound it down.

'Are you ill, madame?'

'No. I'm going.'

She counted ten on the wheel and took the handbrake off. In the notebook that evening she entered the cost, which was eleven minutes at a light, and under it the finding. *A fixed lamp holds.*

* * *

She drafted the sentence at the kitchen table, as she would have drafted a motion.

She had no way to put a question to the other one. Nothing had come back from the medal but a tow. A thing gone over all day arrived, and arrived whole, and she had six days and the nights between.

The first draft ran to four lines. It gave the date and the height of the river and whose order would come, and it told the other one what the order would be worth. She read it aloud and struck it out. A motion of four lines does not survive a sitting, and this had steel to go through.

The second gave the man's name and the cellar. She struck that out as well. The other one knew his name. She had forty-one letters from him.

The third was five words.

*Go to the tonne first.*

It did not say before what. Lesca knew before what. She had stood at that gate with the river ninety over the line and had not gone anywhere first, and the other one had her hands. Whatever she did at the winch when the telephone rang, she was to have walked down the track before she did it.

She began at five, after the fingers, and gave it each hour as she had given the medal. By the third hour it had got loose from the hours. She said it at the sink and at the window. She said it going up the stairs.

'Go to the tonne first.'

Coralie was on the landing with a pile of sheets. She stopped. She stood with the sheets against her chest and looked at Lesca as she had not looked at her in twelve years, with her whole face, and for a moment Lesca thought she was going to speak.

'What did you say?'

'An item. I'm going over it.'

'Say it again.'

'Go to the tonne first.'

Coralie's mouth opened and shut. She went into the box room with the sheets, and the door, which had stood open for nine nights, closed to within a hand's width.

At four on Sunday Lesca got up, stood twenty minutes in the kitchen and went in awake with the five words. She said them in the chair until the alarm began. She heard it through steel, a long way off, in a bedroom. The panel started to go to plaster at the edges.

She fixed on the left lamp. It was not the lamp that had come on for her in 2014. She rubbed her palms together on her knees, the seam against the skin, and kept her eyes on it, and the plaster stopped. The rivets came back. She said the sentence four times more to the warm arms of the chair, with the alarm going in another house.

When she let go of the lamp the clock radio read 06:19. For some seconds she did not know the lampshade.

* * *

The telephone rang at eight, in the kitchen. She had it before Coralie could.

'I'll carry it,' Sarthou said. 'I've not held a flag since my passing-out. Tell me where to stand and for how long.'

'In front of the stone. Forty minutes, with the speeches.'

'I've stood longer for less.' She heard him move something on a table, and a rubber band snap. 'One thing, Madame le maire. Before I come and walk about on it. The plan they sent me in 2016 showed a line through a plot. It didn't show rooms. My kitchen. Is it under the bank, or is it this side of it?'
