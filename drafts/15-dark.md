# 15

On Sunday morning Maylis found that she could not look at Jeannot Castagnet.

She had gone to the café at ten for bread, which it sold on Sundays from a crate on the counter. He was on his stool in his day jacket with a glass of white. She saw the jacket from the door. She crossed the room looking at the crate, and stood beside him, and put her money down with her left hand.

'Lesca,' he said.

'Jeannot.'

She said it to the shelf of bottles. She could see his hand on the counter at the edge of her eye. It had a scar across one knuckle and earth under the nails, and it took hold of the glass and lifted it. She tried to bring her eyes up the sleeve to his face, and they would not go. It was a thing she had done without thought for forty years.

'You have your letter back and your complaint,' he said. 'I stood in that mairie with my cap off like a boy. And now you'll not look at a man. Which of us did the wrong, would you say?'

'I did.'

'Then look at me while you say it.'

She looked at his boots. On the pad at home he was dead in pencil on two pages. She had watched two gendarmes lift the boards of his tonne, and she had kissed his widow. None of that had happened in this café. Her neck did not know it.

Outside the door the owner's wife kept the pots she had not sold at All Saints, marked down to two euros. Maylis had passed them coming in. She took her bread, went out, and picked up a pot of bronze chrysanthemums by its rim. She had gone back in and paid before she knew that she was doing it.

'Who's dead?' said a man at the card table.

She had no answer. Jeannot had turned on his stool to see. She went out with the bread under her arm and the pot in her good hand.

Marie-Jo Labat was coming down the church steps from Mass with her missal. She stopped on the bottom one.

'Those are chrysanthemums.'

'Yes.'

'It's the sixth of December. Nobody carries those after the second of November unless there's a hearse.' She looked at the bandage, and at the pot. 'Where are you going with them?'

'To Maïté Castagnet.'

'With him sitting in the café?' Marie-Jo counted something, and Maylis did not know what. 'What for?'

'I don't know.'

'That's the first true thing you've told me in a fortnight,' Marie-Jo said. She watched her go the length of the square.

* * *

The Castagnet house stood four hundred metres from the mairie, at the edge of the terrace above the Port. Maylis had been to its door perhaps six times, with the commune's notices. She knew the way to the lean-to at the back, and that a ladder hung there on two hooks. She had not been told that.

Maïté opened the door in a housecoat, with a potato in one hand and a knife in the other. She was fifty-five. She looked at the pot before she looked at Maylis.

'Has something happened to him?'

'No. He's at the café.'

'Then who are those for?'

'For you.'

Maïté looked up from the flowers. She was not in black, and she had not lost anyone. Maylis looked at her as she would have looked at a woman at a graveside all the same, and could not put it off her face, and saw Maïté take a step back from it.

'You'd better come in,' Maïté said. 'Before the lane sees.'

The kitchen was warm and smelled of leeks. Maïté took the pot from her, turned once round with it and found no place where a pot of flowers for the dead could be put down in a living house. She stood it in the sink, among the peelings.

'He's sent you. It's about the letters.'

'He doesn't know I'm here.'

'He'll know by dinner.' She put the knife down. 'What do you want with me, Maylis? You've brought me nothing but the notice for the water in sixteen years.'

Maylis had not known until then what she had come to say. It was in her mouth already.

'Your back gutter. Over the bedroom. Is it coming in?'

Maïté went still.

'Since October. It runs down the inside wall and onto the wardrobe. I've a basin up there. He says he'll see to it when the duck are done.' Her eyes had narrowed. 'Who told you about my gutter?'

'Nobody. I could do it. I've a ladder at the shed.'

'With that hand? And you holding your side like a woman of eighty?' Maïté laughed, once, without pleasure. 'The commune's girl up my wall on a Sunday, with chrysanthemums in my sink and my husband at the counter. I'd never hear the end of it. I'd not want to hear the beginning.'

A passage ran from the kitchen to the back of the house. The door at the end of it stood half open. Through it Maylis could see the corner of a bed, and a wardrobe, and on top of the wardrobe an enamel basin with a brown stain on the ceiling over it. Beside the basin lay a suitcase. It was tan, with two straps, and both straps were buckled. It lay flat, with its handle to the room, as a thing lies that is meant to be taken down in one movement. It had dust on it.

Maïté saw where she was looking. She went down the passage without haste and pulled the door shut, and came back.

'Thank you for the flowers,' she said. 'I'll not ask what they're for. Go home and see to your hand.'

At the door she spoke again, lower.

'You looked at me just now as if I'd buried somebody. I've been looked at like that once, at my mother's. Don't do it in the square.'

* * *

She could not fork with either side of her, so at three she put two bales in the bucket of the commune's tractor, to tip over the wire, and drove down through the Port with the bucket at half height.

The Port crossroads lay in front of the care home, where the road off the terrace met the dyke road at a stop line. She stopped at the line. Nothing was coming. She had her left hand on the wheel and the bandaged one in her lap. It was eight minutes past three, in daylight, in a thin rain.

The weight came down on her breastbone.

It was the same weight. It was cold through the waterproof, and the cab filled with the smell of cold steel and old tobacco. She told her foot to come off the clutch, and it stayed. She told her head to turn. Her eyes went left and right along the dyke road, and the rest of her sat on the seat with the engine running.

She had met it lying down for a fortnight. She had let it take her, or gone in past it with her eyes open. She had not thought that it could come to her upright, in gear, with two bales in the air. She did not push. She had no bed to let go into. The rib took her breath in thirds.

A car drew up behind and waited. It sounded its horn. Faces came to the windows of the home, in a row, and stayed there. She could see them without turning.

The side door of the home opened and Coralie came out in her tunic, with no coat, walking fast. She got one foot on the step of the cab and took Maylis's wrist. She did not say anything for half a minute. Then she looked at the right side of her face and the left, and held up two fingers in front of her eyes and moved them, and watched the eyes follow.

'It's not a stroke,' Coralie said, to herself. 'Maylis. Shut your hand on mine.'

The hand lay in hers.

'A hundred and eight. You can hear me. You can't move.' She kept the wrist. The car behind hooted again and she turned her head and looked at it, and it stopped. 'This is what it was. The first night. A hundred and twelve, and you told me it was the rib.'

The little finger of Maylis's left hand bent on the wheel. The rest came back at once, and she got the tractor out of gear and sat with her forearm against her side.

'How many times?'

'By day, this once. At night, each night since the rib. At ten to three.'

'Each night. And I was in the bed.' Coralie had not let go of the wrist. 'I said the box room was for the sleep. I'd not have gone, if I'd known you lay like that with me a hand away.'

'You weren't to know.'

'No. I'm never to know.' She stepped down onto the road. 'Ask me to come back across the landing and I'll come.'

Maylis looked at the row of faces in the windows. She had the words. She had had them since Friday.

'I've the ponies to do,' she said.

Coralie nodded, as she did when a resident refused a tablet. She went back in by the side door.

* * *

The weight came at 02:51 to a bed with one pillow. The phone buzzed at four, and she walked the yard. At twenty-five past she went in by the red figures, with no gap.

She counted. The right hand had no bandage on it there. The weal across the palm was not red. It was a white seam, and old. She read the card.

Then she was given the gate. It came in order, with its papers. A pole had been set at each of three gates, with a camera at the top, at four hundred euros a camera. The gates had no hand winch. Eleven nights of film went in an envelope to the biodiversity office. On nine of the eleven a man knelt on the apron of the gate with a net and looked straight up into the lens, and it was Kévin Labat. He got six months. When he came out she put him to the vote for the outdoor post, and it carried by one, and he was issued the yellow waterproof.

So that was the mower. Over there somebody had looked. Maylis had pushed his money back and had not gone down to the gate after dark for four winters. She had thought that was the straight thing. The other one had kept sixty thousand euros a year for the commune and sent a boy she knew to prison, and then given him her own jacket, and stood by both.

The reflector came behind it, as it had on Thursday. There were the headlamps and the rain, and the back of the white van at the far end of the track, and the red disc burning, round and crooked. There was the hand on the winch, turning fast. This time there was the palm opening on the handle, and the hot line across it.

The alarm began in the box room, across the landing, a long way off. The lamps started to draw together.

She had more to see. She pressed her palms together in her lap and rubbed them, with the seam between, and fixed on the right-hand lamp, which was not hers. She looked at the glass of it and nothing else. It steadied. The alarm went on and grew smaller.

She was still looking at the lamp when she knew that the wall on her left had gone.

She turned her head. There was no door there, and no steel. The floor of the box ran out level onto the boards of the village hall.

It was set for the loto. The trestle tables stood in their rows under the strip lights, and they ran on further than the hall was long. The smell was floor wax and the coffee urn. Somewhere up at the stage end a voice was calling numbers, each one twice, and it was not a voice she could put a face to.

The chairs were taken. She knew everyone. Marie-Jo was there with a clipboard, and Sarthou, and the secretary. They were not quite still. Each of them had an edge that did not sit true, as in a photograph of somebody who has moved. Her mother was at the end of the second row in her coat, pushing a kernel onto a card with one finger. She was seventy. She was also thirty, with dark hair, doing the same.

Nothing was on Maylis's chest. Nothing had been so quiet in a fortnight.

One chair was empty. It stood at the end of the third row, nearest to her, four metres off across the boards. A card was laid in front of it and a saucer of kernels beside the card.

She did not get up. She sat with her hands pressed together and looked at the chair, until the lamp went out of the edge of her eye and she had a ceiling.

* * *

It was twenty past six. She lay for a time before she knew which ceiling it was, and that the door across the landing was Coralie's.

She wrote with her left hand.

*Monday. Cameras on the gates. She filmed Kévin. Six months. Then she gave him my job. That's the jacket.*

*The reflector again. Her hand on the winch, and it tore.*

*The wall went. The hall, set for the loto. Everyone twice. A chair kept for me.*

She read the middle entry again. On Thursday she had written that the other one drove into him. A woman did not drive into a van with her hand on a winch. She had been on the platform that night, turning it for all she was worth, with his van out at the end of the track in her lights and the water coming. They had given her a medal after, and a stone. They had also buried him.

*She was at the gate. She went down the track for him. She got there too late.*

It was why she mended the roof.

Downstairs the telephone rang in the kitchen. Coralie had her coat on to leave. Maylis heard her cross the tiles and lift it.

'Yes, Marie-Jo,' Coralie said.
