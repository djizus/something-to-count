# 15

On Sunday at half past four she was given the cameras, and after them more of the night with the reflector in it. Then she stayed too long.

The cameras came as the lease had come, as a file. She was at the council table with an estimate in front of her for three cameras at four hundred euros each, one above each gate. Under it lay a letter from the prefecture. The commune's Natura grant was sixty thousand euros a year, and conditional on the netting of glass eels at the gates being brought to an end. She put it to the table as sixty thousand euros against the nets of five men, and it carried.

Then there were eleven nights of film. She watched them at the mayor's desk with the door shut. Four men stood at a gate in the dark under hoods. The fifth looked straight up into the lens with a lamp on his forehead and his mouth open, and it was Kévin Labat. He did it on nine of the eleven nights.

She put the film in an envelope for the biodiversity office and signed across the flap with the stroke that crossed itself.

After that there was a courtroom, with Marie-Jo in the second row in her Sunday coat, and a man saying six months. There was a council sitting, and a vote of eight to seven. And there was the mayor's office again, with Kévin Labat standing in it, thinner, and her own hands holding out to him a yellow waterproof with the commune's name across the back.

So that was the jacket. He had been on the mower in it because she had put him there, after she had put him in prison.

Maylis sat in the chair with it. For four winters she had kept the key and looked the other way, and had been paid nothing, and had thought that was the whole of what could be done. The other one had looked. She had looked for eleven nights and sent what she saw to the men in green. Then she had given him the job, and it had carried by one vote.

Then the square of the council table went, and it was raining.

It was the night she had had on Thursday, and she had more of it. The headlamps lay along the track. The white van stood at the end, with the round reflector burning on its back door, crooked. The reflector did not come nearer. The van was standing still, and so was she. She was sitting at a wheel with the engine running, forty metres short of it, and the wipers going.

Then there was the winch, and her right hand on the handle in the dark, turning fast. This time she had the pain of it. The handle ran in her fist and took a line out of the palm.

Then a torch, and the hatch of the tonne open at her feet. She had had the duckboards on Tuesday. She had not had what was under the hatch ladder. It was water. It stood black and still to the second rung from the top.

In another room an alarm was going.

It was Coralie's, across the landing, and it was six. The panel had begun to go pale at the edges. Maylis did what Kévin had told her to do on the way in, and did it on the way out. She stayed on one thing. She took the right-hand lamp, which had not been hers in 2014, and kept her eyes on it. She pressed her palms together between her knees, the bandage against the skin, and the weal stung, and the steel came back.

She held it past the alarm. She held it until the alarm stopped.

Something had changed on her left. She had been afraid of that side since Tuesday, because of the door. She turned her head.

There was no door. There was no wall for it to be in. Where the fourth side of the box had been, the floor went on, and it was waxed boards.

It was the hall. The strip lights were on. Trestle tables stood end to end under them with paper cloth along them, and they did not stop at three rows or at the back wall. They ran on. At every place there was a card and a saucer of maize kernels, and at every place somebody was sitting, with their head down.

She knew them. She knew the backs. They were not quite still. Each one was a little more than one, like a photograph of a person who has moved, a shoulder and then the same shoulder beside it. Her mother was at the end of the second row with her coat on, pushing a kernel onto a card with one finger. Beside her sat a woman of thirty in a summer dress, with her mother's hands, doing the same.

A voice was calling numbers to the back wall.

'Fifty-three,' it said. 'Five, three.'

The hall smelled of floor wax and of the urn. There was no steel in it. Nothing was on her chest, and the rib did not hurt, and her palm had stopped stinging.

At the end of the nearest row, four metres from the chair, one place was empty. A card lay in front of it, and a saucer.

She did not get up. She did not know that she could. She looked at the empty place for as long as she had it, which was the length of one number.

'Seventy-eight. Seven, eight.'

Then she had a lampshade and a ceiling with rain on the tiles above it. For a little while she could not have said whose they were. The clock radio read 06:21.

* * *

She wrote it left-handed, over three pages, and the hall took one of them. Then she went back to Thursday and read what she had put there. *She drove into him.*

She had not. The van had been standing still, and so had the other one, forty metres off. She had not hit anything. She had gone to a winch and turned it until it took the skin off her hand, and then she had gone down to the tonne with a torch. The tonne was full.

So there had been a night as well as a lake. The river had come, in the rain, and he had been in his cellar. The other one had worked the gate and gone down the track for him. She had got there, and looked down the hatch, and the water was at the second rung.

*She didn't run him down. She went for him. She got to the tonne too late.*

That was what the medal was for. The priest asked her each year because of it, and Maïté wore black for it and wiped her cheek. The stone would be for it too, with his name on the line above. Nobody had been to blame. Maylis read it over and found that it was so.

She had decided that by nine. At ten she was in the supermarket at Hontaube, which opened on Sunday mornings, buying a pot of chrysanthemums.

They were bronze, and had been reduced since the second of November to three euros fifty. She did not know why she was buying them. She had not known when she took the van out, either, or when she counted out the coins with her left hand. She knew only that there was a thing she owed, and that it was owed to Maïté Castagnet, and that it had been owed for a long time.

* * *

Jeannot's Trafic was outside the café when she parked by the church. He was getting out of it in his day jacket.

'Lesca,' he said. It was a nod, and no more.

She found that she could not look at him. She tried. She got as far as the collar of the jacket. Above it was a face she had seen every week of her life, and that morning she had stood over his hatch with a torch. She looked at the back door of the van, and the reflector on it.

'What's that you've got?'

'Flowers.'

'Who for?'

'The cemetery,' she said. The cemetery was the other way.

He went into the café. She crossed the square with the pot held against her left side, in front of the café window, and took the road along the edge of the terrace, which led to four houses and no graves. Five men watched her do it. One of them lived in the fourth house.

Marie-Jo Labat was at the door of the hall with the keys, counting stacked chairs through the glass. She turned round.

'Where are you going with those?'

'To Maïté's.'

'Maïté Castagnet.' Marie-Jo looked at the pot for about as long as it takes to count a chair. 'With chrysanthemums. On a Sunday, with him in the café.' She waited. 'Has somebody died that I've not been told about?'

'No.'

'Then you'll want to think what you say when she opens the door,' Marie-Jo said. 'Because I can't think of anything.'

* * *

Maïté opened the door in a blue housecoat, with a tea towel over her shoulder. She was not in black. Maylis had known she would not be, and looked for it all the same.

'I brought you these.'

Maïté looked at the pot. In the Landes chrysanthemums go to the cemetery on the first of November and nowhere else. Nobody had carried them into a kitchen in her lifetime.

'Who's dead?'

'Nobody.'

'Then what are they for?'

'I don't know,' Maylis said. 'I wanted to bring you something.'

Maïté considered her on the step. Then she stood back from the door, which was more than she need have done, and Maylis went in.

It was a kitchen with an oilcloth and a telephone on the wall. Maïté took the pot in both hands, as she would have taken a thing that dripped. She looked at the table and at the windowsill, and stood it in the sink.

'He took his letter back on Friday,' she said, with her back turned. 'And the complaint. Thirty-one years, and I've not known him take back so much as a remark. He came in on Wednesday at one and sat in that chair and didn't speak. I asked him what it was. He said the Lesca girl. He's not said it since. He says Lesca.'

'I asked him to withdraw it.'

'People have asked him things since 1995.' Maïté turned round and dried her hands, which were not wet. She looked at Maylis with attention. 'He's been in at nights since. Three nights, in that chair. He's not been down to the tonne.' She said it as a woman reports a pipe that has started to knock. 'I'd like to know how you did it.'

'I'm sorry,' Maylis said.

'What for?'

It came out before she had anything to put behind it. 'For not getting there. For what it's been for you since.'

Maïté stood with the tea towel in her hands. 'Getting where?'

Maylis did not answer, because there was no answer in that kitchen. Beyond Maïté a door stood half open on a bedroom. There was a bed in it, made, and a wardrobe. On top of the wardrobe sat a brown suitcase with two straps, both buckled, and a paper label tied to the handle. It had no dust on it. Everything else up there had.

Maïté saw where she was looking. She crossed the kitchen and pulled the bedroom door to with one finger.

'Winter things,' she said.

At the front door Maylis bent, as people did, and kissed her on the cheek. It was the same cheek. Maïté held her face still for it. Then she stood in her doorway with her hands in the pockets of the housecoat, and did not take them out.

* * *

The round bales were too heavy for one hand and a rib. At two she took the commune's tractor down to the low meadows with one on the spike.

The road to the dyke crossed the Port at the care home. She stopped at the crossroads for a car, with the bale in the air in front of her, and when the car had gone she did not move off.

The weight had come down on her through her waterproof. It was daylight, and raining, and the engine was running. She tried her right hand on the lever and nothing went down the arm. She had the smell, in an open cab. She had her eyes.

She knew what to do with them. She took the lamp over the door of the care home, which burned all day, and stayed on it. She did not let the windscreen go to steel. It was all she could do, and it kept her on a tractor at a crossroads. It did not give her back her hands.

She was there for six minutes. A car came up behind and went round her. A second sounded its horn. The door of the care home opened, and Coralie came down the steps in her tunic with a cardigan over her head.

She did not shout. She climbed the two steps to the cab and looked at Maylis's face, and at her eyes, which moved. She laid two fingers inside the wrist that was lying on the lever.

'A hundred and four,' she said. 'Can you hear me?'

Maylis looked at her, and then back at the lamp over the door.

'That's yes.' Coralie kept the wrist. She was counting something else now, with her eyes on Maylis's chest. 'So this is what it is at three. I've lain next to it for a fortnight and thought it was you holding your breath.'

The little finger moved. The hand came after it. Maylis took a breath that went all the way down, and said Coralie's name, and it came out.

'Don't drive that,' Coralie said. 'I'll get the minibus key. Your mother's ponies can wait twenty minutes.'

* * *

They did not speak about it at supper. The shopping pad lay between them with its boxes empty.

At eight the telephone rang in the kitchen. Coralie looked at the screen, and then across the table.

'It's Marie-Jo,' she said. 'For me.'

She listened, standing. She said, 'Chrysanthemums.' She said, 'To the house.' Then she took the telephone up the stairs with her, into the box room, and shut the door.
