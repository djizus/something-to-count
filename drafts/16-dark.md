# 16

On Monday morning the other one left the chair first, and Lesca went with her.

She had gone in awake at 04:27 with the five words and said them until the alarm began, a long way off. Then she had fixed on the left lamp and rubbed her palms, and held. On Sunday the seal of the door had given on her left and she had not looked. On Monday there was no door to look at. The wall had gone from that side, and the steel floor ran out level onto waxed boards.

It had come in. It sat on the boards a little way out from her chair, the brown leaf and the rivets, with a back to her and a back to what lay beyond. In twelve years she had not seen it anywhere but on a wardrobe or on herself.

Beyond it was the village hall, set for the loto, with the tables running further than the hall was long and a voice calling numbers. One chair was empty, at the end of the nearest row, and a card lay in front of it. She read anything that was laid at a place. She could not see how to get past what sat between.

While she held, she was given the gauge. It was the one on the pier of the old gate, with her father's red line across it, and rain running down the iron. The river stood fifty-eight centimetres under the line.

Then the warmth went out of the arms of the chair. It went from under her forearms and from behind her spine, all at once, as it goes from a bed when someone gets up. She was still looking at the lamp. She did not let go of it. And she was no longer sitting.

She was on her back under a paper lampshade, with rain on the tiles. Her right hand was bound across the palm, and it stung. A rib caught on the same side when she breathed. There was one pillow. Downstairs a telephone rang, and a woman crossed the kitchen and lifted it, and it was Coralie's step. She heard her say Marie-Jo's name, and then one word, chrysanthemums.

Lesca had that ceiling for nine breaths. She counted them, since they were all she had to count.

Then it was on her. She did not see it come. It was on the breastbone with its back to her, and the tiles over it were dry and made no sound, and Coralie had her hand on the clock radio.

'Twenty-two minutes,' Coralie said. 'I shook you. I said the title, and then I said the other. I had the number for Bayonne in my hand.' She put the clock down. 'I'd like to know which of you I'd have been ringing about.'

She went out, and her door shut.

Lesca counted her fingers, both hands, and made ten. She turned the right one over and looked for a bandage. There was the seam.

She wrote before she moved, and the last entry she wrote as a finding.

*7. When the other one gets up and I am still sitting, I go with her. I was in her bed for nine breaths, awake. It fetched me back.*

For twenty-two minutes there had been a woman in this bed with her eyes open who could not be woken. If it had not gone for her, there would have been one still.

* * *

At eight she took down the flood file of 2014, for the third morning running. The river had stood fifty-eight centimetres under the red line on 7 December of that year. The figure was in her own hand, copied from the association's book.

She looked at it and at the notebook. Then she put the file back in the cupboard behind the secretary's desk. She did not take it down on any morning after that. She knew now what it would say on Tuesday, and on each day up to Friday.

* * *

The commemoration committee sat at ten, with five days to go. Père Honoré had been asked to attend for the order of the procession, and had a chair by the radiator.

The sitting opened with the minute. Marie-Jo Labat set the white timer and stood it on the table, and they got up. Maïté Castagnet stood across from the chair in her black coat, with her handbag held in front of her in both hands, and looked at the radiator. Lesca looked at the timer.

She had stood for that minute for eleven years, and she gave it to the figures, which were forty houses and a care home. That Monday she had been in another woman's bed four hours before, and some of it had not gone back.

He was standing at the driver's door of the Trafic in the first light, with his hands in his pockets. He was telling her that her father would have hooked it from the other side. She had the chain in her fists. And at the end of that month there had been a pay slip with the diesel stopped out of it, for pulling him out. He had not offered a centime. He had sent the mairie a registered letter about the ruts. He owed her for the diesel yet.

She laughed.

It was one laugh, short, through the nose, and it was out before she knew she had it. It was easy. It belonged to somebody who had stood at a counter beside him ten days before and been given a coffee on his say. She had not laughed in that room since she took the chair.

The timer went on. Maïté had turned her head from the radiator. She looked at Lesca as she had not looked at her on the roof or on the dyke, or over eleven wreaths.

'What is there to laugh at in him, Madame le maire?' she said. 'Tell me. I'd be glad of it.'

Lesca had a tank of diesel to tell her, from a January this room had never had.

'I've stood here with you each month for eleven years,' Maïté said. 'I've held my tongue for that minute because you held yours. I've asked who was meant to go round the tonnes. I never thought to ask whether anybody found it funny.'

She took her bag by the handle and went out. She did not shut the door hard. They heard her on the stairs.

Lesca told her mouth to call the committee to order, and it did not open.

It was on her. It was on her breastbone as it was at night, with its back to her and the leaf a hand from her chin, and she was standing up. It had not come down off anything. The council room had a cupboard and a radiator and a clock, and it had come from none of them. It sat on a woman on her feet as it sat on one lying down, and she did not fall. Her knees had locked under her and her hands lay on the table edge. The room had smelled of floor polish. It smelled now of cold steel and old tobacco.

She had laughed with what was not hers, and it had found her before Maïté was off the stairs.

Her eyes moved. They gave her the timer, with forty seconds to run. They gave her seven members, the secretary and the priest, on their feet round a table, not knowing whether a minute was still being kept.

Guy Larrère sat down. He did it without hurry and folded his hands. The first deputy looked at him and sat. Then the rest sat, one after another, and the secretary, and Père Honoré last, on his chair by the radiator, with his hat on his knees.

The mayor of Peyrebarthe did not sit.

'Madame le maire,' Marie-Jo said. She said it to the wall, as she always did, and then she looked down from the wall. 'The minute's broken. You can sit.'

Lesca told her knees to bend. She did not push at it. She stood, and the timer ticked. Nine people watched her not sit down. She could see each of them between the edge of its back and the window. They saw a woman at the head of a table with her mouth a little open, who had laughed at a dead man and would not take her chair.

'Is she ill?' the first deputy said.

'She's counting something,' Larrère said. 'Leave her.'

The timer rang.

It got down. She did not see where it went. Her knees gave, and she had the chair under her, and the air came in to the bottom.

'The sitting is suspended,' she said. 'It resumes at six.'

* * *

Père Honoré found her at the stone at twenty past ten, on the lettered side.

'Madame Castagnet would not have me walk her home,' he said. 'She asked me what there was to laugh at. I told her I did not know. It is the first question of hers in eleven years that I have been able to answer truthfully.'

'He owes me for diesel.'

He waited.

'I towed his van out of the mud and it was stopped from my pay. He wrote to the mairie about the ruts. For a second he was standing there, Father, and he hadn't paid.'

'When was this?'

'January 2017.'

Père Honoré looked at the top line of the stone, and then at her. He had buried the man in December 2014. He did not say so.

'I have heard you once,' he said. 'I know what you carry, and I have not known you to make a thing up. So I will not tell you that you are ill. I do not think you would thank me.' He turned his hat round by the brim. 'You stood for forty seconds and could not sit. I watched your eyes. You were looking for a way past something.' He put the hat on. 'He is in the cemetery. His wife is at her sink. And you laughed. I say only that all three are so.'

He had shaken her hand at the church door for eleven years as he shook everyone's. He went to his Clio without offering it.

* * *

The telephone on her desk rang at ten to one. It was the home.

'I've had three in my office since twelve,' Coralie said. 'The cook had it from the baker's van. It's in the café that you laughed at the minute. Maïté Castagnet walked out, and you stood there after like a post and wouldn't sit, with the priest watching.'

'It was one laugh.'

'Is it true?'

'Yes.'

The line was quiet. Lesca could hear the home behind it, a tray set down and a door.

'There are things I've not asked you about that winter,' Coralie said. 'I've not asked them for twelve years, so that you could stand at that table every month with a straight face. I thought that was what I was for. If you're going to laugh, I'd like to know what I held my tongue for.'

'What haven't you asked?'

'Not on the home's telephone.'

She rang off. Lesca sat with the receiver in her hand. She had always put Coralie's silence down to tact. She saw that it might be put down to something else, and she did not follow that. She had a sitting at six.

* * *

She took the notebook out of the safe and wrote in it by day, which she had not done before. She headed the page as a motion.

*That the mayor remain seated.*

There was a night coming over there, and she knew its date. It had a river in it, an old dyke and a telephone. If she was sitting in that chair when the other one got up, she would go with her. She would wake in a bed with one pillow, with a rib and a bound hand, in a house where it rained on the tiles. She would be at the gate that night herself. When the order came she would not need five words. She would put down the handle, walk the track and knock.

She set against it what she had struck out on Saturday. No flood had come there yet, and the other one had walked no track. Nobody over there had chosen better than Lesca had, since nobody had been asked.

She wanted that village all the same, and she wrote down why. It had a parking space in front of the mairie. It had forty-one letters in a file, and the man to write the next. Marie-Jo Labat told her where to put the trestles. On the twelfth of December its café would open at seven, as on any Saturday, and nobody in it would stand.

Then she put what she would leave. A commune of nine hundred had a first deputy. A prefect could read a speech. Kévin Labat could be shown the Castagnet roof. Coralie had been across the landing since February 2015. And in this bed there would be a woman with her eyes open who did not wake.

She read the motion down. It had one fault.

It had fetched her back. She had been gone nine breaths, and it had been on her before she had finished hearing the rain. To stay gone she would have to keep it off her. In twelve years she had not learned how that was done. Pushed, it pressed as hard. Left alone, it took what it had come for. She had four nights to learn it, and she could not ask the other one which of them would get up.

So the sentence stayed in. It needed until Friday to cross, and it would cross whether she was fetched or not.

She shut the notebook in the safe. The church clock struck three, and after it she said the five words. She said them eleven times before six.

* * *

Seven members came back, and the secretary. Maïté Castagnet's chair was empty, with the agenda squared in front of it. Père Honoré's was against the wall.

Lesca reopened the sitting and gave the first item, which was the order of the procession. Guy Larrère raised a finger. Marie-Jo turned the timer to three and stood it in front of him.

'I ask that two facts of this morning's sitting be recorded,' he said. 'During the minute's silence for Jean Castagnet, the chair laughed. His widow heard it and left. The chair then remained standing for forty seconds after the committee had sat, and did not answer when she was spoken to. I saw both. So did the priest. A member may ask that a fact be minuted.'

'The chair has an interest,' Lesca said. 'She can't rule on it. I'll put it to the committee.'

'I'd sooner you did.' He looked along the table. He had waited eleven years to be handed something in that room, and he held it with care. 'She counts her fingers. She sleeps in the chair. This morning she laughed at a drowned man. I don't say what's the matter with her. I say it should be written down, for whoever has to read it after.'

The timer had two minutes left on it. He did not use them.

'Those in favour,' Lesca said.

Larrère's hand went up, and two beside him. The first deputy looked at the empty chair and raised his. Marie-Jo was last. She had said that nobody was to laugh at the fingers in her kitchen. She put her hand up without looking at the wall or at Lesca, and kept it up until she had been counted.

'Five,' the secretary said. 'Against?'

There were two. Lesca did not vote.

The secretary wrote that at the sitting of the morning, during the minute's silence, the mayor had laughed, and that Madame Castagnet had withdrawn. She wrote that the mayor had then remained standing. She read it back to the table, and it was carried.
