# 16

On Monday, eleven seconds into the minute's silence for Jean Castagnet, the mayor of Peyrebarthe laughed.

It was the last meeting of the commemoration committee before the twelfth. Nine members stood behind their chairs in the council room, and the secretary stood at the end with the register shut. Père Honoré was there by invitation, to settle the blessing of the dyke. Marie-Jo Labat had set the white timer to one minute and stood it on the table. Maïté Castagnet held her handbag in front of her in both hands and looked at the radiator.

Lesca stood at the head of the table with her fingers on the back of the mayor's chair. She was not thinking of the sentence. She was thinking that he had never paid her for the diesel.

It was fourteen euros sixty. It had been stopped out of her wage in January 2017, for the commune's tractor, on the morning she pulled his van out of the mud. He had stood beside it in his waders and told her that her father would have hooked the chain from the other side. Then he had gone home and written to the mairie about the ruts.

She laughed. It was one breath through the nose and the start of a second, the laugh of a woman who has been docked for a morning's diesel by a man who then complained of her. The whole room heard where it began and where she shut it off.

Maïté Castagnet turned from the radiator. In eleven years of minutes she had not once looked at the head of the table while the timer ran.

'What's funny, Madame le maire?'

Lesca meant to beg her pardon. She told her mouth to open.

It was on her. She was on her feet, and it sat on her breastbone as it sat there at night, with its back to her and the brown leaf level with her chin. It had another back to the table. It had never come into this room, and for the rest of that minute the council room smelled of the barn. She tried the hand on the chair, and nothing went down the arm. She had her eyes. She had the committee past the edge of it, and when she breathed in the leaves lifted.

Nobody else looked at it. They were looking at her.

'I've stood for him in this room eleven years,' Maïté said. 'You've been up on my roof twice a winter, and you've never laughed on it. I'd have liked to hear what it was.'

She waited. What she had in front of her was the mayor, upright, with one hand on a chair and her mouth shut, looking at her and not answering.

Maïté took her bag into her left hand, which she did not do in that room, and went out. The door closed behind her on its spring. Père Honoré had his coat off the hook and was after her before it had latched.

The timer rang.

Seven members sat down, and the secretary. The chairs scraped and were still. Lesca stood. She could hear Marie-Jo turn the timer back to nought, and a lorry changing down in the square.

'Through the chair,' Marie-Jo said, to the wall. 'The minute is over.'

Lesca did not sit. Guy Larrère had turned in his seat to see it better. The secretary had her pen up. The priest had stopped in the doorway with his coat half on. There were nine of them in the room, and they watched the mayor of Peyrebarthe not sit down.

Her little finger moved on the back of the chair, and the hand came after it. It got down off her as it got down off a bed. She heard it go across the floorboards, with its dry noise, and there was nothing between her and the table.

She sat.

'Item one,' she said. 'The prefect's time of arrival.'

* * *

It had not been her laugh, and she knew how she had come by it.

That morning she had gone in awake at 04:26 with the five words. It was in the room before her. It sat on the steel floor inside the door, where the seal had given on Sunday, with a back to her and a back to the door, and she did not look at it for long. She said the sentence to the lamps. Between two sayings she was given the gauge on the gate pier, in rain, with the river seventy-four centimetres under her father's line.

Her alarm began at six, a long way off, in the dry house. She took the left lamp and held, with her palms together and her eyes on the wire behind the glass. On Sunday she had stayed nineteen minutes. She meant to see how long there was.

The other one got up.

Lesca did not see it. She had it through the chair. The warmth went out of the arms, on the right and then the left, as it goes out of a bench when the person beside you stands. And the chair went with her. Lesca had hold of one lamp, and the lamp stayed where it was, small, as at the wrong end of a field glass. She was getting out of a bed.

It was her bedroom, with the paper lampshade, and it was not her bed. There was one pillow on it, on the wrong side. Rain was running on the tiles. She sat up by stages, and a rib took her under the right arm, and her forearm went to it before she had told it to. It had been going there for a fortnight. The right hand had a bandage across the palm. On the chest of drawers an envelope stood against the clock radio.

She had the other one's fortnight on like a coat. She knew where the commune's van was parked. She knew there were bales to take down, and that Jeannot Castagnet had taken a letter back on Friday, and that she could not look him in the face. He owed her for diesel. In that bedroom it was a thing to be annoyed about over coffee.

It lasted three breaths, and they hurt on the right.

Then the cold was on her breastbone, in that bed too. Her hand came off the lamp as a hand comes off a hot plate. She was on her back in the dry house with it sitting on her, and the clock radio read 06:37.

It got down when Coralie came round the bed. Coralie had the telephone in her hand.

'Thirty-seven minutes,' Coralie said. 'I said yesterday I didn't know who to ring. I rang your mother.'

'What did she say?'

'On your side. And that she'd told you so.' She laid the telephone face down on the blanket. 'You came back looking at that lampshade as if it belonged to somebody else.'

'It did, for a bit.'

Coralie did not ask what that meant. She had stopped asking on Sunday. She went down, and she did not leave for the home until half past eight, which made her late for the first time since she had run it.

Lesca wrote before she moved. It came to two lines.

*Held 37 minutes. She got up, and I went with her. Three breaths in her bedroom.*

*Finding. If I hold when she gets up, I go where she goes. It fetched me back, and I let go.*

At eight she was in the mairie with the notebook. Before she shut the safe she took down the flood file of 2014 once more, and found the seventh of December. The river had stood seventy-four centimetres under the red line.

She put the file back in the cupboard. She did not take it down again. There was nothing in it now that she would not be given at four in the morning, a day at a time, to the centimetre.

At the red light on Saturday it had come to her with the sentence in her mouth. In the council room it had come on a laugh. Both had been the other one's before they were hers.

* * *

She took the items in order.

She took the prefect's arrival and the closing of the square to traffic from eight. The flag would be carried by Sarthou, for his cousin, and it went into the minutes in those words. Larrère raised his eyebrows and found no article against it.

Père Honoré came back at eleven and sat down by the door with his coat on.

'Madame Castagnet asks that the meeting go on,' he said. 'She will lay the wreath on Saturday. She asks not to be kissed.'

'It will go in the order of ceremony,' Lesca said.

She had kissed Maïté Castagnet on the cheek at eleven commemorations, in front of the stone, with the prefecture's photographer waiting. Each year Maïté had held her face still for it, and afterwards had taken off a glove and wiped the cheek with her bare hand, twice. This year it had been seen to in advance.

The church clock struck. Lesca counted ten on the agenda, with eight people watching her do it. Then she gave the five words to the water jug without moving her lips.

A mayor who has laughed at a dead man in front of his widow, and then stood like a post, may suspend the sitting. She had never suspended one. She stayed in the chair, and it was there, between the marquee and the bull, that she went through the other question as she had gone through the dyke and the lake and the cameras.

She had the figures.

No village existed in which she had done better. She had struck that line out on Saturday and initialled it. There was a village in which nothing had been asked of her yet, and it was raining there. On the night of Friday to Saturday the river would come over her father's line. A telephone would ring. A woman with her hands and a cracked rib would be told to keep the gate shut, and a man in waders would be asleep in his cellar at the end of a track. That woman had never stood there. Lesca had.

On the other side of the account was this room. There were nine hundred people, and a stone with her name on the second line. There was a speech, typed, and a gutter not yet seen to. And there was Coralie, standing over a bed with a telephone.

The commune was provided for. If a mayor died in office, the first deputy acted until the council met to elect another. He had bought his own sash. The speech was in the top drawer of her desk, and anyone could read it out.

She had put forty houses against one man, once, and had been right. This sum was one man against one mayor, and a commune can find another mayor. In that village he had only the one woman with the key.

She would have said that she was going there to knock. It was true, and it was not all of it. She wanted a café where he stood at the counter owing her for diesel. She wanted to have laughed as she had laughed that morning in a room where nobody turned round.

She would stay in the chair. On Friday night she would take the left lamp and keep it, whatever rang and whatever sat on her, and when the other one got up she would go down those stairs with her. She would be at the gate with her this time, and she knew which way to walk.

She had let go that morning after three breaths. She had not meant to. It had come for her, and her hand had opened. She did not know that it would stay shut on Friday. The sentence needed its week, and it had had two days. If she failed at the lamp, it had still to arrive without her, and so she went on sending it.

'Item five,' she said. 'The bull.'

By then Maïté had been seen crossing the square with her bag in the wrong hand, and the priest after her. By noon the café had it, in two versions. In the first the mayor had laughed at the widow, and in the second at the drowned man. In both she had then stood and stared the widow out of the room, and in both she had always been hard. Neither had any diesel in it.

* * *

At ten past twelve she came to any other business.

'I've one thing,' Guy Larrère said.

'You've three minutes.'

'I'll not need them.' He had his hands folded on the agenda. 'At the opening of this sitting, during the minute's silence for Jean Castagnet, the mayor laughed. I ask that it be minuted.'

The secretary looked at the chair.

'Through the chair,' Marie-Jo said. She was looking at the wall above Lesca's head. 'Against. It isn't a fact of the sitting. It's a noise that got out of a person. I've made one myself at a christening.'

'A member may ask that a fact of the sitting be recorded,' Larrère said. 'It's the rule the chair applied to me on the dyke, about her fingers. I didn't make it.'

'No,' Lesca said. 'I did.'

She looked down the table. Maïté's chair was empty and pushed in. Père Honoré sat by the door and had no vote. In front of Marie-Jo the timer stood at nought.

'He's within the rule. I'll put it. The chair has an interest and doesn't vote.' She uncapped her pen. 'That it be minuted that during the minute's silence the mayor laughed. Those in favour.'

Six hands went up, and Larrère's was the first.

'Against.'

Marie-Jo raised hers, by itself, and kept it up until the secretary had counted it.

'Carried,' Lesca said, 'by six votes to one.'
