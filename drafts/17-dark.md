# 17

On Tuesday the other one got up first, and Maylis went with her.

She had gone in at twenty-five past four by the red figures. The wall was back on her left that night, with the door in it. The door stood open inwards, and it was sitting in the doorway, against the door, with its back to her. It had come in that way and had gone no further. Over the hump of leaf she had the strip lights of the hall and the end of the third row, and the empty chair with its card. A voice was calling numbers. She held the right-hand lamp at the edge of her eye and looked past its back for a long time.

Then the warmth went out of the chair. It went from the arms and from behind her spine at once, as it goes from a bed when somebody gets out. She had not let go of the lamp. She was no longer sitting.

She was on her back in the middle of a bed, under the lampshade she knew, and no rain was falling on the tiles. The window was white with frost. A blue notebook came up over her face in a right hand that had no bandage on it, and a pen. The hand wrote a date across the top of the page, quickly, in a script that leaned. There was nobody on either side of her. In the doorway a woman in a dressing gown said, 'Fourteen minutes,' and it was Coralie's voice, and she said it as she would have read a chart to a doctor.

Maylis had that for as long as it took the pen to reach the end of the line.

Then it was on her breastbone, in her own bed, with the rain going on the tiles and one pillow under her head. It got down. Coralie was in the doorway there too, in her tunic, with her coat over her arm.

'Your phone went at four,' Coralie said. 'I came across at six. Your eyes were open. I said your name four times, and you weren't behind them.'

'I'm here.'

'You are now.' She went down.

Maylis counted her left hand on the blanket, thumb first, and made five. She took the pad from under the pillow and wrote with that hand, since the other was still bound.

*Tuesday. She got up and I stayed sitting, and I went with her. Her bed. Dry, frost, a notebook. It fetched me back.*

* * *

She came down slowly. On the stairs she had the banister under her hand and could not have said who had painted it.

The kitchen was lit. A woman stood at the sink with her back turned, rinsing a bowl, and Maylis looked along the table for the keys of the home, which lay beside that bowl on any morning. There was a shopping pad with three boxes on it. She looked at the window for the frost. It had rain on it.

She had the river in her head. It was rising, and there were forty-two beds behind a dyke.

'When does the director get in?' she said. 'I want her in my office at nine. The home needs a plan for the minibus before Friday.'

Coralie turned round with the bowl in her hands.

'Your office.'

Maylis heard it then. She had a shed, with nine boxes for the lights and a spike of delivery notes. The director of the home was a Madame Duhalde from Bayonne, who came in at nine and had spoken to her twice, about the hedge.

'I meant the shed,' she said.

'You didn't.' Coralie put the bowl on the rack. 'You said it as if I answered to you. You've an office now, and a widow you take flowers to, and a thing you want that isn't here. I've sat across this table from you for sixteen years. I don't know who I gave coffee to this morning.'

'It took me a minute. I was slow coming back.'

'From where?'

Maylis did not say. Coralie waited, with her coat on, for as long as she could spare before an early shift.

An envelope stood on the dresser behind the fruit bowl. It had stood there since September, when the home was told it would close in June. It was addressed to the personnel office of the hospital at Bayonne and it had a stamp on it. Maylis had dusted round it for three months and had not asked. Coralie had not said.

Coralie took her keys from the hook. On her way to the door she lifted the envelope out from behind the bowl and put it in her bag.

* * *

At eight Maylis was on the platform at the gate, in the rain, with her bound hand in her pocket.

The river was at her father's line. It lay along the red paint from one edge of the gauge to the other, brown, with branches going down it, and each small wave put the line under and gave it back. On Thursday it had stood a metre below. The line was the height of the doorsteps in the Port. From now on the dyke was holding the river above forty houses.

The low meadows were green behind her, with a hand of water in the grass from the rain alone. The ponies stood on the rise by the fence. The hump of the tonne was out in the middle. No lamp had burned there for six nights.

Sarthou came along the dyke from his house in a cape, with a notebook. His house was the last in the Port, hard against the bank.

'On the line,' he said. He wrote it down, with the hour. 'My step is twelve centimetres under your father's paint. I measured it in 2009. If it comes over the bank I'm the first to be told, and it'll not be by telephone.'

'It stays shut over the line. Larrère said so on the dyke.'

'I heard him. I was the one in the gendarme's cape.' He shut the notebook. 'My cousin's file is closed. The eighteenth is on again, and I've asked Marie-Jo for the hall a fifth time. I don't ask what you had on him. I've been a gendarme thirty-one years and I know when not to.'

'Thank you.'

'Don't. He's been at home six nights, in his kitchen, in his waders. He's not been out to the tonne. And the café has it that you're courting his wife.' He looked at the river. 'Chrysanthemums, they say, on a Sunday. They say the girls at Arlette's are down to one, and the one's looking about her.'

'I took her a pot of flowers.'

'I know what you took. I'd leave that house alone. My cousin staying in of a night puts out more people than you'd think.' He did not say which people. He went back along the bank to his hedge, looking over his shoulder at the water.

* * *

Her mother was at the kitchen door when she brought the van back into the yard for the rake.

'Two lights on your landing since Friday,' Arlette said. 'One each side. I'm not asking. I see what I see from my sink.'

'Coralie's on earlies. It's for the sleep.'

'Marie-Jo rang me before she rang Coralie. Chrysanthemums, to a woman whose husband's sitting in the café. Your father took me a goose once, and that was understood.' She held her cardigan shut. 'What's the river doing?'

'It's at his line.'

'Do I bring them up?'

'It stays shut over the line. The meadows only take what falls on them.'

'Then they stay. They've never been brought up for rain.' Arlette counted something on the yard that was not there. 'If that gate were ever opened with the river up, I'd want an hour's warning. It takes an hour, with the old mare.'

'Nobody's opening it.'

'I know that. I'm saying what it takes,' Arlette said.

* * *

The post came at eleven, to a mairie that was shut until two. Maylis signed for nothing. She carried the bundle in and laid it in the mayor's tray in the order it had come out of the bag.

On top was a reminder from the prefecture. The commune had not yet returned its count of road signs, which would need changing at the merger, and the count was due on the eleventh. Under it lay a sheet with a yellow band across the head. It gave notice that the lower Adour would pass its first alert level in the night, and asked the mayors of riverside communes to inspect their works, look to their safeguard plans and warn the inhabitants of low quarters.

She read it twice. Then she squared the reminder on top of it, as it had come.

Guy Larrère parked under the third tree at two, between the bollard that stood and the one that leaned, and went in. He was out again in twelve minutes. He lowered his window a few centimetres as he drew level with her.

'The signs,' he said. 'Every panel with the commune's name on it, and the ones at the limits. Pau wants the figure by Friday. Hontaube pays for the new ones, so count them all.'

'There was a notice under it. The river's on the line.'

'It's yellow. It was yellow in 2018 and in 2021, and I had the same sheet.' He looked at the wipers. 'The gate stays shut. You'll keep your telephone by you at night, as your father did, and you'll do nothing until it rings.'

'And the low quarters? It says to warn them.'

'They live behind a dyke. They know there's a river.' He put the car in gear and did not drive off. 'They tell me Castagnet has stopped going out to his hide. A man who's hunted that meadow since he could walk, at home every evening. And the commune's employee at his door on a Sunday with flowers.'

'I took his wife a pot.'

'Leave his wife alone.' It came out harder than his voice for the signs. He heard it, and brought it down. 'The commune's employee doesn't go calling. It gets talked about. I can't send Hontaube a staff file with that in it as well. You're not to worry about the river.'

The window went up.

* * *

She began in the square, with a sheet from the shed on a board and a pencil in her left hand.

There was the panel at the mairie door, and the one on the hall. There was the blue plate on the school and the arrow to the cemetery. Each had the commune's name on it, and each would have another by the spring. She made a stroke for each and bundled them in fives. The rain ran off the board.

She had been in the other one's bed that morning. She had it still, more clearly than the kitchen. It was the same lampshade. The pen had gone across the page without stopping, as it had across thirty-eight pages of a lease. Nobody over there told that hand to keep by a telephone and do nothing. When that woman walked into a room, it stopped talking.

It was simple enough, once it had been done to her. If she sat still in that chair when the other one got up, she went too. It had fetched her back this time. If it did not, she would wake over there, where the sash was, with a council that waited for her to put the question. And here there would be a woman in a bed with her eyes open. Coralie had found one that morning. She had said her name four times and gone to work.

She put down what she would be leaving, as strokes, without names against them. There was a key that went to Hontaube in twenty-four days. There was a wage, and a waterproof. There was a bed with one pillow, and an envelope gone from behind the fruit bowl. There was a mother who would need an hour. There was a man who called her by her surname now, for a thing she had done to him with knowledge that was not hers.

She knew what was over there as well. She had it on the pad. There was a stone with a line above her name, and a widow who wiped off her kiss, and a tonne with water to the second rung. The other one had got there too late and had mended a roof for it ever since. Maylis had looked at that for two days. She found that she would take the roof with the rest.

She turned the sheet over and wrote two words on the back, large, with the wrong hand.

*Stay sitting.*

She had counted eleven panels and had the dyke road still to do.

A car came round the church and stopped by the yellow post box at the foot of the steps. It was Coralie's. Her shift had ended at two. She got out in her tunic with her bag on her shoulder, and saw Maylis under the fourth tree with the board, and stood where she was.

Maylis went across.

'It's the hospital,' Coralie said. 'Geriatrics, on nights. I filled it in the week they told us June. I've had it stamped since September.'

'I saw it on the dresser.'

'I know you saw it. I put it where you'd see it.' She took the envelope out of her bag. 'I thought one day you'd move the fruit bowl and say, don't. Or say, do. I'd have taken either. I've waited three months behind a bowl of apples for you to want it one way or the other.'

Maylis looked at the envelope, and at the slot. She had two words on the back of a sheet of road signs. She could not say them here either.

'It's twenty minutes to Bayonne,' she said.

'Yes,' Coralie said. 'That's what I thought you'd say.'

She held the envelope in the slot for a moment by its corner. Then she let it go, and it dropped into the yellow box by the church.
