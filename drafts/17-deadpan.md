# 17

On Tuesday she came back late, and for some minutes she had the wrong kitchen.

She had gone in at twenty-five past four, by the red figures. She counted five and five, and read the card. Then she turned her head to the left, since on Monday there had been no wall on that side.

The wall was back, with its rivets. The door was back in it, and it stood open. It had come in that way. It sat on the steel floor against the open door, with a back to her and a back to the doorway, and it did not move while she looked at it. She found that she could look past it. Beyond its leaves the floor went on into waxed boards and strip lights, and the tables ran out of sight. The one chair stood empty at the end of the third row, with its card. A voice gave a number whole and then in its parts.

Coralie's alarm began across the landing at six. Maylis took the right-hand lamp and rubbed her palms together in her lap, and held.

The warmth went out of the chair. It went from the arms and then from the back, as when somebody gets up from a bench, and the chair went after it with Maylis in it.

She was getting out of a bed, from the middle. No rain was on the tiles. She sat up in one movement, and nothing took her under the arm, and she noticed the lack of it as she would have noticed a tooth gone. Her right hand shut on a pen. A notebook lay open on the blanket with the night in it, numbered, like minutes. The clock radio had been turned on its base to face the pillow.

She had the other one's morning on her, and it fitted. She knew the combination of a safe, and that a sash lay in it in a box. She knew that a speech was typed and in a drawer, and that a stone stood where the mayor's car ought to be, with her name on the second line. She went down the stairs with all of it, and they were her stairs.

Coralie was in the kitchen in a coat, with a bowl in front of her. A bunch of keys lay beside the bowl. She looked up as a woman looks up who has a home to run and has been kept waiting.

Then the cold had Maylis by the breastbone, and she was on her back with rain overhead.

The clock radio said 06:38. She lay and looked at the lampshade until it was hers. When she went down there was a bowl on the table, and Coralie behind it in her tunic, with her name on the pocket and under it the words care assistant.

'When does the director get in?' Maylis said.

She had meant Coralie. She was looking at the oilcloth beside the bowl, where the keys should have been.

Coralie put her spoon down. 'At nine. As she has since March. She's the one who's shutting us in June.' She waited. 'What do you want with the director?'

'Nothing. I thought.' Maylis sat down. 'I'd the wrong kitchen for a minute.'

'You'd the wrong something for thirty-eight minutes. I came across when mine went and stood in the door. Your eyes were open and your hands were going. I counted your breathing, since nobody was going to stop me.' Coralie pushed her bowl away. 'On Sunday I told you to ask me back across the landing. This morning you ask me about my employer.'

'It won't happen when I've had coffee.'

'It'll happen tomorrow at six,' Coralie said. She took her bag off the back of the chair. The shopping pad lay face up between them, with three boxes empty on it.

* * *

It had rained on fifteen days out of fifteen. At eight she drove down to the gate and read the gauge, as her father had read it in any wet December, with his thumbnail against the mark.

The river stood forty centimetres under his red line, and it was the bottom of the ebb.

He had painted the line at the height of the doorsteps in the Port. Below it the river was the river. Over it, the river was above forty houses and a care home, and the dyke with its patch of clay was what kept it there. She looked at the patch on her way back. It was darker than it had been on Thursday.

The mairie opened at two. The post had come at eleven, and since the secretary would not be in before the door was unlocked, Maylis took it from the box and carried it to the mayor's tray, as she did on Tuesdays and Fridays.

There were two envelopes from the prefecture at Pau. The top one was a reminder. The commune had not yet returned its count of road signs, all of which would need changing in January, and the count was now required by Friday. The one under it was thinner. She could read the heading through the window of the envelope, and it said flood watch, Adour, yellow.

She laid them in the tray in the order they had come.

Guy Larrère drove into his space at five past two. He lowered his window the usual amount and she told him through it that the river was forty under at the ebb.

'I've had the yellow,' he said. He had been in the mairie four minutes and had read the top two. 'Pau sends a yellow each time it rains on Pau. Over the line it stays shut. You had that from me on the dyke.'

'It sticks at nineteen. If it's down and wants raising, or up and wants lowering.'

'It will be down, and it will stay down. There's an estimate being sought.' He held a sheet out through the gap. It was a form with four columns. 'The signs. Name signs at the entries, the hamlets, the direction boards. They want the number by Friday. Go round with that. Your father would have known it without going round.'

She took the form.

Then he put the window down as far as it went. She had not seen that done in eighteen years.

'They tell me you were at the Castagnet house on Sunday,' he said. 'With flowers. Twenty minutes in the kitchen.'

'I took her a pot.'

'What for?'

'I don't know.'

'A man's wife is no business of the commune's.' His face was not the face he had for the winch. 'He's sat at home every night since, in the kitchen, like a dog in a doorway. Nobody can get near the house. You'll keep away from it. That's not advice.'

The window went up, all the way.

By then the café had settled it. Maylis had it from the owner's wife at half past two, across the crate of bread, in a low voice and at some length. The commune's girl was courting the Castagnet woman. She had gone to the house with chrysanthemums, for want of knowing better, while the husband was at the counter. She had frightened him off his own complaint the week before to clear the way. The other girl at Arlette's had moved into the box room over it. In sixteen years the café had found nothing to say about the two of them, and it was making up the time.

* * *

Her mother had her ponies off the low meadows in any December when the river reached the line. Her father had painted it for the Port. Arlette had used it for forty years for the grass.

At three they went down with the halters. Arlette drove the commune's van, which she was not insured for, and Maylis walked in front of it up the dyke road with the old mare on a rope in her left hand. The other ten came behind the mare without being asked. They went through the Port at a walk, with the hazard lights going, past the shuttered fronts and the windows of the home.

Sarthou was at his gate. He had a spirit level laid on his doorstep and was looking at the bubble.

'The file's shut,' he said, as the mare went by. 'My cousin took it back on Friday. I've booked the hall again. The eighteenth is on.'

'That's the fifth time.'

'It's the last. I've said so to the caterer.' He looked up at the dyke over his roof, and at the clay. 'That patch is weeping. I've told the mairie by telephone. The mairie has taken note.' He put the level in his pocket. 'He's going down again tonight, since you'll want to know. Five nights he's sat in his kitchen. But the water's coming into the meadows on the tide now, under the flap, and he'll have his hand of it at the tonne and more. The duck will be down. He'll not miss that for you or for anybody.'

'Tell him it's over the line by this evening.'

'He can read a gauge. He's sent the mairie twenty-six letters on what it says.'

She put the ponies in the paddock behind her mother's and counted them through the gate. At ten past four she was back on the platform. The tide was at the top. The river stood two centimetres over her father's line, brown, and going by faster than a person walks.

Arlette had come down again in the van to see it. She sat with the window open.

'He painted that in 1987,' she said. 'I held the pot.' She looked at the water for a time. 'Marie-Jo tells me the café has you courting Maïté Castagnet.'

'I took her a pot of flowers.'

'Chrysanthemums. I heard what kind.' Arlette did not turn her head. 'I can see your landing from my sink. There's been a light in the box room window since Friday, and none in the other till four in the morning. I asked you on Friday what you'd been turning. You didn't say, and I've not asked again. I'm saying what I can see from a sink.'

'It isn't Maïté.'

'I know it isn't Maïté. I've known you forty-three years, and you've never looked twice at anything that wasn't the commune's.' She wound the window halfway up. 'Whatever it is, it's got you in the day now. The whole home watched you at that crossroads. One of you has stopped in that house, Maylis, and I don't think it's Coralie.'

* * *

She wrote it at the kitchen table at five, on the back pages, with the left hand.

*Tuesday. Held. She got up and I went with her. Her bed, her stairs, her kitchen. No rib there. C. runs the home.*

Under it she set down what she had here, as she had once set down the stone and the sash and the water. The key was going to Hontaube on the first of January. Hontaube had a van. The winch belonged to an association that was seeking an estimate. The job was a question of staff, which was not settled. Her partner slept in the box room. The mayor wanted his road signs counted by Friday, with the river over the line.

There she had the sash. For eleven years every decision in Peyrebarthe had gone across that one's desk, and she had signed each of them without slowing down. Maylis knew something of what they had cost. She had seen ponies go up a ramp and a boy looking into a lens, and black water under a hatch. That one had a stone to walk past each morning with a man's name over her own. Maylis had read all of it. She found that she wanted it with the stone in.

She knew how it was done. It took a lamp, and not letting go. If she held when the other one got up, she went down those stairs, and this morning she had been fetched back from the foot of them. She would not be fetched the next time. It had a back to her and never looked.

*I mean to stay in the chair. That is where the sash is.*

She read the line twice, and it did not change.

Coralie's car came into the yard at twenty past five and did not stop. It went on through to the lane with its lights on. Maylis had the switch to throw at half past, and she walked up to the square after it.

Nine trees came on. Coralie's car was standing by the church with the door open and the engine running, and Coralie was on the pavement in her tunic, in the rain, in front of the yellow box. She had an envelope in her hand. It was white, the long kind, with a typed label, and one corner had gone soft from being carried.

'I filled it in on the fourteenth of March,' she said, when Maylis was near enough. 'The week they told us June. It's the hospital at Bayonne, the geriatric floor, nights. I've walked it to this box four times and walked it home. It's been under the tea towels. You've seen the edge of it.'

'I didn't ask.'

'No. I've wondered whether that was manners.' Coralie held the envelope against the slot without letting go of it. 'They're saying at the home that you want Maïté Castagnet. I told them you don't. I couldn't tell them what you do want, and I've been asking you that since 2010. This morning you looked at my place at the table for something that wasn't there. So I'm asking it the once more, with this in my hand. What do you want, Maylis?'

Maylis had the answer in pencil on the kitchen table. It could not be said in a square.

'It's a good hospital,' she said.

Coralie looked at her until the church clock had finished striking the half. Then she opened her fingers, and the envelope dropped into the yellow box by the church.
