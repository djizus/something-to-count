# 18

On Wednesday three councillors were waiting for the mayor in her mother's kitchen, where the village took what it would not say across her desk.

Lesca crossed the yard at half past six, over frozen gravel. She had gone in awake at four and held. Each morning since Monday she came back with more of the other one's day, and it took her longer to be sure of the lampshade.

The first deputy had the chair by the range, with his hat on his knee. The two beside him had been on her list since 2015 and had voted with her on the dyke, the lake and the cameras. Each had a bowl of coffee. None of them got up.

'Sit down, Maylis,' Arlette said. 'Have you eaten?'

'No.'

Her mother put the loaf in front of her, and the knife.

'We've not come about the lake,' the first deputy said. He said it to Arlette, and then turned his hat round and said the rest to the oilcloth. 'It's Saturday. The three of us have talked. We think the prefect should speak alone.'

'The mayor speaks after the prefect. She has for eleven years.'

'For eleven years the mayor hadn't laughed at the minute. Madame Castagnet has asked not to be kissed. It's in the order of ceremony, in print, and the prefecture has a copy. Six thousand people will stand in the Port, and half of them will have had it from the café by then.' He looked up as far as her hands. 'And you stood there on Monday, Madame le maire. Forty seconds. I've been your deputy eight years and I didn't know whether to fetch a doctor or a priest. We had the priest.'

'What are you asking?'

'That you stand at the stone, in the sash, and let him do the talking. Nobody would think the less of it. They'd think you'd been asked by the family.'

'It's for you we're asking,' said one of the others. 'Not against you.'

Arlette had her back to them at the sink. She spoke without turning.

'Coralie telephoned me on Monday at twenty past six, with her lying there and her eyes open. I said on her side. I've said it since she was nine.' She dried a bowl. 'I've not set foot in that mairie in seven years, and I'll not tell it who's to speak. But I can count a daughter who's had four hours a night for a fortnight. So can they. That's all they've come to say, and they've said it in my kitchen because they were ashamed to say it in hers.'

The first deputy looked at his hat.

Lesca had the speech in her bag. It was four pages, typed on Friday, and it had been the same speech since 2016 with the year changed. She had moved, on Monday, that the mayor remain seated. If the motion held, there would be no mayor at the stone on Saturday to speak or to keep quiet. She could not tell three councillors that across a loaf.

'You'll have my answer tonight,' she said.

She left the bread where it was. Her mother came with her as far as the step.

'My mother said the words to it every night of her life,' Arlette said, low. 'I gave them to you a fortnight ago. You've not said them once. I'd know. I only gave you the first line, and you've not come back for the rest.'

'No.'

'Then you want it there. I'm not asking what for.' She pulled the door to behind her against the cold. 'On your side, Maylis.'

She said that loud enough for the three at the table.

* * *

The telephone on her desk rang at eleven. It was a number at Hontaube.

'Madame le maire,' Père Honoré said. 'I am at Peyrebarthe tomorrow from five. I told you so on the dyke and at the church, and I tell you by telephone so that you need not be seen deciding.'

'You said on Monday you weren't asking.'

'I am not. I am saying that the door is open, and that Madame Castagnet comes at five, as she does before each twelfth. I will keep the two of you apart, or I will not, as you say.'

'What would I tell you that I didn't tell you in 2015?'

'The same. You would tell it again, and hear it. You have not heard it in eleven years. You have only carried it.' He waited. 'I saw you held on Monday. I have no French word for what I saw, and I do not need one. I know what a person looks like who cannot put a thing down.'

'I may not be at the stone on Saturday, Father.'

'Where would you be?'

She did not answer that. She could hear a kettle behind him, and a door.

'Then I say it a fourth time,' he said. 'From five.'

She put the receiver down and gave the five words to the back of the stone, through the window, without moving her lips. They had had four days. She said them at each hour until six, and between hours she found her right hand shut on the desk.

* * *

Coralie's car was in the yard at seven. She sat down at the kitchen table with the staff rota and a pencil. Lesca sat opposite with the four pages of the speech. The soup was on. The wall clock over the range said ten past.

She read as far as the second page.

She woke with her forehead on her arm and an oilcloth under it. A woman was sitting across the table with a pencil, filling in squares. Lesca looked along the table for the shopping pad, with its boxes. She listened for the rain on the tiles and did not hear it, and thought that it must have stopped for the evening.

She had been wanting to say it since the afternoon. She had stood under the fourth tree with a board of road signs and watched it go into the slot.

'It went, then,' she said. 'The hospital. I saw you post it.'

The pencil stopped.

'Post what?'

'The envelope. From behind the fruit bowl. Bayonne, geriatrics, on nights.'

'I run a home,' Coralie said. 'I've forty-two beds and a rota. I've not put in for a hospital since 2011.'

Then it was on her.

It had not come down off anything. She was sitting up at a table, and it sat on her breastbone as it did on a woman lying flat, with a back to her and another to Coralie. Her arms lay on the typed pages and would not lift. She could see the lamp over the table past the edge of the leaf, and the wall clock, which said nineteen minutes to eight. The kitchen had smelled of leeks. It smelled now of the barn.

She had said a thing that was not hers to say, in her own kitchen, and it had been across the table before the pencil was down.

Coralie did not get up. She had shaken her on Sunday and fetched her mother on Monday. She laid the pencil on the rota and looked at the clock, and then at Lesca, as she looked at a resident through a seizure, with her hands flat and her mouth shut.

'You can hear me,' she said. 'I'm not going to touch you. I'm going to time it.'

The minute hand moved. Lesca did not push. She kept her breath where it was and looked at the clock past the leaf, and when she breathed in the leaves lifted. In twelve years it had sat on her in the dark, at an hour when Coralie was across a landing. It sat on her now under the kitchen lamp with Coralie a metre away, looking straight at where it was.

'Two minutes,' Coralie said.

A lorry went through the square, a long way off. The soup caught on the bottom of the pan and Coralie did not go to it.

'Three.'

At a quarter to eight the little finger of her right hand bent on the paper. It got down. She heard it on the tiles, the dry noise, going towards the stairs.

She counted her fingers on the oilcloth, both hands, and made ten.

'Four minutes,' Coralie said. 'You weren't breathing six to the minute. You were breathing as you do awake, and you couldn't lift a hand.' She took the pan off the ring. 'So there's a kitchen where I post a letter to Bayonne.'

'Yes.'

'And you saw me do it.'

'Yesterday. At the yellow box by the church. In your tunic.'

'In a tunic.' Coralie stood with the pan in her hand. 'I've not worn one since they gave me the office. Am I across the landing there as well?'

'Since Friday.'

'Friday.' She set the pan down. 'It took me eleven years less there. I'd like to meet that woman.' She did not laugh. 'I'm going up. I'll leave the door.'

* * *

Lesca took the notebook from her bag. She had not opened it at a table before.

She headed the page as an inventory and ruled it into two columns, as the commune's accounts were ruled. Over the first she wrote Received.

It took the whole of the page. She had twelve years of Jeannot Castagnet alive. She had him at the counter of the café on a Friday in waders, and laughing in the rain, and on his feet in the hall with a card in each hand. She had twenty-nine letters he had not lived to write here, and could have given the subject of each. She had a van in the mud at dawn and a chain, and fourteen euros sixty stopped for the diesel. She had a February afternoon on a mower with a telephone going, and two hundred euros pushed back across a dashboard. She had a rib, and a hand that Coralie had bound, and a pot of chrysanthemums in a sink. She had her mother at a fence that morning, counting ponies as far as eleven.

None of it had the wrong furniture any longer. She had it as she had her own Monday.

Over the second column she wrote Removed.

She went down her own December for it, line by line, as she would have gone down a till that would not balance. The telephone was there. The track was there, and what stood at the end of it. The winch was there, and the palm opening on the handle. She came to the tonne, in the first light, with a torch, and the hatch open at her feet. The water was standing in the cellar to the second rung from the top, black, and it did not move.

It was all there. It was no fainter for twenty-nine letters. Under the second heading she wrote one word, as in the accounts of a year with no disposals.

*Nil.*

She sat and looked at the two columns.

She had moved that the mayor remain seated. She had wanted a village where the twelfth of December was a Saturday, and she had put it to herself that a woman who woke there would have the parking space in place of the stone. But whoever got up from that chair woke with both. She had been getting both for a fortnight, a night at a time, and nothing she had been given had taken one thing out.

If she sat still on Friday and went, she would stand beside him at that counter, and he would owe her for diesel, and she would know what the water looked like in his cellar. He would be alive, and she would have drowned him all the same. It would be a Saturday to everybody in that café but one.

There was nothing to be had there that she had not got already. She could go there to knock. She could not go there to forget.

She did not withdraw the motion. It had lost one of its grounds, and she left it on the table with the other.

At the foot of the page, under both columns, she wrote a date. It was the day the rain had begun over there, and the morning after the first night she had woken with an afternoon that was not hers. She gave it no heading.

*24 November.*

She shut the notebook. The telephone was on the wall by the door. She said the five words once to the receiver before she dialled, since the clock had struck nine and they were owed.

She rang the first deputy, and then the other two, in the order they had sat at her mother's table. She told each of them the same thing, and it was the first sentence she had said in three days with a Saturday in it.

'I'll give the speech,' she said. 'As typed.'
