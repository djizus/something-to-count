# 18

On Wednesday three members of the council of Peyrebarthe brought a request to the mayor, and they brought it to her mother's kitchen.

They were there at half past six, each with a bowl of coffee. The first deputy had the chair by the range. The member for the Port sat on the bench with his cap on his knee. The third had seconded the lease in 2019 and had been given the stool. None of them got up when Lesca came in from the yard, over the frozen gravel, in her coat.

'Sit down, Maylis,' Arlette said. 'They've been here since six. They'd not say it till you came, and they'd not go up to the mairie with it.'

'The mairie's open to its own council.'

'Yours is. Mine's warmer.' Arlette put the loaf in front of her.

The first deputy turned his bowl round by the rim. He said that it was about Saturday. He said that he spoke for more than three. Six thousand people would be in the square, and the television from Bordeaux, and the prefect. On Monday the mayor had laughed in the minute and then stood at the head of the table for the rest of it with her mouth open, and Madame Castagnet had walked out, and it was in the minutes by a vote of the committee. The café had it. Hontaube had it by Monday night.

'If it took you at the stone,' the member for the Port said. He did not say what it was. 'With the wreath going down. With her standing there.'

'We thought the prefect might speak alone,' the first deputy said. 'You'd be on the platform. You'd have the sash on. It would be put about that you had a throat.'

'I've given that speech eleven times.'

'You have. Nobody's saying anything against the eleven.'

Lesca had the notebook in her coat pocket. In it, in her own hand, she had put it to herself on Monday that the mayor remain seated, and in a bed on Saturday morning there was to be a woman who did not wake. She looked at the three of them. They were asking her not to speak on a day she did not mean to see.

'You'll have my answer at half past eight tonight,' she said. 'Here, since you like the coffee.'

She had never in eleven years taken a day to answer a councillor. The first deputy looked at the other two, and they put their bowls in the sink and went out across the yard by the gate.

Arlette shut the door after them.

'Coralie rang me on Monday. Twenty-two minutes, she said, with your eyes open.' She stood with her back against the door. 'My mother said the words to it every night for seventy years. You don't say them.'

'No.'

'You lie on your back for it. You get up before light and lie down again. You do everything she said not to, and you write it down before you've eaten.' She had worked it out by herself at the sink. 'You're not keeping it off, Maylis. You're going to meet it.'

'I count my fingers.'

'That one was for coming back,' Arlette said. 'See that you do.'

* * *

The telephone on the mayor's desk rang at ten. It was Père Honoré, from the sacristy at Hontaube, between a baptism and the bus to the school.

'I did not like to say it across a committee,' he said. 'So I say it into this. Thursday, from five, at Peyrebarthe.'

'On Thursday at five I shall be in council.'

'Friday, then.'

'On Friday I've the rehearsal, with the prefect's office.'

'And on Saturday you have six thousand people. You keep a full diary, Madame le maire.' She heard a door shut at his end. 'I walked Madame Castagnet home on Monday. She asked me what there was to laugh at in him. I said I did not know, and that I did not think it was him you had laughed at.'

'It wasn't.'

'Then there is a thing you could tell her, and she would be glad of it.'

'It's a tank of diesel, Father. From a January she never had. I can't give her that across a wreath.'

He was quiet for some seconds. He had heard her in January 2015, for eleven minutes, with a bucket dripping in the aisle, and he had the whole of what she had said then. He did not have this.

'You told me once what you had to tell,' he said. 'It did not get any smaller for being told to me. I have watched you carry it past my porch since. If there is now something more, I will hear that as well. By telephone I cannot absolve you. I can listen, and the line is good.'

She looked through the window at the back of the stone.

'There's more than there was,' she said. 'I've not finished adding it up.'

'Then add it up. I am here until the bus.'

'It'll take me longer than the bus.'

'It has taken twelve years,' Père Honoré said. 'I ask early, so that you have the time.'

* * *

She had been up at four on eleven mornings. That morning she had gone in, said the five words and been given the gauge, with the river over her father's line and going by fast. She had been given a white envelope dropping into the yellow box by the church. It had sat on the boards between her chair and the hall for the whole of it, and she had let go of the lamp at six, before the other one moved.

At seven in the evening she was at the kitchen table with the speech. It was typed, on four sheets. She was to read it to herself once a day until the twelfth, as she had each December, and she had got to the foot of the second sheet.

She woke with her cheek on the third.

It was a kitchen with an oilcloth. Coralie sat across the table with a pencil. Lesca looked for the shopping pad, which lay between them at this hour, with its boxes. She looked at the back of the door for a yellow waterproof. She listened for the rain on the window.

'Has it gone, then?' she said. 'The envelope. For Bayonne.'

Coralie's pencil stopped.

There was no pad. There was a staff rota, ruled in squares, and beside it a bunch of keys on a wooden tag. The window was dry. The woman across the table ran the home and had no need of a hospital at Bayonne.

'What envelope?' Coralie said.

Lesca meant to say that she had been dreaming. She told her mouth to open.

It was on her. It had come to her sitting, in her own kitchen, under the light. It sat on her breastbone between her and the edge of the table, with a back to her and a back to Coralie, and its leaves hung over the third sheet of the speech. She tried her hand on the oilcloth. Nothing went down the arm. Over its back she had the wall clock above the dresser, which said three minutes past seven, and under the clock she had Coralie.

Coralie was looking straight at it. She was looking at the place where it was, and what she saw there was a woman who had asked her a question and then stopped.

She did not get up. On Monday she had come round the bed with a telephone. She sat where she was, with the pencil in her fingers, and looked from Lesca's eyes to the clock and back. She did it as she must have done it at a great many bedsides. Once she said Lesca's first name, quietly, and Lesca heard it and could not turn her head for it.

The clock said four minutes past. It said five. Coralie laid the pencil down along the edge of the rota. It said six.

At seven minutes past, Lesca's little finger moved on the oilcloth. It got down. She heard it reach the tiles.

She counted her fingers before she spoke, both hands, on the speech. She made ten.

'Four minutes,' Coralie said. 'I watched the clock, since it was no use watching you.' Her voice was not steady, and she waited until it was. 'That's what it is. That's what I've been hearing through a wall since 2015.'

'Yes.'

'You asked me about an envelope for Bayonne.'

'I'd been asleep. I had the wrong kitchen.'

'Whose?'

Lesca did not answer.

'Nothing's gone from this house,' Coralie said. She gathered up the rota and the keys, and stood. 'I'd know. I do the post.'

She took them upstairs. She had not asked which envelope, or said that there was none. Across the landing the door of the box room shut, and the catch went home.

* * *

Lesca fetched the notebook from her coat and opened it on the oilcloth, beside the speech.

Twice now she had woken with the other one's furniture. For some days she had been taking delivery without keeping a stock sheet. She headed a page with the date and the one word held, ruled it down the middle, and began with what had come across.

It took her forty minutes and filled the left-hand column to the foot. There were forty-one registered letters, with a tall L on each card. There was a rib, and a weal dressed with gauze. There was a morning in January with a chain in her fists and a man telling her how her father would have done it, and the diesel stopped out of her pay. There was a hall with strip lights and a ham. There was Marie-Jo across an oilcloth with the code open, saying her first name twice in a sentence. There were eleven ponies counted along a wire, and hay forked short, and her mother at a van window saying that she had stopped asking. There was a shopping pad with boxes that she did not tick. There was Coralie in the same bed for sixteen years, and then the bed with one pillow.

And through all of it there was Jeannot Castagnet. He was at the counter with a glass of white. He was in the Trafic with the wipers going, laughing. He was on his feet with a card in each hand. He stood in the mairie with his cap off, and after that he would not look at her. She had twelve years of him, in his waders, alive.

*31. J. Castagnet, living, 2014 to date. Twelve years. Owes me for diesel.*

Then she took the right-hand column, which was her own. She had expected to find it shorter than it had been.

She wrote the telephone ringing. She wrote the dyke road in the rain, and what stood at the end of the track. She wrote the winch, and the handle going through her fist. She wrote the hatch, and the torch, and the cellar. She wrote a funeral at which she had sat in the third row, and a medal, and a stone with two lines on it, and eleven wreaths.

Each of them was where she had left it. The cellar was there. She had been in it that morning at nine minutes to three, as on every morning, before she was given a gauge or an envelope or anything else.

She had signed the commune's accounts for eleven years, and she knew a transfer from an addition. She had taken that village for a transfer. She had thought that whatever came across from it would displace something on her own side. A man at a counter would take the place of a man in a cellar, and the twelfth of December would be a Saturday. Nothing had been displaced. The left-hand column had been added to the right, and the total carried.

If she stayed in the chair on Friday, she would wake in a bed with one pillow with both columns in her head. She would know the way down the track, and she would know why. She would stand at that counter beside him for the rest of her life, and it would still be the twelfth.

*Nothing of mine has gone. It adds.*

The motion stood. She would still be at that gate when the telephone rang, and she would still walk. But she had liked one of its grounds better than the others, and that ground was struck out.

She looked at the two columns for some time. At the foot of the page, under both and belonging to neither, she wrote a date, and she gave it no heading.

*24 November.*

* * *

At half past eight she crossed the yard. The three of them were in her mother's kitchen with their coats on, and Arlette had not given them coffee a second time.

'Well?' the first deputy said.

'The prefect speaks first, as he has each year. I speak after him.'

'Madame le maire. If it takes you.'

'If I'm prevented, you read it. It's typed. It's in the top drawer of my desk, on the left, and you've your own sash.' She had her hand on the back of her mother's chair. 'I'm not prevented yet. I shall give the speech.'
