# 19

On Thursday the river stood forty-one centimetres over her father's line at the top of the tide, and Sarthou telephoned the shed for sand.

Maylis brought it down in the bucket of the tractor, a cubic metre off the commune's winter heap, and tipped it at his gate. He had the bags. They were jute, from a bale he had kept in his lean-to since 2009, and he held each one open while she shovelled with the shaft under her left arm. Her right hand was out of its bandage. The weal had gone white along the middle and would not close on a shovel.

The dyke stood over his roof. The clay patch on its face was black and shining, and a thread of water ran from the foot of it across the road to his step.

'That's through, not over,' he said. 'Over I could watch. Through, I find out when I put my feet out of bed.'

They laid the bags across the doorstep three deep and then along the foot of the front wall. He set each one himself, with the neck folded under, and trod it. He counted aloud as he laid them, as he had counted everything in that house for thirty-one years.

'Thirty-six,' he said, at the last. He took out his notebook and wrote it down, with the date and the hour. Then he turned the page and wrote again, in capitals, and tore it out and gave it to her. It was addressed to the commune of Peyrebarthe. It charged thirty-six bags, filled and laid, at one euro fifty, to the owner of a dyke that leaked onto a private doorstep.

'For the mayor's tray,' he said. 'He'll take note of it.'

She folded it into her chest pocket with the slips.

'You asked me at the loto why my cousin won't file against Larrère by name. I didn't tell you. I was a gendarme with a file open.' He kicked the last bag square. 'It's shut now, so I'll say it once. Larrère has forty hectares of maize on the high ground. Every summer he pumps out of the creek for it, at night, with no meter on the pipe. Jeannot's hunted that bank since he could walk. He knows where the pipe goes in. And Larrère knows what's under Jeannot's boards, as you found out you did.'

'So neither says.'

'Each has the other by the collar. Jeannot can write the mayor forty-one letters about a hand of water, and he'll not put Guy Larrère's name to a complaint. And in eighteen years Larrère hasn't sent the men in green to a gate. Persons unknown suits the two of them.' He looked at her. 'You were the only one on that dyke with nothing on anybody. Then you had something. I don't ask where you got it.'

A car came along the dyke road and stopped above them. The window went down a few centimetres.

'Who put the commune's sand on a private step?' Guy Larrère said.

'He asked for it. It's coming through at the patch.'

'It's weeping. It weeps every December.' He looked down at the bags and at the thread of water, and then at the river side, which he could see and she could not. 'The gate stays shut. Your telephone stays by your bed. Have you my number of signs?'

'Forty-three.'

'Your father would have had it at forty-four. There's one behind the hall.' The window went up.

Sarthou watched the car to the end of the dyke.

'He'll be at a kitchen table tonight,' he said, 'and it'll not be his own.' He did not say whose. He picked up the shovel and carried it to the tractor for her.

* * *

Coralie dressed the palm that evening with cream and nothing over it. She had come home with the home's keys in her pocket, which were not hers to carry.

'The director's gone to Bayonne till Monday,' she said. 'She left me the minibus and the list. Forty-two names, and which of them can walk.' She turned the hand to the lamp. 'You were going to say something at the post box on Tuesday. I saw it get as far as your teeth.'

'I'll say it on Saturday.'

'Why Saturday?'

'I'll know by then whether it's true.'

Coralie let the hand go. She went up at nine, to the box room, and left the door open.

* * *

It came at 02:51, and Maylis let it take her. The phone buzzed at four. She walked the yard in the rain, and at twenty-five past she went in by the red figures.

There was no wall on her left. It was sitting on the waxed boards between her chair and the hall, as on Monday, and behind it the tables ran out of sight under the strip lights, and the voice was calling. It did not move for any of what came. She had its back for the whole of the other one's night.

It began with a telephone, and it did not skip.

She was in bed, in the dark, with Coralie asleep against the wall, and the telephone was ringing on the chest of drawers. Rain was coming down on the tiles as it had for three weeks. She had the receiver to her ear before her eyes were open. It was Guy Larrère. He said the river was ninety over the line and rising. He said, 'You'll go down and stand by that gate, and you'll keep it shut. Over the line it stays shut. That's an order of the president.' Behind his voice a clock was ticking, close, of the kind that hangs in a kitchen.

She drove down through the Port in the commune's van with the wipers at full. It was Friday night, the twelfth of December. Every house had a light on. The care home had all of them on.

At the end of the road the dyke was coming through. The patch on its face had opened, and water was going down the clay in a sheet the width of a door, in her headlamps, and across the road, and in at Sarthou's gate. She got out and put her hand flat on the bank beside it. The clay moved under her palm like the side of an animal.

She knew dykes. Her father had taught her on that one. She gave it ten minutes.

She drove up onto the crown and along to the gate, and stopped with her lamps down the track.

The white van stood at the far end of it, where the track ran out among the tonnes. The round reflector burned on its back door, crooked. It was a duck night. He had written to the mairie on the eighth to say that he had less than a hand of water at his tonne. He had one now. He would be down the ladder in the cellar, by the stove, with the hatch shut over him against the rain.

She had it all at once, as a sum is had. Behind the dyke were forty houses and forty-two beds, and the river was standing over their doorsteps by the height of a child. The gate would take it off them. Raised, it gave the river three hundred hectares, and the meadows went under with whatever was on them. The track was four hundred metres of mud. It was twenty-five minutes on foot, out and back, to knock on a hatch. She had ten.

She took her telephone out of her waterproof and found his number, and rang it.

The clock on the dashboard said 02:51.

It rang once. She heard the one ring, in her ear, in the van, with the rain on the roof. She did not wait for the second. She put the telephone down on the dashboard and got out.

In the chair Maylis took her eyes off the lamp, and the night went on without it. She looked at the back on the boards in front of her. It had come down off her wardrobe at that minute on seventeen nights, and she had thought it was her hour. It was not hers. It had sat on the other one at nine minutes to three for twelve years, and that was the minute it was kept for.

Then she had the winch.

The handle was wet. She stood square to it and turned, and counted, as she had counted every time. The gate came up out of the drain a centimetre at a turn. At thirty the river found it. The handle went heavy and then ran back in her fist, the whole weight of the Adour on the pawl, and took the skin off her palm in one line from the heel to the forefinger. She did not let go. She turned it with that hand to sixty-two.

The river went under the platform and out into the dark with a noise she had not heard before or since. She went back to the van and sat in it with her hand in her lap. The reflector burned in the lamps at the end of the track for four minutes more. Then the water came up the door to it and put it out.

On the gauge the river dropped a hand in a quarter of an hour. At the end of the Port the sheet on the dyke thinned to a thread. Sarthou's step had water on it and no more. Not a house behind that bank took any.

The rest came fast and in its order. There was first light, and a boat, and a torch held down an open hatch, and black water standing still to the second rung. There was a coffin, with Sarthou at the left front corner. There was a square with four hundred people in it and a prefect who pricked his thumb. There was a council that sat three times on a stone.

And she had the stone from the front, at last, in frost. The line above her name was the same capitals, the same depth. JEAN CASTAGNET. Hers was under it. There was no verb.

Each December after that she bent, in front of the stone, and kissed his widow on the cheek. Each December Maïté held her face still, and took a glove off, and wiped it.

Nobody in that village knew that she had looked down the track first. One man knew, and he had been told in a confessional, with a bucket dripping in the aisle. She had rung once. It was on her telephone still, at that minute, and it had not been answered.

Maylis let go of the lamp.

* * *

The clock radio said two minutes to five. She was in her own bed with one pillow, and the rain was on the tiles.

She counted her fingers, and then she looked at the palm. She took the pad and wrote with the hand that had the weal.

*Friday. Her whole night. She saw his van. She knew he was down there. She rang once and opened.*

*Forty houses and the home. Him. That is what it cost.*

She had written on the back of a form that she meant to stay in the chair, where the sash was. She had wanted it with the stone in. She had not known what was cut on the stone.

He had gone down again on Tuesday. Sarthou had said so. There was a hand of water at the tonne, and the duck were on it, and it was a duck night.

She went down in her T-shirt to the telephone on the kitchen wall. She knew the number. She had rung it once, twelve years ago, in a van, and she had never rung it in her life.

It rang. She let it ring. It rang a second time, and a third, and she stood on the tiles with her feet going cold and counted them. On the landing a door moved, and Coralie came to the top of the stairs.

At the ninth ring he answered.

'Who's dead?' said Jeannot Castagnet.

'Nobody. It's Maylis Lesca.'

There was a silence, with water in it, and the creak of a ladder.

'It's five in the morning,' he said. 'I've three hundred teal on a hand of water, and you've put up every one.' Then he swore at her. He did it at length, in French and after that in Gascon, and he called her by her surname and her father by his. She stood in the kitchen with the receiver to her ear, and listened to all of it.
