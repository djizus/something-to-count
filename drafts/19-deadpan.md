# 19

On Thursday the commune of Peyrebarthe sandbagged one doorstep, and received an invoice for it.

The river had been over her father's line at each tide since Tuesday, and that morning it stood forty centimetres over at the top. Sarthou's step was twelve under the paint. At eight Maylis brought the tractor down through the Port with a trailer of the commune's road sand and a bale of hessian bags, and found him at his gate with two shovels.

They filled and laid until eleven, in the rain. She held each bag open with her left hand and her knee, and he shovelled. He would not let her lift. He laid them himself, three courses across the step and two along the foot of the hedge, with the joints broken like brickwork, and he counted each one aloud as it went down.

'Forty-two,' he said.

He went indoors and came back with a duplicate book. He wrote in it standing, under the porch, and tore off the top copy. It was addressed to the commune. It charged for the filling and laying of forty-two sandbags in defence of the commune's dyke at its lowest point, four hours of one man at a labourer's rate, fifty-one euros twenty.

'It's your doorstep,' Maylis said.

'It's the commune's bank. My step is where it shows first. If it comes over, it comes here, and I'd take it kindly to be knocked up before I'm floated out.' He held the invoice out. 'Put it in his tray. It'll be the first thing in thirty years I've had off that mairie.'

She buttoned it into her chest pocket, with the two slips and the card.

He looked up at the dyke. Water stood in the grass at the foot of it, and the clay patch had a shine.

'You asked me at the loto why my cousin won't put Larrère's name in a complaint,' he said. 'I didn't say. I've eight days to a pension, so I'll say it now, and then I'll not have said it.' He leaned on the shovel. 'My cousin nets at your gate. He has since before you had the key. Larrère knows it. And Larrère has a pump on the creek behind his sheds that waters sixty hectares of maize, and it has never had a meter on it. My cousin has sat over that creek at night for thirty years and heard it run. So neither says the other's name to a gendarme. They write to each other through the mairie. He's sent forty-one letters about the level of the water, and not one says why it's low.'

'And you take the letters as complaints.'

'I take what's filed. Nobody's filed a pump.' He did not say what he had been told about nets, or by whom, or what he had done with it. 'That's the two of them. The gate's between, and you turn it.'

A car had stopped on the road down from the terrace, forty metres off, with its engine running. It was Guy Larrère's. He did not get out. In eighteen years he had not come down to look at that bank, and he looked at it now through a window lowered the usual amount.

'Whose sand is that?' he called.

'The commune's.'

'On whose order?'

'Nobody's,' Maylis said.

He considered the forty-two bags, and the gendarme, and her. 'Put it on a sheet. And keep your telephone by you tonight and tomorrow. Shut means shut.' The window went up, and he reversed to the crossroads to turn.

'He'll ring you,' Sarthou said. 'He'll ring from somewhere warm.'

* * *

Coralie changed the dressing at seven, at the kitchen table. She unwound the bandage and looked at what was under it for some time.

'That's closed,' she said. 'In six days. It's white along the middle. I've seen burns take a month to do that.' She did not put the gauze back. 'I'm on tomorrow night. They've asked for all of us. We're to have the minibus fuelled and the chairs by the door.'

'It won't come to that.'

'You don't know what it'll come to. Nor do I.' She folded the bandage. 'Are you getting up at four?'

'Yes.'

'Then I'll hear you,' Coralie said, and went up to the box room.

* * *

It came at 02:51, and she let it have her. At four she walked the yard. At twenty-five past she went in by the red figures and counted five and five.

The wall on her left had gone again. It was sitting out on the waxed boards between her chair and the hall, with a back to each. Beyond it the tables ran on under the lights and the voice gave its numbers. She did not look at them for long. It stayed where it was for the whole of what came, and what came took the rest of the night.

It was the other one's, and it came entire. The medal had been gone over for a day. This had been gone over every night for twelve years, and it was worn as smooth as a step.

It began with a telephone, in a bed, in the dark. Coralie was asleep against the wall. It had rained for nineteen days. The voice was Guy Larrère's, and he said that the river was ninety over the line and rising, and that the gate stayed shut. She was to go down and stand by it, and do nothing until he rang again.

She went down. She took the commune's van through the Port, and each house had its shutters closed and people asleep behind them. The home had a light on the stairs. On the platform she put a torch on the gauge. The river was ninety-two over her father's line. It was above the doorsteps of forty houses and the beds of the home, and the bank was holding it there. The bank was running in three places. She had patched it each spring for six years, and she put what was left in it at ten minutes.

Behind her were the low meadows, three hundred hectares, dark, with no water on them but the rain. Raised, the gate would give the river all of that and take it off the bank. She had known it since she was twelve. Her father had drawn it for her on the back of a feed sack.

Before she touched the winch she turned the van on the dyke, to put its headlamps down the track. She did it to see that the meadows were empty.

They lit the rain. At the far end they lit the back of a white van, and low on its left-hand door a round reflector, screwed on crooked, burning red.

There was one of those in the commune. He had written on the sixth that he had less than a hand of water at his tonne. He had his water now, and the duck would be down to it, and he would be in the cellar under the hatch between flights, asleep in his waders. It was twenty-five minutes out along that track in that rain, and twenty-five back. The bank had ten.

She took out her telephone. She had his number, for the gate. The screen said 02:51.

It rang once.

She did not let it ring twice. She put the telephone in her pocket and both hands on the handle, and turned.

Maylis sat in the chair and had it in her own arms. The handle went round faster than it had ever been let go. At some turn the pawl slipped, and the handle ran back through her right fist and took a line out of the palm, and she caught it and went on. On her knee in the chair the white seam was wet for as long as that lasted. The gate came up. The river went through the throat under her feet with a noise she had not heard it make. Out on the meadows the dark began to shine.

She kept the torch on the gauge. Eighty over. Sixty. The bank stopped running. At four it was twenty over and falling. Not a house in the Port had water in it, and nobody behind any shutter had woken. The reflector had gone out some time before that, from the bottom.

The cellar came after, and by daylight. There was a boat, and the hatch, and the torch going down. The water stood black and still at the second rung. Under it the torch found the sole of a wader.

Then it went quickly, as things do that are filed. Seven councillors stood up in the council room and left it. A prefect looked for the cloth of her lapel, and read a citation that did not say whose order she had gone against. A stone was set in the mayor's parking space, with two lines cut in it, and she read the top one. It said JEAN CASTAGNET. His widow in a black coat held her face still for a kiss and then wiped it off with her bare hand. In a confessional, with a bucket dripping outside it, the other one said to a priest in eleven minutes what Maylis had now been given in one night. She had known whose van it was, and she had rung once.

It did not move. It sat on the boards with its back to her through all of it.

She looked at the leaf and the rivets, and she had the hour. She had seen that screen. It had come down off a wardrobe at nine minutes to three on every night since, to the middle of that bed, and sat on the woman who had made the call. Since the rib it had sat on Maylis too. It had not come at her hour. She had never had one.

She did the sum, since nobody had asked her to. Forty houses and forty-two beds stood against one man at the end of a track, with ten minutes in hand. It came out as it had come out for the other one. She could not make it come out another way.

She did not know that she would have let it ring twice.

She had written on the pad that the other one drove into him, and then that she went for him and came too late. Both were kinder than this. The other one had not been late. She had been at the gate in good time, with a telephone in her hand, and had chosen.

That was what the sash had cost. The other one had paid it in ten minutes and had gone on paying for twelve years, each night, on her back. On Tuesday Maylis had written that she meant to stay in the chair, where the sash was. She had its price now, to the minute.

* * *

She let go of the lamp herself. She was on her bed with it on her breastbone, and the clock radio said 04:57. It got down.

She did not write. Her telephone was under the pillow, and his number was in it, for the gate. It was Friday. The river was over the line, and since Tuesday he had gone down each night to his hand of water. The same voice would ring her tonight and say the same thing.

She pressed the number at five o'clock.

It rang once. She kept it to her ear. It rang twice, and three times, and she sat on the edge of the bed with her forearm against her side and let it ring. Across the landing a floorboard gave. It rang nine times.

'What?'

He was out of doors. She could hear rain on a hood, and water.

'It's Maylis Lesca.'

'It's five in the morning. I've a flight of teal coming in over the creek and you've put the lot up.' Then Jeannot Castagnet swore at her. He did it at length and without repeating himself. He began with the commune and went back through her father to the association of 1962, and he used two words she had last heard from him across a grave.

She listened to all of it.
