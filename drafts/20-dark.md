# 20

On Thursday the council of Peyrebarthe was called to an extraordinary sitting, at six in the evening, on a single item.

Five members had asked for it in writing, as the code allowed, and Lesca had signed the summons herself at nine that morning. The item was in the first deputy's words. That at the commemoration of 12 December the prefect should speak alone.

Fourteen of the fifteen came. The secretary had the register and the white kitchen timer. Outside the window a firm from Bayonne was putting up the stage round the stone, and for the whole of the sitting there was hammering in the square, and the ring of scaffold poles dropped on tarmac.

She had gone in at four that morning with the five words and been given a river forty-one centimetres over her father's line. It was the sentence's fifth day.

The first deputy moved the item and gave his reasons, which were the laugh, the forty seconds and Madame Castagnet's cheek. He said it was for the mayor's sake. He was under a minute and did not look at her.

Guy Larrère asked for the floor. The secretary turned the timer to three and stood it beside the water jug.

'I'll be brief,' he said.

He had no ledger with him. He had the minutes of Monday, which he had asked to have written, and he held them flat under his hand.

'On Saturday six thousand people will stand in the Port, and the prefect, and a widow. The chair will stand in front of them in the sash. I ask the council what it knows of her this fortnight. It knows that she counts her fingers at the striking of the hour. It knows that she slept through a vote, and laughed through a minute, and that she stood at that table for forty seconds and could not be got to sit.' He looked round the fourteen. 'I say nothing about the gate. I've said it for eleven Decembers and I've been timed. I say this. On the night of the twelfth she was given an order and didn't keep it. On Monday she was asked to sit and couldn't. I don't know what she'll be unable to do on Saturday. Nor does she. A commune doesn't put that in front of a microphone, with the television there, and a man from Pau who has never'

The timer rang. He took his hand off the minutes.

There was hammering from the square. Lesca let it go on for the length of four blows.

'I'll speak to the item,' she said. 'I'm entitled, and I'll be shorter than he was. I opened that gate. I've stood at that stone each year since and said so, in front of the man's widow. If the council would sooner have it said by a prefect who was in another department that night, it can vote for that. But it will be voting that the commune has nobody of its own to answer for it.'

She put the item.

Hands went up down the left side of the table and two on the right. The first deputy's was among them, and he held it at the height of his ear, as a man holds a hand up who would like it noticed that he took no pleasure. Larrère's was the highest.

'Seven,' the secretary said.

'Against.'

The hands went up on the other side. Lesca raised her own. On Monday she had stood aside from a vote on a fact about herself. This was a question of who spoke for the commune, and the code gave the chair a voice in it.

'Seven,' the secretary said. She looked up from the register. 'It's tied, Madame le maire.'

'In a tie the chair's vote decides. The item falls.' She uncapped her pen. 'The mayor will speak after the prefect, as in each year. Record it as lost on the casting vote of the chair.'

'She's voted herself onto the platform,' Larrère said, to the table.

'Yes,' Lesca said. 'It's in the code. I didn't make that one.'

She closed the sitting at twenty-six minutes past six and signed the sheet. In the square they had the first deck boards down, and the stone stood up through the middle of them to its shoulders.

* * *

Coralie's car was in the yard before hers. The kitchen was dark. There was a light on the landing.

She went up. Coralie was standing in the doorway of the bedroom with the four typed sheets in her hand. Lesca had left them on the kitchen table on Wednesday, under the notebook, and had not missed them.

'I read your speech,' Coralie said. 'I've heard it eleven times from the second row. I'd not read it.'

'It's the same each year.'

'I know it is. I could say it with you.' She turned to the third sheet. 'Jean Castagnet was at his tonne that night, as on any night of the season. No one came to tell him.' She read it without weight, as she read a dosage. 'Each December you say that into a microphone. It's true, every word. And each December I stand in my good coat and think, she rang him.'

Lesca did not move from the top of the stairs.

'The morning after,' Coralie said. 'You came in at seven with your hand in a tea towel and you lay down in your waterproof. Your telephone was on the chest of drawers. Mine was flat, and I picked yours up to ring the home. It opened on the last call. It was his number. One call, not answered, at nine minutes to three.'

She had said the minute aloud, in that doorway.

Lesca looked at the top of the wardrobe. It was a quarter to seven in the evening, and the landing light lay across the bedroom floor as far as the foot of the bed. The top of the wardrobe was bare. There was dust on it, and the box for a hat. She looked all the same. For twelve years that minute had had something sitting on it, and she had never heard it said by another person.

Nothing came down. It did not come by day for a thing that was hers.

'I put the telephone back,' Coralie said. 'At nine they came up the lane to say he was in his cellar. At ten you were asleep, and I stood here and looked at you. I didn't ask you then. I didn't ask in February, when you began to shout. I took my pillow across the landing. I told myself it was for the sleep.'

'Why didn't you ask?'

'Because you don't ring a man at that hour unless you think you know where he is. And if I'd asked, I'd have had the answer in the house, at meals. I wanted you in the house. I thought I could have the one without the other, if I kept a landing between.' She held the sheets out. 'I couldn't. I've had it for twelve years as well. I've had it through a wall.'

Lesca took the speech. It was warm from her hand.

'I rang once,' she said.

'It says one.'

'I thought there was one man who knew, and that he couldn't speak.'

'The priest.' Coralie nodded. 'I thought of going to him myself, some years. I'm not asking you tonight either. I'm not asking what you saw, or what you'd been told. I watched four minutes of it yesterday across a table, and I'd thought for twelve years it was a thing you had by yourself. I'm telling you that you didn't.'

'Why tonight?'

'Because you came back from the mairie having voted yourself onto that platform. And because for a week you've been saying five words on the stairs, to somebody, and I think you mean to be gone before Saturday.' Her voice held. 'I don't know where a person goes who lies with her eyes open for twenty-two minutes. I know you came back slower each time. If you're going to stand at that stone and read page three, I wanted you to read it knowing I was in the second row, and what I was thinking.'

'And if I'm not at the stone?'

'Then I'll have said it, and you'll have heard it. I've held my tongue long enough to know what it's worth.'

She went across the landing. She did not shut her door.

Lesca stood with the speech. She had counted on a seal for twelve years. She had put one man on the far side of it and thought the account closed. The other had been in the next room the whole time, with a pillow.

She read the two sentences on the third sheet. They were true, every word. She did not take up a pen.

* * *

The firm from Anglet had asked for the square at ten, when it would be empty. She walked up.

The stage was finished. It stood a metre high across the front of the mairie, with steps at each end and a rail, and the stone came up through it at the back like a thing in a crate. Two lamps on poles lit the boards. Nobody had thought to turn the stone's own lamp off, and it lit JEAN CASTAGNET from below, through the planks.

The hired man had a van with its back doors open and a frame laid across two trestles in front of the stage. He was sixty, with a burn scar on the back of each hand, and he did not look up when she came.

'Are you the commune?'

'I'm the mayor.'

'Then it's your signature.' He passed her a sheet on a board, without interest. 'One test piece, one fuse. Sixty metres clear, which I've paced. Wind's nothing.' He wet a finger and held it up all the same. 'I've done Bayonne nineteen years. You'd be surprised what won't light in a damp December.'

'It's been dry here.'

'So I'm told. It's wet enough everywhere else.'

She signed. It came out in the one run, with the stroke that crossed itself.

The frame was the bull. It was willow and wire, the length of a calf, with two horns and a harness for a man's shoulders, and along its spine he had wired a single wheel and a short run of fuse. On Saturday there would be forty wheels on it, and a man underneath, running at the crowd. The other village had a bull each July that did not light. She knew that as she knew its ponies.

The church clock struck ten. She counted her fingers by the stage lamp, both hands, and made ten. The hired man watched her do it and asked nothing. He had seen mayors.

'Stand back of the trestle,' he said.

He took a box of kitchen matches from his shirt pocket. He struck one on the side of the box, shielded it and put it to the end of the fuse.

The bull lit at the first match.
