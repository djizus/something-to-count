# 20

On Thursday the council of Peyrebarthe sat in extraordinary session, at one clear day's notice, on a single item. The item was whether its mayor should open her mouth on Saturday.

The first deputy had asked for the sitting on Wednesday night, in writing, with six signatures under his own. Lesca had convened it herself for half past ten. Fourteen of the fifteen members came. The sitting was public, as the law requires, and the public was two men from the café on the chairs by the door. The secretary had the register open and the white timer beside the water jug.

The first deputy moved it, standing, with his hat on the table. He moved that at the commemoration of 12 December the prefect speak alone. He said it was moved in friendship. He said the top drawer had not been forgotten.

Guy Larrère seconded, and asked for the floor. The secretary turned the timer to three.

'I'll be brief,' Larrère said.

He had not said it in that room since the rule was made, and he said it now to the timer. Then he read from the register. On 28 November, on the dyke, the mayor had counted her fingers during a vote and found five. On 3 December she had slept in the chair for twenty-two minutes while a budget was read. On Monday she had laughed in the minute for Jean Castagnet, and then stood for forty seconds at the head of this table and could not be got to sit, and the widow had gone home. Each of those was minuted. Two were minuted on his own motion, under her rule.

'I don't say she's ill,' he said. 'I say there'll be six thousand people and a camera, and she is to stand a metre from that stone and talk about that night for eight minutes. She's talked about it for eleven years. She has the medal for it. There's a manoeuvre in my association's book with nothing written under it, and she's the only one who was on that platform.' He had turned in his chair. He was not looking at the council. 'I've sat here since 2015 and voted against her and lost. I've never once asked her. I'll ask her now, with a minute in hand. When you stood on that platform, before you laid a hand on the winch, did you'

The timer rang.

He stopped. He looked at it as he had looked at it on the dyke and at the committee, and then at her. She had made that rule in her first month, for him, and she had not thought on what day she would be glad of it.

'Your time, Monsieur Larrère,' the secretary said.

He could have asked for the floor again on the next item. There was no next item. He shut his mouth on whatever came after did you. She thought, watching him, that he did not altogether want it said. He had telephoned an order that night from somewhere, and in twelve years nobody had asked him where.

'The chair has an interest,' the first deputy said. 'On Monday she said so herself, and didn't vote.'

'On Monday the motion was about a noise I made,' Lesca said. 'This one is about whether the mayor of this commune speaks at a ceremony of the commune. That's the office. The office has no interest. It has a duty.' She uncapped her pen. 'I stay in the chair, and I vote. Those in favour.'

Seven hands went up. The first deputy's was among them, and the two from her mother's kitchen, and Larrère's.

'Against.'

Six went up. She raised her own, and the secretary counted it with the rest.

'Seven and seven,' Lesca said. 'In a tie the chair's vote carries. The motion falls. The mayor speaks after the prefect.'

'Then it's minuted that we asked,' the first deputy said.

'It is. And that I answered.'

It was the first vote on herself for which she had not left the room. She had now two motions before her that could not both stand. One was in the register, and said that she would speak on Saturday. The other was in a notebook in the safe, and said that on Saturday she would be a woman in a bed who did not wake. At four that morning she had given the five words to the lamps for the sixth day, with it sitting between her and the hall, and had let go at the alarm. She had voted for the first with her own hand, in front of fourteen people.

The church clock struck eleven as they went out. She counted ten on the register, and nobody asked for it to be minuted.

* * *

She was home at five, to change. The scaffolders had been in the square since noon.

Coralie came up after her. She had the speech in her hand, the four sheets, squared and clipped.

'You left this under your face on Wednesday,' she said. 'I've read it. I'd not read it before. I've only heard it, eleven times, from the second row.'

'It's the same each year. I change the date.'

'I know. I could say it with you.' Coralie did not hold it out yet. She turned to the third sheet. '*On that night no one in this commune knew that a man was out on the meadows.*'

'It's been in since 2016.'

'I've stood and listened to it since 2016. And each year, at that line, I've thought about your telephone.'

Lesca had her coat half off. She left it where it was.

'The morning after,' Coralie said. 'The thirteenth. You came in at eight, in your wet things, and lay down on this bed with your boots on and your hand in a tea towel. Your telephone was on the chest of drawers, going flat. I picked it up to put it on the charger. It was open at the calls.' She said the rest as she would have read it off a chart. 'Outgoing. Castagnet J., mobile. Not answered. Two fifty-one.'

She looked up.

'Nine minutes to three,' she said. 'I've had that for twelve years.'

Lesca looked at the top of the wardrobe.

It was a quarter past five in the afternoon. The minute had been said aloud in that room, by somebody else, for the first time. The top of the wardrobe had a folded blanket on it, and dust, and nothing sat there. It did not come by day for what was her own.

'You shout at it,' Coralie said. 'Across the landing I look at my watch, and it's always that. I told myself for eleven years that it was a coincidence. I'm trained. I know what a coincidence is.'

'You never asked.'

'No.'

'Why not?'

'Because you'd have told me.' Coralie sat down on the edge of the bed, on the side that had been hers. 'I worked nights at the home then. I'd put thirty of them to bed that evening, behind that bank. I knew by breakfast what you'd saved. I didn't want to know what you'd known. So I didn't ask, and I took my pillow across, and I said it was the shouting.'

'It rang once.'

'It doesn't say how long. It says not answered.' She put the speech down on the blanket between them. 'That's all I've got. You rang him, so you thought he might be out. I don't know what you saw. I'm not asking now either. I'm telling you I've had that much of it since the morning after, in the next room, and you've had the rest by yourself. You needn't have.'

Lesca found that she had her right hand shut. She opened it. The seam was where it had been since the tea towel. She sat down on the other side of the four sheets.

'I thought one person knew,' she said. 'And he's not allowed to speak.'

'The priest.'

'Yes.'

'Then there's two. And nobody's told me I can't.' Coralie did not touch her. 'You've said a thing at my sink for six days. Go to the tonne first. Who's that for?'

'Somebody who's not been there yet.'

'I thought it might be. It's twelve years late for anybody here.' She looked at the third sheet. 'Read that line on Saturday or take it out. It's your speech, you voted for it. But I'll be in the second row, and I'll know which you did.'

She went down. She did not shut any door behind her.

Lesca stayed on the bed with the speech. She read the line on the third sheet, and looked up once more at the wardrobe, and it was bare. Then she clipped the sheets together as they had been. She did not take out her pen.

* * *

By seven the stage was up. It was the platform borrowed from Hontaube, twelve metres by six, on steel trestles, and they had built it round three sides of the stone, so that the granite stood at the front edge in a bay of planks. From the steps the two names were at the level of a speaker's knees. The prefect's office would walk it at ten the next morning. The tarmac was dry and the frost had begun on it. Nothing had fallen on that square since the middle of November.

The bull stood by the church. It was a frame of welded rod in the shape of a bull, as long as a man, with handles underneath for whoever carried it and wire horns a metre across. The commune paid nine thousand euros for it each December, with its operator, from a firm at Anglet, and the firm guaranteed ignition in writing.

The operator was a short man in a leather apron. He was going along the frame with a torch in his teeth, touching each fuse where it was tied, and he counted them under his breath as far as sixty-four.

'I test one horn,' he said, when he saw the mayor standing by the stone in her coat. 'The night before, or two nights. Never on the day. On the day they've all come to see it go, and that's when a man fumbles.'

'Does it ever not light?'

'Not mine.' He took the torch out of his mouth. 'People think it's the powder. It's the match. They keep them in a van, in the damp, and they strike six and swear. I keep mine in my shirt.' He showed her the box, which was an ordinary kitchen box. 'They tell me you've a small one here in July that takes six. I'd like to see those matches.'

It was Marie-Jo's, on six hundred euros, and the matches came out of the cupboard in the hall.

By the motion in the safe she would not be on the dyke at nine on Saturday to see sixty-four fuses go. By the vote in the register she would be standing at this stone eight hours before that, with four sheets in her hand. She watched him choose a match.

He wet a finger and held it up for the wind. Then he bent to the left horn, struck one match on the side of the box, and put it to the fuse.

It lit at the first match.
