# 21, comic and held scenes

Three scenes, in the order of `drafts/21-dark.md`: Hontaube by telephone (comic), Larrère at the window (held), the back door of the home as far as Madame Etcheto's count (held). The dark draft takes over at Coralie's "It's the end room", and everything from there is its own.

Every fact of the dark draft is kept. Added: the form that would go on Monday, Hontaube's two men saying they would come, the signs form asked for and not handed over, Coralie on what the minibus holds, Bixente's seven minutes, and Monsieur Daguerre calling "Here" at the count.

* * *

On Friday the envelope from Pau said orange.

Maylis read it through the window of the envelope at eleven, on the mairie step, in the rain. Flood watch, Adour, orange. At eight the river had stood sixty-six centimetres over her father's line. The thread of water from the clay patch to Sarthou's step had widened to a hand, and his bottom course of bags was dark.

She let herself in, laid the envelope in the mayor's tray and picked up the secretary's telephone. She had not used it before. She rang the mairie at Hontaube and asked for its two outdoor men, for one night, at the Port.

'Who's calling?' the woman at Hontaube said.

'The keyholder. At Peyrebarthe.'

'Has your mayor made the request?'

'I'm making it.'

'Then it isn't one. A loan of staff goes from mayor to mayor.' A hand went over the mouthpiece, and came off it. 'There's a form. I can put one in the post to you. It wants his signature and ours.'

'When would it go?'

'Monday.'

'It's for tonight.'

'Then it wanted posting last Monday.'

The hand went over again. Behind it Maylis heard a man's voice, and a second, and a chair pushed back.

'They say they'd come,' the woman said. 'I've told them they can't. The agreement between the communes runs from the first of January. Before that date our men aren't covered on your works. If one of them does his back on your dyke tonight, nobody pays him for it.'

'From the first of January they hold the key to that gate.'

'From the first of January, yes. You'll not need a form then. You'll be ours.'

'I was told they have a van.'

'They have. It's in the shed at four on a Friday, and so are they.'

'It's eleven kilometres,' Maylis said.

'We've a river as well,' the woman said, and rang off.

* * *

Guy Larrère was in the mairie nine minutes. He came out with the orange sheet in his coat pocket and lowered his window the usual amount.

'The signs,' he said. 'Pau wanted the form by today. It wasn't in my tray.'

The form was in her chest pocket, with a line of her own on the back of it.

'It got wet. The number's forty-three.'

'Forty-four. I told you, behind the hall.' He looked at the wipers. 'Then Pau has it late. This commune has sent Pau nothing late in eighteen years, and it does it in its last month.'

'Pau's sent an orange.'

'I've read it. You'll take the van down at dusk and stay by the gate. All night. Shut. The telephone on the dashboard, charged, and you touch nothing till I ring.'

'It's coming through at the patch. There are forty-two people in the home, thirty metres from it, and most of them can't walk.'

'They've staff. It's orange. Red is when people are moved, and I've not had red.' He did not look at her. 'Raise that gate tonight and you put the river through three hundred hectares and whatever's standing on them. A gate is a door in a bank. You don't open it because the house is full.'

'And if the bank goes?'

'It weeps. It wept in ninety-six, at eighty over, and it held. Your father slept on that platform two nights with the pin in and never rang me once.' He put the car in gear. 'Stay by it, as he would have. You'll hear from me.'

The window went up. She had the other one's night in her head from the first ring to the cellar, and she knew what she would hear.

* * *

The commune had nineteen sandbags left. At four she took them to the back door of the home, which gave onto the yard at the dyke end.

The bank stood over the yard wall. The minibus was at the door with its tank full and a can beside it. Inside, down the length of the corridor, the night shift had lined up wheelchairs, each with a blanket folded on the seat and a name on a strip of tape. There were three on tonight, Coralie and Nadine and Bixente, who drove. Coralie had the list on a cord round her neck. It had forty-two names on it, and against each a letter, for walks, or frame, or carried.

'Nine walk,' she said. 'I've put them nearest the front. If the siren goes, Bixente takes fourteen up to the hall and comes back. Three turns. I've not timed it in water.'

'It holds twelve.'

'It holds twelve when somebody's counting,' Coralie said.

'Seven minutes up, in the dry,' Bixente said. 'I did it for the loto.'

A woman sat in the first chair by the back door, with her coat buttoned over her nightdress and her handbag on her knees. She was ninety-four. She had won a duck at the loto.

'You're the one from the crossroads,' Madame Etcheto said. 'On the tractor, with the hay up in the air. It had you. I saw it on you through the glass.'

Maylis put down the bag she was carrying.

'I said the words for you,' the old woman said. 'From my window. They're slow through a window. You went on after, all the same.'

'I didn't hear them.'

'You don't need to hear them. It does.' She looked at the clock over the door, which said ten past four. 'It comes to my room at night. Tonight they want me out here in a chair, and it'll not know where to find me. So I say them now, where it can hear.'

She shut her eyes. She said them to the door, in a voice with no hurry in it.

'Inguma, enauk hire beldur.'

Then she opened her eyes and looked at the sandbags.

'It has to be given something to count. I've no stars in here. Put them down one at a time.'

Maylis laid them across the foot of the door, with the necks folded under, as Sarthou laid his. Madame Etcheto counted each one aloud as it went down.

'One,' she said. 'Two.'

'Here,' said Monsieur Daguerre, from his wheelchair, halfway down the corridor.

'He thinks it's the loto,' Coralie said. 'Leave him. He's been one off the strimmer since November.'

He called at each. Nadine had stopped in the corridor with a blanket over her arm. Coralie did not look at her list.

'Nineteen,' Madame Etcheto said. 'That's few. It counts quickly.'

Nobody called.

'There are fifty-four more on the gendarme's step,' Maylis said.

'Then it can walk down there after.' She folded her hands on her bag. 'I'll not sleep in a chair. I've slept in room twelve for nine years. I'll have them put me back at ten.'
