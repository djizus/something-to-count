# 21

On Friday the envelope from Pau said orange.

Maylis read it through the window of the envelope at eleven, on the mairie step, in the rain. Flood watch, Adour, orange. At eight the river had stood sixty-six centimetres over her father's line. The thread of water from the clay patch to Sarthou's step had widened to a hand, and his bottom course of bags was dark.

She let herself in, laid the envelope in the mayor's tray and picked up the secretary's telephone. She had not used it before. She rang the mairie at Hontaube and asked for its two outdoor men, for one night, at the Port.

The woman at Hontaube asked who was calling. Maylis said the keyholder. The woman asked whether her mayor had made the request, and Maylis said that she was making it. There was a hand over the mouthpiece. Then the woman said that the agreement between the communes ran from the first of January, that before that date Hontaube's men were not covered on Peyrebarthe's works, and that they finished at four on a Friday.

'It's eleven kilometres,' Maylis said.

'We've a river as well,' the woman said, and rang off.

* * *

Guy Larrère was in the mairie nine minutes. He came out with the orange sheet in his coat pocket and lowered his window the usual amount.

'You'll take the van down at dusk and stay by the gate,' he said. 'All night. Shut. The telephone on the dashboard, charged, and you touch nothing till I ring.'

'It's coming through at the patch. There are forty-two people in the home, thirty metres from it, and most of them can't walk.'

'They've staff. It's orange. Red is when people are moved, and I've not had red.' He looked at the wipers. 'Raise that gate tonight and you put the river through three hundred hectares and whatever's standing on them. A gate is a door in a bank. You don't open it because the house is full.'

'And if the bank goes?'

'It weeps. It wept in ninety-six, at eighty over, and it held. Your father slept on that platform two nights with the pin in and never rang me once.' He put the car in gear. 'Stay by it, as he would have. You'll hear from me.'

The window went up. She had the other one's night in her head from the first ring to the cellar, and she knew what she would hear.

* * *

The commune had nineteen sandbags left. At four she took them to the back door of the home, which gave onto the yard at the dyke end.

The bank stood over the yard wall. The minibus was at the door with its tank full and a can beside it. Inside, down the length of the corridor, the night shift had lined up wheelchairs, each with a blanket folded on the seat and a name on a strip of tape. There were three on tonight, Coralie and Nadine and a boy called Bixente who drove. Coralie had the list on a cord round her neck. It had forty-two names on it, and against each a letter, for walks, or frame, or carried.

'Nine walk,' she said. 'I've put them nearest the front. If the siren goes, Bixente takes fourteen up to the hall and comes back. Three turns. I've not timed it in water.'

A woman sat in the first chair by the back door, with her coat buttoned over her nightdress and her handbag on her knees. She was ninety-four. She had won a duck at the loto.

'You're the one from the crossroads,' Madame Etcheto said. 'On the tractor, with the hay up in the air. It had you. I saw it on you through the glass.'

Maylis put down the bag she was carrying.

'I said the words for you,' the old woman said. 'From my window. They're slow through a window. You went on after, all the same.'

'I didn't hear them.'

'You don't need to hear them. It does.' She looked at the clock over the door, which said ten past four. 'It comes to my room at night. Tonight they want me out here in a chair, and it'll not know where to find me. So I say them now, where it can hear.'

She shut her eyes. She said them to the door, in a voice with no hurry in it.

'Inguma, enauk hire beldur.'

Then she opened her eyes and looked at the sandbags.

'It has to be given something to count. I've no stars in here. Put them down one at a time.'

Maylis laid them across the foot of the door, with the necks folded under, as Sarthou laid his. Madame Etcheto counted each one aloud as it went down. Nadine had stopped in the corridor with a blanket over her arm. Coralie did not look at her list.

'Nineteen,' Madame Etcheto said. 'That's few. It counts quickly.'

'There are fifty-four more on the gendarme's step.'

'Then it can walk down there after.' She folded her hands on her bag. 'I'll not sleep in a chair. I've slept in room twelve for nine years. I'll have them put me back at ten.'

'It's the end room,' Coralie said, low. 'Under the bank. She will, too.'

She came out onto the step with Maylis and pulled the door to behind her.

'You've had something since yesterday,' she said. 'You've not written it down, or I'd not know either. I'm not asking for it. I've forty-two to think of till seven.'

'Come back across the landing.'

Coralie stood with the list in her hand.

'Say it again.'

'I'm asking. Come back across.'

'I told you I'd come if I was asked.' Her voice was not steady. She looked up at the bank, and the rain coming off the crown of it. 'I'm off at seven. I'll carry the pillow over myself. Be in the house.'

* * *

She was on the dyke by five, with the commune's van facing the gate. The river stood eighty over the line. It had stopped being brown. It was the colour of the sky, and wide, and it went past the platform at the height of her knees.

On her other side the low meadows lay under a hand of rainwater, going dark. Her mother's ponies were up on the rise. Beyond them the track ran out a kilometre to the tonnes.

Headlamps came along the dyke from the Port. It was the white Trafic. It stopped level with her, door to door, and Jeannot Castagnet put his window down. He was dressed for the night, in waders and two jackets.

'You rang me at five,' he said.

'I wanted to hear you answer.'

'You heard.' He was not laughing. 'What are you sat here for, Lesca?'

'I'm to stay by it. Shut.'

'Then I'm dry.' He jerked his chin at the meadows. 'Eighty over on that side and a hand on mine. That's what a bank's for.'

'Don't go down tonight.'

He looked at her properly, for the first time since the day he took his letter back.

'Why not?'

She had it to the minute, and none of it could be said to him. It had happened to another man in the same waders.

'The bank's coming through at the patch.'

'If it goes, it goes at the Port, and I'm a kilometre off with a ladder under me.' He let the clutch up a little. 'And don't ring. The telephone stays in the van tonight. I'll not have it go off in the cellar a second time.'

'Then I'll come and knock.'

'Not in that van. The track's soft at the dip, where I went down in seventeen. You'd sit there till Easter.'

The Trafic went down off the crown onto the track. She turned her own van a quarter and put her headlamps after him.

They lit the rain. The back of the white van went away down the track in them, throwing mud, and low on its left-hand door the round reflector burned red, screwed on crooked.

She counted her fingers on the wheel, both hands, and made ten. She was awake. She was on a dyke on a Friday night in December, with the river over the line and a telephone on the dashboard, and it was in front of her as she had been given it. Nobody had given her this one.

She watched the reflector to the end. It grew small and stopped where the track ran out. His lamps went off, and hers were the only ones on it. A torch showed low down beside the van, and went under the ground.

When she turned the van back she could see the home from the crown, across Sarthou's roof. Its back door stood open on the yard. Coralie was in the light of it with her arms folded, looking up at the bank. Then she went in, and the door shut on the nineteen bags.

* * *

At ten the river stood eighty-six over. The telephone lit on the dashboard. It was Coralie.

*Etcheto back in 12. Walked it on my arm. Said them again.*

Maylis read it twice. The end window of the home was dark.

She switched off the lamps. The river went by on her right, close under the crown, with the sound of a crowd a long way off. On her left a man was sitting in a cellar. Behind her, under the bank, were Sarthou and the Darrigrands' baby and old Lafitte, and thirty-seven houses more with their shutters closed. There was Coralie, and a woman of ninety-four in her own bed.

The van had no wardrobe. It had a roof a hand above her head, and the rake and two shovels behind the seats. She did not know where it would come down from. It had found her on a mower and on a tractor, and she did not mean to be held to a seat at nine minutes to three with a telephone ringing.

She said the words as she had heard them said, to the windscreen.

'Inguma, enauk hire beldur.'

It was not true. She said it again.

'There are nineteen sandbags at the back door of the home,' she said. 'There are fifty-four on Sarthou's step. Count them. Count the grains in each. You're not to come till you have.'

Nothing answered. The rain went on, on the roof.

She laid the telephone where she could reach it lying down, and set its alarm for four. Then she put the seat back as far as it went, with her forearm against her side, and looked up at the roof of the van.
