# 21

On Friday the prefecture raised the Adour from yellow to orange, and the commune of Peyrebarthe learned of it at two, when the mairie opened and the envelope was taken out of the box.

Maylis had read the gauge at eight. The river was fifty centimetres over her father's line at the bottom of the ebb, and it had not been under the line since Wednesday. At Sarthou's step the thread from the clay patch had become a ribbon as wide as her hand, and it ran over the top course of his bags and under his gate.

She had been awake since five. He had answered on the ninth ring and sworn at her. She had gone down the stairs with that in her ear and had not written anything on the pad.

Guy Larrère drove into his space at five past two and was in the mairie nine minutes. When he came out he had two sheets of paper in his hand. He got into the car before he spoke to her, and lowered the window the usual amount.

'Orange,' he said. 'It's not red. At red they tell me to move people, and they've not told me.' He held up the second sheet. 'I rang Hontaube this morning for their two men, to stand by the gate with you. I have the reply. Hontaube regrets that its outdoor staff cannot be placed at the disposal of another commune before the date on which the merger takes effect. After the first of January they will be glad to hold the key.'

'They have a van.'

'They have. It's in Hontaube.' He folded the sheet. 'So it's you. You'll take the commune's van down at dusk and park on the dyke at the gate, and you'll stay by it. Your father did the same in eighty-one, on the planks, with a storm lantern. The gate stays shut. It was shut at ninety over in my father's time, and the bank held. You'll keep your telephone on and you'll do nothing until it rings.'

'And the Port?'

'The Port has a dyke.'

'It's coming through at Sarthou's.'

'I've had his invoice.' He looked at the green bench with the slat missing. 'It's been coming through there since I was a boy.'

'And the tonnes. Is anybody to go round the tonnes?'

He turned his head. She had not known that she was going to ask it.

'What about the tonnes?'

'If the meadows had to take it. Somebody would be in one.'

'The meadows aren't taking it. I've said shut.' He put the car in gear. 'Hunters are grown men, and there's no article about them. You're not to worry about the tonnes. Your father never did.'

'I'll be by a telephone,' he said, and the window went up.

* * *

The care home stood at the Port crossroads, with its side door open and the minibus backed up to it. At four Coralie was in the yard in her tunic and a waterproof, pouring diesel into the tank from a jerrycan through a funnel. Two other care assistants were carrying folding chairs out to the porch.

Maylis stopped the van at the line and got down.

'Twelve seats,' Coralie said, without looking up. 'Forty-two beds, and nineteen of them can't do stairs. That's four runs up to the hall, if it comes to it, and twenty minutes a run. Nobody's said it'll come to it. They've said have the tank full.' She screwed the cap on. 'I'm on till seven tomorrow.'

'I'm at the gate. He's told me to stay by it.'

'All night?'

'Till he rings.'

Coralie stood the jerrycan down. She looked at the bound hand, and then at the unbound one, which was white across the palm now and needed nothing.

'You'll be on your back in that van at ten to three,' she said.

'That's why I've come.' Maylis had the words ready, and for once they were the ones she meant. 'I want something. Madame Etcheto's words. What she says to it before her light goes out. I want them as she says them.'

Coralie did not answer at once. She had asked a question at that kitchen table for sixteen years, and had posted an envelope on Tuesday for want of an answer to it. This was the first time it had been answered, and the answer was four words of Basque.

'*Inguma, enauk hire beldur,*' she said. She said it as the night staff heard it through a door each evening, flat, with the stress in the wrong place. 'That's the part where she tells it she's not afraid. I don't know what the words are one by one. Then she gives it its counting. The stars, the grass, the sand on the coast. It's not to come till it's counted them.'

'Say it again.'

Coralie said it again. Then she took the biro from her tunic pocket, turned Maylis's left hand over and wrote it across the palm in capitals, as she wrote a room number on her own when she had no paper.

'She's ninety-four,' she said, with the hand still in hers. 'She's said that every night since she was a girl, and she rings at three all the same. I told you that.'

'I only need it to be late.'

'For what?'

'For the telephone.'

Coralie let go of the hand. Behind her the first of the chairs went up into the minibus.

'If that gate moves tonight, you ring me before you touch it,' she said. 'Four runs. I want my eighty minutes.'

The bank had ten, by the other one's reckoning. Maylis did not say so. She got back into the van, and Coralie picked up the jerrycan and went to see to the chairs.

* * *

It was dusk at half past five, and raining as it had rained on eighteen days. She drove along the dyke with her headlamps on and stopped at the gate, at the mouth of the track.

The gauge read sixty over. The top of the night tide was due at three.

She had the handle out of its box and laid on the planks under the tarpaulin when the lights came up behind her. They sat on her bumper. She pulled in against the rail, and the white Trafic drew level, with mud to the door handles, and stopped. Jeannot Castagnet put his window all the way down. He was dressed for the night, in waders and two jackets.

'You rang me at five.'

'Yes.'

'What for?'

'To hear you answer.'

'I answered.' He looked at the van, and the tarpaulin, and the handle under it. 'He's put you out here for the night. Shut and stay by it. He'll have said your father did.'

'In eighty-one.'

'Your father had a lantern and a bottle. I took him the bottle.' He tipped his chin at the meadows. They were grey to the far trees, with the grass standing in a hand of water and the rain going into it. 'That's the first water I've had at the hatch since the Sunday before your rib. There's teal on it you could walk across. I'm not sitting in my kitchen for an orange.'

'Don't go down tonight, Jeannot.'

'Why not?'

'It's sixty over. It'll be ninety by three. If the gate had to be raised, the meadows would be under before you'd your boots on.'

'It stays shut over the line. It has since 1962.' He looked at her as he had at the counter. 'Who's raising it?'

She did not answer.

'No,' he said. 'I didn't think you'd say.' He took his telephone from the dashboard, showed it to her and put it back. 'That stays up here in the Trafic. There's a metre of concrete over that cellar, and nothing gets through it. So you can ring till the battery goes. If you want me in the night, Lesca, you'll walk out and knock on the hatch, like anybody.'

He let the clutch in. The Trafic went past her and turned down off the dyke onto the track.

Then the back of it was in her headlamps, going away. Low on the left-hand door the reflector lit, round, screwed on crooked. She had read it in a chair, and on this road from behind, and at five that morning she had watched water come up a door to it and put it out. She read it now. She looked away at the gauge and counted the fingers of her left hand against the wheel, over the biro. She looked back, and it was the same reflector.

It went down the track between the two ditches, burning red, growing small. At the far end it stopped. His brake lights came on beside it and went off. After that there was only the reflector, in her lamps, in the rain, not moving.

She watched it for as long as her battery could be asked to stand. Then she switched the headlamps off, and it went out with them.

* * *

She turned the van on the dyke, nose to the track, as it would have to stand.

The Port was behind her, forty houses with their shutters closed against the rain, and a light on the stairs of the home. Nobody had been told to move. On Tuesday she had written that she wanted a village that waited for her to decide. She had one now. It was asleep, or going to bed, and it did not know that it was waiting.

She put the telephone on the dashboard, face up, with the sound on.

The van had no wardrobe. It had a roof a hand above her head, and behind the seats the commune's shovels and a coil of rope, and she did not know where it would come from in a place like that. It came to the bed. She had worked that out on a dyke road a fortnight ago. But it had come to her on a mower and on a tractor, and it kept an hour. At nine minutes to three a telephone might already have rung, and a handle might want two hands.

She turned her left palm to the light of the dashboard.

'*Inguma, enauk hire beldur,*' she said. She said it as Coralie had, with the stress in the wrong place, to the windscreen and the rain.

She was not sure that it was true. Madame Etcheto had not been sure for ninety years.

Then she gave it its counting. She had no stars to give it, under that sky. She gave it the sand. There were fifty-four bags of it at Sarthou's step, laid three courses high with the joints broken, and it was to count what was in them, grain by grain, bag by bag, and not come to the gate till it had done.

She let the seat back as far as it went, with her forearm against her side, and lay down on her back under the roof of the van. Then she reached up to the dashboard and set the alarm for four, as on any night.
