# 22, comic and held scenes

Three scenes, in the order of `drafts/22-dark.md`: the aide walks her mark (comic), the front row (held), Arlette in the mairie (held). The dark draft keeps the bedroom and the chair.

Every fact of the dark draft is kept. Added: the aide walking the ceremony as the prefect, his two questions from the sheet, Monsieur Daguerre answering to the date, the secretary's day book, and Arlette saying whom she voted for.

* * *

On Friday at eleven a young man came from the prefecture at Pau to walk the ceremony.

He had a clipboard and a roll of white tape. He paced from the steps of the stage to the stone, tore off two strips and laid a cross on the planks beside it.

'Monsieur le Maire stands here,' he read.

Lesca stood on the cross. He looked at his sheet again, and at her.

'I have Monsieur le Maire.'

'You have. It's been on your letters for eleven years.'

He wrote something in the margin. Then he went back down the steps and came up them as the prefect, with the clipboard under his arm, and shook her hand.

'The prefect speaks. Eight minutes. Then Monsieur.' He stopped. 'Then the mayor, eight minutes. Then the wreath. I've a note that the widow is not to be embraced.'

'She's asked not to be.'

'Is that usual here?'

'It's this year's.'

He wrote that down as well. 'And if the mayor is prevented? I'm to ask it of every commune. It's a line on the sheet.'

'The first deputy reads it. It's typed, and he has his own sash.'

'Is the mayor likely to be prevented?'

'It's your sheet,' Lesca said.

He looked at her for a moment and ticked it. She stood on the cross, a metre from the stone, with a line of tape under each shoe. She did not mean to be there to stand on it tomorrow.

* * *

Then he turned to the square, and to the chairs.

'The front row is for the elected members and the services,' he said. 'It reads better on camera.'

'The front row is the home's,' Lesca said. 'It has been each year.'

Coralie had come up from the Port with the list. She ran the home, and she had the places by heart. She took the tape from him without asking and went along the foot of the stage, tearing off lengths and pressing them to the tarmac a wheelchair's width apart. He followed her with his plan.

'Nineteen this year,' she said. 'They come up in the minibus in two lots. Eight of them were in their beds that night.' She did not look at the list for those. 'Madame Etcheto, who was in twelve, at the dyke end. She says she heard the river go away, like a bath being let out. Monsieur Daguerre, the baker. When the prefect reads out the date he'll call "Here". He does it each year.'

'Can he be asked not to?'

'He can be asked.' She tore off another length. 'The two Lassalle sisters. Madame Hirigoyen. Old Lafitte's brother. Madame Dufau, who doesn't know what year it is and knows that one. And Monsieur Castéra, who was carried.'

She pressed down the eighth strip.

'There were forty-two in their beds. Thirty-four have died since, in them, of what people die of.'

Lesca had said forty houses and a home for eleven years, in that room behind her, in under a minute. She had shaken those eight hands each December along the front row. She had not had them given to her as rooms.

'And the staff?' the young man said. 'Do they sit?'

'There were two of us on,' Coralie said. 'Nadine's in the third row. I'm in the second.' She gave him back his tape. 'Madame Etcheto goes at this end, nearest the stone. She's ninety-four, and she asks for it.'

He marked it on his plan. The stone stood behind them in its bay of planks, with the two names at the level of a speaker's knees.

* * *

At four the mairie was open, and Lesca was signing the day's post.

The secretary came to the door of the office and did not speak. She stood aside. Arlette was behind her, in her coat, with her bag on her arm.

She had not been inside the building since the fourteenth of June 2019. She came three steps into the office and stopped. She looked once round it, at the desk and the green safe, and at the window with the back of the stone in it.

'I said I'd not set foot,' she said. 'I've set it.'

The secretary had the day book open, and her pen.

'Don't put me in that,' Arlette said.

'I enter callers.'

'I'm not calling. I'm her mother.' She waited until the pen was down. 'And I never voted for her to be in here. Three times I've voted for Guy Larrère against her, and I'll say so while I'm stood in it.'

'Sit down,' Lesca said.

'I'll not be here that long.' She kept hold of her bag. 'Coralie rang me again last night. She says you mean something by tonight. She doesn't know what, and nor do I. I know what my mother's rules were for, and I know you've broken each one of them on purpose. I told you that on Wednesday. I told you to see that you came back.'

'I heard you.'

'You hear everybody. Then you do the sum yourself.' Arlette looked at her hands on the blotter, and at the right one, which was shut. 'On your side, Maylis. Tonight. With your knees up, so it has nowhere to sit. I'm not asking.'

Lesca did not answer. She meant to stay in that chair tonight, and she had not changed her mind.

'Then I'll say them, since you won't,' her mother said.

She said the words in the mayor's office, standing, as she said the rosary.

'Inguma, enauk hire beldur.' She drew breath. 'There. It's been told. It wants something to count, and I've no stars either. I've given it my ponies. There were eleven, and they went up a ramp, and I counted them. It can go and find where they are now. That should keep it till morning.'

The secretary had her hand over her mouth.

'I want you at my table at seven,' Arlette said. 'Before the prefect has you. With your fingers counted.'

She went out past the secretary and down the steps. She had been in the mairie four minutes, after seven years, and she was not in the day book. Lesca watched her cross the square to the church side, going round the stage, and she did not look at the lettered face of the stone.
