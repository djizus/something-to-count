# 22

On Friday at eleven a young man came from the prefecture at Pau to walk the ceremony.

He had a clipboard and a roll of white tape. He paced from the steps of the stage to the stone, tore off two strips and laid a cross on the planks beside it. He read from his sheet that Monsieur le Maire would stand there. Lesca stood on the cross. He looked at his sheet again, and at her, and wrote something in the margin.

Then he turned to the square, and to the chairs.

'The front row is for the elected members and the services,' he said. 'It reads better on camera.'

'The front row is the home's,' Lesca said. 'It has been each year.'

Coralie had come up from the Port with the list. She ran the home, and she had the places by heart. She took the tape from him without asking and went along the foot of the stage, tearing off lengths and pressing them to the tarmac a wheelchair's width apart.

'Nineteen this year,' she said. 'They come up in the minibus in two lots. Eight of them were in their beds that night.' She did not look at the list for those. 'Madame Etcheto, who was in twelve, at the dyke end. She says she heard the river go away, like a bath being let out. Monsieur Daguerre, the baker. The two Lassalle sisters. Madame Hirigoyen. Old Lafitte's brother. Madame Dufau, who doesn't know what year it is and knows that one. And Monsieur Castéra, who was carried.'

She pressed down the eighth strip.

'There were forty-two in their beds. Thirty-four have died since, in them, of what people die of.'

Lesca had said forty houses and a home for eleven years, in that room behind her, in under a minute. She had shaken those eight hands each December along the front row. She had not had them given to her as rooms.

'And the staff?' the young man said. 'Do they sit?'

'There were two of us on,' Coralie said. 'Nadine's in the third row. I'm in the second.' She gave him back his tape. 'Madame Etcheto goes at this end, nearest the stone. She's ninety-four, and she asks for it.'

He marked it on his plan. The stone stood behind them in its bay of planks, with the two names at the level of a speaker's knees.

* * *

At four the mairie was open, and Lesca was signing the day's post.

The secretary came to the door of the office and did not speak. She stood aside. Arlette was behind her, in her coat, with her bag on her arm.

She had not been inside the building since the fourteenth of June 2019. She came three steps into the office and stopped. She looked once round it, at the desk and the green safe, and at the window with the back of the stone in it.

'I said I'd not set foot,' she said. 'I've set it.'

'Sit down.'

'I'll not be here that long.' She kept hold of her bag. 'Coralie rang me again last night. She says you mean something by tonight. She doesn't know what, and nor do I. I know what my mother's rules were for, and I know you've broken each one of them on purpose. I told you that on Wednesday. I told you to see that you came back.'

'I heard you.'

'You hear everybody. Then you do the sum yourself.' Arlette looked at her hands on the blotter, and at the right one, which was shut. 'On your side, Maylis. Tonight. With your knees up, so it has nowhere to sit. I'm not asking.'

Lesca did not answer. She meant to stay in that chair tonight, and she had not changed her mind.

'Then I'll say them, since you won't,' her mother said.

She said the words in the mayor's office, standing, as she said the rosary.

'Inguma, enauk hire beldur.' She drew breath. 'There. It's been told. It wants something to count, and I've no stars either. I've given it my ponies. There were eleven, and they went up a ramp, and I counted them. It can go and find where they are now. That should keep it till morning.'

The secretary had her hand over her mouth.

'I want you at my table at seven,' Arlette said. 'Before the prefect has you. With your fingers counted.'

She went out past the secretary and down the steps. She had been in the mairie four minutes, after seven years. Lesca watched her cross the square to the church side, going round the stage, and she did not look at the lettered face of the stone.

* * *

Coralie came up at ten. Lesca had the speech squared on the chest of drawers, four sheets, with the third as it had been typed.

'You've not taken it out.'

'No.'

'And you've not said you'll read it.' Coralie stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame. 'The deputy says you told him where it's kept. If you're prevented.'

'It's the code's word.'

'It's a word for somebody who's expected back.' She looked at the clock radio, turned on its base towards the pillow. 'I'll have my door open. If I come in and you're lying there with your eyes open, I'm not timing it tonight. I'm ringing Bayonne, and I'll tell them whatever name gets you an ambulance.'

She went across the landing.

Lesca counted her fingers and made ten. She lay down on her back in the middle of the bed with her hands outside the blanket. She did not set the alarm. Over there the river would be at ninety by now. She had the figure from her own file.

She did not sleep. The red figures read 02:49, and 02:50.

At 02:51 she turned her eyes to the top of the wardrobe. The folded blanket was on it, and the dust. Nothing else was. In twelve years there had not been a night when she looked there at that minute and found it bare.

Nothing came down. There was no sound on the boards.

Then she had no clock.

* * *

She was standing. The panel was in front of her with both lamps lit. The wall on her left was steel again, with its rivets, and the door was shut in it.

It was in the chair.

It sat there as it had sat on the wardrobe. It filled the seat from arm to arm. The brown leaf hung over the steel on either side, and the rivets ran up its spine to the height of her chest. It had its back to her. She took a step round the chair, and it had its back to her there as well.

The arms of the chair, where they showed, were cold. Nobody had been sitting in it.

She had come to stay. To stay she had to sit.

She turned and put herself down on what there was of the seat, on the front edge, with her spine against it. The cold came through her to the breastbone from behind. It did not move away. She bore down on it with her back and her legs, to keep the place, and it bore up. She pushed harder. So did it, by exactly as much. She had known that of it for twelve years, lying flat, and had left it alone. She could not leave it alone and keep the chair.

She fixed on the left-hand lamp. She rubbed her palms together on her knees, with the seam between them. It held her there, on the edge, and the effort of it was in her thighs and along her back like the winch.

Then she heard the numbers.

They came through the steel on her left. It was a voice with a wall in front of it, giving each one whole and then in its parts, and she could not put a face to it.

'Twelve. One, two.'

Under the voice there was something nearer. It was rain. It was falling on a tin roof a hand above her head, steadily, and a river was going by close on one side. It had not rained on her own tiles since the middle of November.

She knew the roof. It was the commune's van, and it was parked on a dyke. A woman was lying in it on a seat laid back, with a telephone on the dashboard and the key on her belt. She had the same hands. It was her night. Lesca had had hers.

It had begun to rain over there on the twenty-fourth of November. She had written the date at the foot of a page and given it no heading. She gave it none now.

She pushed, and it pushed. She could hold it. She had held a red lamp on a bridge with a lorry behind her. If she held until the other one stood, she would go with her and wake under that roof, and walk the track herself.

And a man would come up a ladder, swearing. She would not owe him anything. Nobody had drowned him. The man she owed was under the planks of a stage, with her name beneath his, and his widow was four hundred metres from this bed. Coralie was across a landing with her door open. In the morning there were eight people to be wheeled to the front row, and one of them had asked to be put nearest the stone.

None of that could be paid from a van in the other village.

'Forty-one,' the voice said, through the steel. 'Four, one.'

She said the five words. She said them aloud, to the left-hand lamp, which was not hers, for the last time.

'Go to the tonne first.'

Then she stopped pushing. She took her hands from her knees and her back from its back, and got up from the chair.
