# 23

At nine minutes to three nothing came down in the van, and she was in the room.

She was standing. Both lamps were lit. It was in the chair, hunched, with the leaf hanging over the steel arms, and it had its back to her. She went a step round it. On that side it had a back as well, turned to where somebody else might have stood, and nobody stood there. The chair was cold. Whoever had sat against it had got up.

She did not sit. She had written on the back of a form that she meant to. She looked at what was in the seat, and at the lamps, and kept her feet.

There was a thing in her head that had not been there when she lay back. It was five words. She had them as she would have had a line off a notice, read once in passing.

*Go to the tonne first.*

A telephone was ringing, a long way off, on a dashboard.

Then she had the roof of the van a hand above her face, and the rain on it. The screen said 02:53. Under the time it said Castagnet, house.

She knew the voice before it had finished her name.

'You're at the gate,' Guy Larrère said. 'Good. Pau has it at ninety over the line and rising. It stays shut. You'll stand by it and touch nothing until I ring again. That's an order of the president.'

Behind him a clock was ticking, close, of the kind that hangs in a kitchen. He was in Jeannot Castagnet's house, at Jeannot Castagnet's telephone, and Jeannot was a kilometre out on the meadows under a hatch.

'Castagnet's in his tonne,' she said.

'I know where he is. He's dry as long as you do what you're told.'

He rang off.

She put the seat up and drove back along the crown, two hundred metres, to where the road came up from the Port. She left the engine running and got out with the torch.

The patch had opened. Water was going down the face of the bank in a sheet the width of a door, across the road and through Sarthou's hedge. His bags were under it. She put her hand flat on the clay beside the sheet, as she had seen it done, and the clay moved under her palm like the flank of an animal.

She had put eleven barrows into that patch in April. She gave it ten minutes.

The screen said 02:55.

It was the other one's sum, and she had it whole. Below the bank were Sarthou, and the Darrigrands' baby, and old Lafitte, and Coralie with forty-two in their beds. The gate would take the river off them. It would put it over the meadows, and over a hatch. The other one had rung once and turned the handle.

The five words lay across the sum like a hand.

She meant to do both. It was a kilometre of track. The Trafic had gone down it at dusk, and where a Trafic went a van would go. Three minutes out. Two to get him up a ladder. Three back. That was eight, and then the handle, with her shoulder under it at nineteen.

She rang Coralie as she drove.

'It's going at the patch,' she said. 'Ten minutes. Start them now. Don't wait for a siren.'

'Where are you?'

'Going for Jeannot. Then I open it.'

'Maylis.'

'Start with the dyke end,' she said, and dropped the telephone on the seat.

The van went off the crown onto the track at 02:57. She kept it in second. The ruts were full, and the wheel pulled in her hands, and each time it pulled the rib caught. Rain came at the lamps. At the far end of them a red disc showed, small, crooked.

At six hundred metres the track dipped. He had told her about the dip.

The nose went down and did not come up. She gave it the throttle, and the back wheels dug, and the van settled across the ruts to its axles with the engine screaming.

02:59.

She got out and ran.

She had not run since the rib. It let her have a third of each breath. The mud took her boots to the ankle. She held the torch in her left hand and her right arm across her side, and counted her steps, since counting was what she had. The reflector grew. At four hundred steps it was the size of a plate, and she went past the Trafic and found the hump of the tonne with her shins.

The hatch was shut. She beat on it with the butt of the torch.

03:03.

It lifted. A lamp came up under it, and Jeannot Castagnet's face, and behind his shoulder a concrete room with a stove lit and a flask on a shelf. The floor was dry. It was as dry as a kitchen.

'I told you not to knock,' he said.

'Come up. I'm opening the gate.'

'You're what?'

'The bank's going at the Port. I'm opening it. You've four minutes after I do, and then this is under.'

'It's shut. He told you shut. Look at that floor, there's not a centimetre on it. I've three hundred teal sitting, and you come down here in the night like your father never'

She took him by the collars of both jackets with her left hand and hauled. He was fifty-eight, and heavy in his waders, and he had a rung under his boots and did not use it. Something went in her side that she heard. He came up over the lip of the hatch on his knees in the mud, swearing, and she did not let go until he was standing.

'Little Lesca,' he said. 'You've gone off your head.'

'Drive.'

He looked at her in the light of his own lamp. Then he drove.

03:06. The Trafic went back along the track faster than she had come down it. He knew the ruts. At the dip his lamps picked up the back of the commune's van, sunk across the track from edge to edge, with its door hanging open.

He stopped a metre short of it.

'I told you,' he said. 'In that van.'

There was no way round. The meadow either side stood in water. She was out before he had the brake on. Her telephone lay on the seat of the commune's van, where she had dropped it, and she took it as she went past.

03:08. Six hundred metres. She ran, and he came behind her in the waders, falling back. She had the telephone in her fist with the screen lit. It said 03:09. It said 03:10. She could see the lamp over the platform, and under it the winch.

She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it went.

It was not loud. It was the sound of a lorry tipping gravel, a long way to her right, and it did not stop. Then the street lamps of the Port went out, from the dyke end, one after another.

03:11. She had given it ten minutes. It had held sixteen.

A siren started, the hand one, at Sarthou's.

She came up onto the crown and put the torch on the gauge. Eighty over. It was falling as she looked. The river was going off the bank by itself, through a gap the width of a house, into the Port.

The handle was in its box. The key was on her belt. She had not turned it a centimetre.

'Open it now,' Jeannot said, behind her. He had no breath. 'It'll take the weight off.'

She was already running along the crown towards the lights that were left.

* * *

The water in the road was at her thighs, brown, moving. Sarthou was in it in his cape with the Darrigrands' baby held above his head. His own door stood open and the river went in at it.

The home had its front lit by the minibus. It had done one turn before the bank went. Fourteen were up at the hall. Bixente had it back at the door with water over the step, and they were bringing the chairs out to it by its headlamps, through the yard, one at a time.

She took the handles of the nearest. It was Monsieur Daguerre. The water was at the hubs, and she pushed with her left side and the chair pulled to the right, and she got him to the step. Jeannot went past her into the corridor in his waders and came out with a woman on his back.

They did that until the telephone buzzed against her leg. It was four o'clock. She had set it herself.

The dyke end had gone first. The corridor on that side was dark, and the water in it was higher than in the yard. Coralie went down it with a torch in her teeth and the list round her neck. She was not gone long. She came back by herself, wet to the collar, and took the next chair by its handles without a word to anyone.

* * *

The village hall had its strip lights on. Somebody had set chairs along the trestles in three rows and put the urn on.

They sat in their blankets with their heads down. Coralie went along the rows with the list. She touched each one on the shoulder, and said a number, and looked at the list, and went on. Maylis stood by the door in her wet things with her arm across her side. Jeannot stood beside her in his waders, dripping on the waxed boards.

'Thirty-nine,' Coralie said. 'Forty.'

She came to the end of the third row. There was a man in the last chair but one, and she touched him.

The last chair was empty.

The water had come in at the dyke end. When she got back to room twelve it had stood level with the bed.

'Forty-one,' Coralie said.
