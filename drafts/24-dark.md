# 24

On Saturday she woke at six on her back, in a dry room, with nothing in her head that was not hers.

Nothing had come after she stood up. She did not know whether five words had got across, or what a woman in a van had done with them. She counted her fingers and made ten. At seven she was at her mother's table, and she ate.

By half past ten the square was full to the church wall, and people were still coming up from the dyke road. The home had the front row. Coralie had brought them up in two lots and set the chairs on her strips of tape, nineteen of them, with rugs. Madame Etcheto was at the end nearest the stone, with her handbag on her knees. Her lips were moving.

Sarthou stood at the foot of the steps in his uniform, with the flag in its sling. He had not been in the square since 2022. When she came to the steps he took a folded sheet from inside his tunic and held it out.

'I had the band off on Sunday,' he said. 'You heard it go.'

She opened it. It was a photocopy of a page from a small notebook, ruled, in capitals. Night of 12 to 13 December 2014. Under the date was one line.

*02:40. WHITE TRAFIC, END OF TRACK.*

'The patrol went along the dyke road at twenty to three,' he said. 'I put the lamp down the track and wrote what it showed me. Then I drove on to the Port, where the water was. In the morning I left it out of the report. I knew whose van it was. I thought I was the one man who'd known he was out there, and I'd driven past him.'

'Why are you giving it to me?'

'You asked me to carry a flag for him. I can't hold both.' He looked at her hands. 'I don't know what you saw from that platform. I've never asked. I know what was there to be seen.'

He had it eleven minutes before she did. She folded the sheet into the speech, behind the fourth page, and went up.

* * *

The prefect spoke first, for three minutes. She stood on the cross of white tape with the stone at her right knee.

Then she had the microphone. Six thousand people stood from the stage to the plane trees and down the road to the Port. Coralie was in the second row in her good coat, behind a wheelchair. Maïté Castagnet stood by the wreath in black. Guy Larrère was at the back, under the fourth tree.

She read it as typed. The first sheet was the rain, and the second was the river. She turned to the third.

'Jean Castagnet was at his tonne that night, as on any night of the season.' Her voice went out over the square and came back off the church. 'No one came to tell him.'

It was true, every word. She had seen his van and rung once, and had not gone.

She waited for it. She was standing up, in daylight, in front of all of them, and she had her knees ready to lock. Nothing came onto her breastbone. There was the wind, and the flag moving. It did not come for what was her own.

Coralie had not looked down. She had heard the line. She knew now which had been done with it.

Lesca read the fourth sheet. In the front row eight people sat who had been in their beds that night, below the river. She did not have a line for them, and she looked along the row once, as far as the old woman at the end, and stopped reading.

She had stood at the stone for eleven minutes. Maïté laid the wreath. She was not kissed.

* * *

The Castagnet house was dark at the front. The light was in the kitchen. It was seven in the evening, three hours before the bull, and from the terrace she could hear the bar in the Port.

Maïté let her in without asking why. She still had her black coat on. She did not offer a chair, and Lesca did not take one. A telephone hung on the wall beside the door, an old one with a cord, and over it a clock.

'There's a thing I've had for twelve years,' Lesca said. 'I told it to the priest in 2015. He's told me each year since that it wasn't him it was owed to.'

Maïté stood with her hands on the back of a chair.

'When I got to the gate that night I put my lamps down the track. His van was at the end of it. I knew it for his. I knew he was in the cellar.' She had gone over the medal for a day, and five words for a week. She had not gone over this. 'I rang his telephone. I let it ring once. I didn't wait for him to answer. Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him.'

The clock ticked over the telephone.

It was the word the council had sat on three times and not put on the stone. She had left the room for each of those votes.

'Once,' Maïté said.

'Once.'

'How long would it have been, to go down and knock?'

'Twenty-five minutes.'

'And the bank?'

'I gave it ten.'

Maïté looked at her hands on the chair. She had asked a question at eleven commemorations, and at the committee before each one.

'What was behind it?'

'Forty houses. Forty-two people in the home, in their beds. And Coralie. She was on nights that week. I'd read the rota.'

'Which were you counting?'

Lesca did not answer. She took the photocopy from her pocket and laid it on the table, open.

'Sarthou's patrol saw the van as well. At twenty to three. He left it out.'

Maïté read the one line. She was a long time over it.

'Then that's the gendarme, and you,' she said. 'And I'll give you the third. He rang you from here.'

'Who did?'

'Guy. From that telephone.' She did not turn her head to it. 'He was in that chair. He came when Jeannot's van went down the lane, on a duck night. He'd done it for four years. So he knew Jeannot was out, and he couldn't have told you how he knew. He rang you and said shut. I stood where you're standing and said, Guy, he's out there. He said if the gate stayed shut it made no odds where he was.' Her voice did not change. 'Then he went home by the back. At nine they came up the lane to tell me.'

Lesca looked at the clock. She had heard it once before, down a telephone, in the dark.

'I'd a suitcase packed on the wardrobe,' Maïté said. 'I unpacked it the week of the funeral. I've stood at that stone for twelve years asking who was meant to go round the tonnes. I asked it at him. He's sat across that table from me and looked at the wall.'

'You never said.'

'Nor did you. Nor did the gendarme.' She took her hands off the chair. 'I've wanted somebody to say it. I didn't think it would be all of us in one day.'

She did not say that she forgave it, and Lesca had not come for that. She had come to pay it where it was owed. It had not got smaller for being said. It was in the kitchen now, with two people who knew its weight.

Down in the Port the band had started.

Maïté went to the door and held it. She looked up at the eaves, in the light from the kitchen.

'That back gutter's coming over again,' she said. 'Look at it before you go.'
