# 25, held scenes

The tone map marks no scene in this chapter comic. Four are held, and they are here in the order of `drafts/25-dark.md`: the Port at eight, the bus, letter 42, the second shovel. The dark draft keeps the wardrobe at 02:51, Marie-Jo at the hall, Coralie in the kitchen and Père Honoré at the cemetery gate.

Every fact of the dark draft is kept. Added: Sarthou counting his sandbags and calling off the eighteenth, old Lafitte and the pad, Maïté's fare, and three things Jeannot says about the letter: that forty-one were paid for, that the rib is in it, and that it is long.

* * *

By eight she was in the Port. The water had gone back through the gap as the river dropped, and left the road grey to the height of a knee. Furniture stood on the pavements the length of it. There were mattresses on end, and a dresser with its drawers out, and the Darrigrands' pram.

Old Lafitte sat outside his door in an armchair that had been under water. He had the score pad drying on his knee.

'You said,' he told her. 'On Tuesday, twice. I'd the pencil in my hand, and I wrote down the colour of a pot.'

She had nothing to say to that, and he did not wait for it.

Sarthou's hedge lay flat under silt. He was on his step with a broom, and his bags were in a heap by the gate, where he had been fetching them back from the road.

'Forty-nine,' he said. 'There's five I'll not see again.' He leaned on the broom. 'The eighteenth's off. You can tell Marie-Jo she has her hall. I've a file, and this one has a name in it.'

The home had its doors open and nobody in it. The ambulances had taken the forty-one to Bayonne on Saturday. It had been due to close in June. The door of room twelve was shut, with a strip of the gendarmes' tape across it.

* * *

At twenty to nine the Bayonne bus stood at the church with its engine running. Maïté Castagnet came along the terrace road in her good coat, carrying a tan suitcase with two straps. She did not look at the café, and nobody came out of it.

She gave the suitcase up to the driver. She had the fare ready in her glove, in coins. It was short, the fare having gone up twice since she counted it out, and the driver looked at the coins and at the suitcase and let her on.

The bus went.

* * *

Jeannot Castagnet came up the path from the gate. He was in his waders, and he had an envelope in his hand. He stopped at the foot of the string.

'I've a letter,' he said. 'I've brought it by hand. Forty-one I paid to register, to have his name on a card, and I never had it once. I'm not paying for this one.'

He held it out. In the top left corner he had written No. 42.

'It's not a complaint,' he said. 'It's the first that isn't. It's for my cousin, with a copy for the mayor's tray. It says that at three on Saturday morning the keyholder was at the hatch of my tonne, a kilometre from her gate, pulling me out of it by the neck. It says why she thought she had to. It says the cellar was dry.' He turned the envelope over. 'It says who had rung her, and from whose kitchen. My cousin had that off the screen of your telephone on Saturday, and he's my cousin.'

'She went on the bus.'

'I saw the wardrobe.' He did not say anything else about that. 'You told me three times to put it in forty-two. It's in. The rib's in, as I said it would be. It's four sides, and it doesn't thank you. I was in no danger, and I'll say so to anyone. But they're saying you went for a walk, and I was what you walked to. That goes in writing.'

She took the letter and buttoned it into her chest pocket, with the slips and the card and the form.

* * *

He looked at the string, and at the cut turf, and at how she held the spade.

'Whose is it?'

'Madame Etcheto's.'

'She gave my mother her first communion dress.' He looked round. A second shovel leaned against the wall of the hut, where her father had kept it. 'You'll not get that down two metres with one arm, Lesca.'

He fetched it. Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it.
