# 25

At nine minutes to three on Sunday the top of the wardrobe was bare.

Maylis had lain awake for it, on her back, in a bed with one pillow. The clock read 02:51 and then 02:52. Nothing sat up there, and nothing came down. In the morning she shook out the blanket over the boards, and no leaf fell.

By eight she was in the Port. The water had gone back through the gap as the river dropped, and left the road grey to the height of a knee. Furniture stood on the pavements the length of it. There were mattresses on end, and a dresser with its drawers out, and the Darrigrands' pram. Sarthou's hedge lay flat under silt, and he was on his step with a broom.

The home had its doors open and nobody in it. The ambulances had taken the forty-one to Bayonne on Saturday. It had been due to close in June. The door of room twelve was shut, with a strip of the gendarmes' tape across it.

At twenty to nine the Bayonne bus stood at the church with its engine running. Maïté Castagnet came along the terrace road in her good coat, carrying a tan suitcase with two straps. She did not look at the café. She gave the suitcase up to the driver, and got on, and the bus went.

Marie-Jo Labat was at the door of the hall with the keys, counting stacked chairs back against the wall. She stopped at Maylis.

'I'll tell you what they're saying, so that you have it from me,' she said. 'I was in the café for bread. Somebody came in late, and nobody said he was still in the box. They said he had the key and went for a walk.' She looked at how Maylis held her side. 'Nobody laughed. They've retired the other.'

'It's true.'

'I know it's true. I was in this hall at five on Saturday with the urn on.' She went back to her chairs. 'Forty-one,' she said, to the stack.

* * *

Coralie came in at nine. She had been thirty hours in her tunic, and it had dried on her twice.

She sat down at the kitchen table in her coat. The shopping pad lay on it, with Monday's boxes empty, and Dressing, and Tonight. She did not pick up the pencil.

'You rang me at four minutes to three,' she said. 'You said to start with the dyke end. I did. I went to twelve first. She said she'd not go out to a minibus in her nightdress, and to come back when I had her coat. So I got the chairs moving. When I went back it was level with the bed.' She said it as she would have given it to a doctor. 'That's mine. I'll have it now for as long as I have anything.'

'I was on the track. I had the key.'

'I know where you were. Castagnet's told the Port.' Coralie looked at her. 'You asked me something on Friday, at the back door. I said seven. At seven I was counting.'

'I'm still asking.'

'I heard you.' She put both hands flat on the oilcloth. 'I've asked you one question for sixteen years and drawn you boxes for it. I'll ask it once more, with no box. What do you want, Maylis?'

Maylis had the answer. She had not had to look for it.

'She's to be buried here, on Tuesday. I want to dig it myself.'

Coralie nodded slowly. It was the first thing she had been told in that kitchen that was a want and not a refusal.

'Then you'll dig it,' she said. 'With that side. I'll strap it first.'

She strapped it, with the wide bandage from the box. Then she went up. Maylis heard her on the landing, and a door, and did not listen for which.

* * *

The cemetery was above the village and had kept dry. Père Honoré was at the gate under his umbrella.

'Tuesday at ten,' he said. 'Her daughter asks that the words she said each night be said over her. I have had them written out for me. I am not sure that they are a prayer.'

'They're for a thing that sits on you.'

'Then they will serve.' He looked at the plot, by the wall, beside her husband's. 'You will dig it?'

'Yes.'

'It is the eighth this year. I have kept count as well.'

He went down to his Clio. She took the pegs and the line from the hut. She set the first peg at the head and measured two metres from it, and eighty centimetres across, and ran the string round the four. On the first morning she had read a stone as an eighth grave, and had thought it was her own.

She cut the turf along the string with the edge of the spade, left-handed.

Jeannot Castagnet came up the path from the gate. He was in his waders, and he had an envelope in his hand. He stopped at the foot of the string.

'I've a letter,' he said. 'I've brought it by hand. I'm not paying a registered fee for this one.'

He held it out. In the top left corner he had written No. 42.

'It's not a complaint,' he said. 'It's the first that isn't. It's for my cousin, with a copy for the mayor's tray. It says that at three on Saturday morning the keyholder was at the hatch of my tonne, a kilometre from her gate, pulling me out of it by the neck. It says why she thought she had to. It says the cellar was dry.' He turned the envelope over. 'It says who had rung her, and from whose kitchen. My cousin had that off the screen of your telephone on Saturday, and he's my cousin.'

'She went on the bus.'

'I saw the wardrobe.' He did not say anything else about that. 'You told me three times to put it in forty-two. It's in. It doesn't thank you. I was in no danger, and I'll say so to anyone. But they're saying you went for a walk, and I was what you walked to. That goes in writing.'

She took the letter and buttoned it into her chest pocket, with the slips and the card and the form.

He looked at the string, and at the cut turf, and at how she held the spade.

'Whose is it?'

'Madame Etcheto's.'

'She gave my mother her first communion dress.' He looked round. A second shovel leaned against the wall of the hut, where her father had kept it. 'You'll not get that down two metres with one arm, Lesca.'

He fetched it. Then he stepped over the string, in his waders, and got down into it.
