* * *

Coralie had the shopping pad in her hand when Maylis came in at seven, with both boxes empty on it and Either written across the bottom.

'I don't cook Either,' she said. 'There's no recipe for it. Soup or duck.'

'Whichever's less trouble.'

'That's Either again, with more words.' She dropped the pad on the table, pulled the tea towel off the oven rail and rubbed Maylis's hair with it, hard, where she stood in her waterproof. 'Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you, and that was me. Eggs, then. Eggs is what Either gets in this house.'

She broke six into the pan. Maylis sat down by stages and told her about the registered letter, and the postman, and the man in the car who was looking at the church.

'He never said he'd tell him.'

'He said, I'll tell him.'

Coralie put the fork down. She opened the oven door the width of two fingers, bent, and spoke through the gap into the dark of the oven.

'I'll be brief,' she said, in Larrère's voice, which was lower than hers and came from the back of the neck. 'The eggs. Your father had his eggs done by Saint Catherine's, and never asked the commune for a pan. There's the question of the eggs. It isn't settled, and you're not to worry about it.' She looked at the oven shelf. 'In my tray,' she said, and shut the door on him.

Maylis laughed. It got as far as the rib and stopped there, and she sat with her forearm against her side and her eyes shut, laughing with what was left.

'Don't,' Coralie said. 'No. Wait.' She took the pan off the ring and fetched the box she kept from the home. She strapped the rib where Maylis sat, over her T-shirt, three turns of the wide bandage, pulled tight. 'Now you can.'

'It's gone.'

'Then I'll do the president,' Coralie said. She did him, as far as the room he had been in as mayor, and it came back.

They ate the eggs out of the pan. Afterwards Coralie ticked both boxes herself. She wrote eggs under them, drew a third box and ticked that.

At ten she pulled the blanket off the bed and shook it out over the boards. A few crumbs of brown leaf came down, as they had on most nights for twelve years.

'Those planes,' she said. 'You bring the square home in your hair.'

She had the early shift. She lay down on the wall side and was asleep before the light was out. Maylis put it out at ten past ten. She lay on her back, as she had been told to, and looked at the top of the wardrobe.
