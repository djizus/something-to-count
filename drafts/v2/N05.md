* * *

On Wednesday Coralie was on lates, and at a quarter to ten Maylis drove down to the Port to fetch her, as she did in a wet week.

The home had its night lights on. Coralie was in the corridor at the dyke end, in her tunic, with her arms folded, outside the last door. It had a twelve on it and stood a hand open.

'Two minutes,' she said, low. 'I can't go till she's done. It's in the care plan.'

'What is?'

'Listen.'

An old woman was speaking inside, slowly, to somebody.

'Inguma, enauk hire beldur.'

'That's Madame Etcheto. She's ninety-four, and she's had that room fourteen years. She says that every night to a thing that sits on her chest. It means she's not afraid of it.' Coralie put her mouth to Maylis's ear. 'Now she gives it its work.'

'You'll count the stars,' the voice said. 'The ones behind the cloud as well. Then the grass on the low meadows, each blade. Then the sand on the coast, from Bayonne to the border. You're not to come till you've done.'

'Does it work?'

'She rings at three.'

At the far end of the corridor a boy stood with a mop and came no nearer.

'That's Bixente. He drives the minibus. He has the Basque from his grandmother, and in his first week he said the words along with her, through the door, to be friendly. She reported him. She said it had been told twice and given two lots of stars, and it got through them early and came at one.' Coralie's shoulders had started. 'It's in the plan now. Staff to wait. Staff not to join in.'

Maylis laughed. The rib caught it and turned it into a bark, and that finished Coralie, who put her forehead against the wall beside the door and shook. Maylis got her arm across her side and leaned on the wall as well. They could not look at each other. Down the corridor Bixente studied his mop.

'Who's that at my door?'

Coralie wiped her eyes on her sleeve and pushed the door open.

'It's Coralie, Madame Etcheto. And Maylis Lesca, come to take me home.'

The room had a bed, a wardrobe and a window with the bank in it. Madame Etcheto lay propped on two pillows with her hands outside the blanket. She took her time over Maylis, and over the arm she held across herself.

'The commune's girl. Your father dug for my husband.' She had every word of her own voice. 'You were laughing.'

'It was the rib.'

'A rib doesn't laugh. What's wrong with it?'

'She cracked it on Monday,' Coralie said. 'She's to sleep on her back. And on Tuesday at three she was awake with a pulse of a hundred and twelve, and she tells me that was the rib as well.'

'Where did you sleep before Monday?'

'On my side. At the edge.'

The old woman nodded, as at a sum that had come out.

'Then that's Inguma. It has a name, so use it. It's no good my saying the words for you. It wants telling by the one it sits on, and it wants work. What have you a great many of?'

'Leaves,' Maylis said. 'In the square.'

'Then give it those. Go on. I've said mine.'

Maylis glanced at Coralie, who had stopped laughing.

'It's to count the leaves in the square.'

'Before it comes.'

'Before it comes.'

'That will do for one night.' Madame Etcheto shut her eyes. 'It hasn't come for you, girl. It comes to that bed. You've been lying where it sits since Monday, and you've given it nothing to count.'
