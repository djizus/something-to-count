Lesca was standing. The panel was in front of her with both lamps lit. The wall on her left was steel again, with its rivets, and the door was shut in it.

Inguma was in the chair.

It sat there as it had sat on the wardrobe. It filled the seat from arm to arm. The brown leaf hung over the steel on either side, and the rivets ran up its spine to the height of her chest. It had its back to her.

A woman was standing on the far side of it.

She was a chair's width away. She had on a yellow waterproof, zipped to the neck, with rain on the shoulders. The cuffs were turned back once. Her right forearm lay across her side, under the ribs, and stayed there, holding. The hand at the end of it had a turn of bandage across the palm. There was a key on her belt, and mud on her boots to the ankle.

She was looking at Lesca.

Lesca had come to that room for a fortnight. She had sat in the warmth somebody left in a chair. She had read a card in her own hand, and lain nine breaths under another lampshade, and opened a hand that somebody else was shutting. She had put all of it in a notebook, under numbers. She had no number for this.

She knew the waterproof. She had handed it in with her keys. She knew how the weight was carried on the left foot, to spare that side. She knew the hand under the bandage, since she had one.

The woman's eyes went down to Lesca's right hand, which was shut. They stayed on it.

Neither of them spoke. Lesca had spoken first in every room since she took the chair, and she stood with her mouth closed.

It had been dry in her village since the middle of November. The rain on those shoulders was tonight's.

She had come to stay. She had meant to sit in that chair and hold a lamp until the other one got up, and go with her. She would wake under the roof of a van, on a dyke, with a telephone ringing. She would put down the handle and walk the track and knock on a hatch herself.

The chair was taken. To sit she would have to sit on Inguma's back and bear down, and it would bear up as hard as she pushed. She had known that of it for twelve years. She could do it all the same. She had held a red lamp on a bridge with a lorry behind her.

And she would be doing it a chair's width from this woman, with this woman watching.

If Lesca held, and the other one turned and went to her own night, there would be two of them in that waterproof. There would be two in the one right hand, with a bandage on it. She had opened that hand once already against its owner. At the winch, with the river at ninety over, it would go to whichever of them held on longer, and Lesca had been holding on to that night for twelve years.

She looked at the forearm across the ribs. The rib had gone on a winch that Lesca had sold by weight to a dealer. The woman had got into a van with it and driven to a gate, since a man had told her to through a car window. She was holding herself together with one arm, and she had come. It was her night. Lesca had had hers.

And what was there to take? A man would come up a ladder over there, swearing. Lesca would owe him nothing. Nobody had drowned him. The man she owed was under the planks of a stage, with her name cut beneath his, and his widow was four hundred metres from her bed. Coralie was across a landing with her door open. In the morning eight people were to be wheeled to a front row, and one of them had asked to be put nearest the stone.

None of that could be paid from inside another woman's coat.

Through the steel on her left a voice was calling, a long way off, each number whole and then in its parts.

'Twelve. One, two.'

The woman had not moved. She stood square, as she had been taught to stand to a handle.

Lesca opened her right hand and turned the palm up. The seam lay across it, white, from the forefinger to the heel. She held it where it could be seen.

She had given five words to a lamp at each hour for a week, and had not once thought that there would be a face to give them to. She took her eyes off the chair.

'Go to the tonne first,' she said, aloud, to a woman with her arm across her ribs.
