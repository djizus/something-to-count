At nine minutes to three Maylis was standing in the room, and a woman was looking at her across the chair.

Inguma was in the seat, hunched, with the leaf hanging over the steel arms. It had a back to each of them. Both lamps were lit.

The woman was barefoot, in the T-shirt she slept in, and dry. Her right hand was shut at her side. Then it opened and the palm came up. A white seam ran across it from the forefinger to the heel.

Maylis had that line on her own palm, a week old. This one was twelve years old. It was the hand that had turned a winch in the dark with the skin off it. Before that it had held a telephone, at this minute, and let it ring once.

She looked at it for as long as it was held out.

'Go to the tonne first,' the woman said.

It was her own voice. She knew it off the commune's answering machine. It was lower than she used it, and it asked for nothing.

She had written on the back of a form that she meant to stay in that chair, where the sash was. The seat was there to be fought for. She would have to sit on Inguma, which she had given sandbags to count, and bear down, and outlast a woman who had borne down for twelve years. Then she would wake in a dry bed with that seam in her hand, and with what had made it.

She had seen the price of it on Thursday. Now it stood in front of her on bare feet.

Behind her, a long way off, something had begun to ring. She knew what it was lying on, and what bank that was parked on. It was her own night. The river was over her father's line. A man had gone under the ground at dusk with a torch. Coralie was behind the bank with a list round her neck, and had told her to be in the house at seven.

Maylis stepped back from the chair, and left it to Inguma.
