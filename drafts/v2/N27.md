# N27, chapter 23, comic then dark. Joins: after the first two paragraphs of 25a, before "By eight she was in the Port."

* * *

At first light on Sunday Maylis walked down into the Port, since nothing on wheels could get there.

The road was silt from wall to wall, grey, and it took a boot to the ankle. Guy Larrère's car had got as far as the crossroads by the home. It sat there to its sills with the driver's door open, and he was coming on down the road on foot. He had his folder in his hand and nothing to hold it over. He walked as a man walks on ice, with his arms a little out, and the silt had his trousers to the knee.

Sarthou stood in the road outside the last house, in uniform, with his cap on. He let the mayor come to within a metre. There was no car round him.

'They tell me,' Larrère said, and stopped.

Sarthou held a telephone up at the length of his arm, with the screen lit. It was hers. He had asked her for it in the hall on Saturday, and she had given it to him. He held it where a man of that age could read it without his glasses.

'Friday night,' Sarthou said. 'Seven minutes to three. Incoming. It says Castagnet, house. That's a telephone on a kitchen wall, in a house that isn't yours, and the man whose house it is was a kilometre off under a hatch. I've the keyholder's statement of what was said down it.' He lowered his arm. 'That goes in the file. You'll come to the brigade at two. I'd not try it by car.'

Larrère read the screen. Behind it the door of the gendarme's house stood open, and the water had left its mark along the wallpaper at the height of a table. His mouth worked. He turned to Maylis, as he had for eighteen years through a gap, and found nothing between them to wind up.

He turned round in the silt, which took some time, and walked back up the road to his car.

The commune's van was still across the track, in the dip. Kévin Labat backed his own down to it, put a chain on the towing eye and took up the slack.

'Castagnet's been writing to the mairie about that dip since seventeen,' he said. 'You went in where he did.' He shrugged. 'It'll come.'

It came on the third pull, backwards, with its door hanging open.

'What do I owe you?'

'Nothing. Four winters you looked the other way for nothing. I can do a morning.' He coiled the chain. 'The boxes stood on the pavement at the home till the battery went. I tipped them back in the river.'

He sat down on the step of his van.

'I flew,' he said. 'Saturday night. I'd not slept since Thursday, and I went straight in with no counting, and I was up. Over the hall, with the lights on in it. Properly, as high as the church.' His shoulders stayed where they were. 'I didn't like it. You can see what's under you. The Port with no lamps, and the gap.'

He held up his left hand.

'On Friday in that yard, in the water, with the chair in my hands, I counted. I made five. I counted them again.' He closed the hand. 'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything as much.'

'It was five,' she said.

He got into the commune's van and drove it up onto the crown for her, since she could not turn the wheel with that side.
