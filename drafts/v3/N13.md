On Monday morning the other one left the chair first, and Lesca went with her.

She had gone in at half past four and fixed on the left lamp. She was given the gauge on the pier of the old gate, with rain running down the iron and the river a forearm under her father's line. An alarm began a long way off, in the dry house, and she held past it. Then the warmth went out of the arms of the chair, as it goes out of a bench when the person beside you stands. She was holding the lamp, and she was no longer sitting.

She lay on her back under a paper lampshade, with rain on the tiles. It was her bedroom, and the bed had one pillow on it. The other half of the mattress kept the shape of somebody who had slept there until Friday. A rib caught on the right when she breathed. The right hand lay shut on the blanket, bound across the palm.

Across the landing a second alarm rang and was stopped. The door of the box room opened, and bare feet came over the boards. For eleven years Lesca had heard that step go the other way.

Coralie came in with her hair flat on one side and sat down on the edge of the bed. She lifted the right wrist off the blanket, laid two fingers on the inside of it and watched the clock radio. She was warm from her bed. The Coralie that Lesca had at home came no nearer than a door frame.

'A hundred and thirty,' she said. 'You've been up since four again. What is it this time?'

The other one meant to say that it was the rib. Lesca had the answer going past her on its way to the mouth. She told the hand to open, and it opened. She turned it over under Coralie's fingers and took hold of them.

They were warm, and rough at the tips from the soap at the home. Then the hand she held them with began to draw in on itself, from inside, as a fist closes on a thing it means to keep. The other one wanted it shut. Lesca kept it open. It hurt across the bandage, in somebody else's palm.

'Maylis?' Coralie said.

Lesca told the mouth to open. It was stiff, and it was hers to use.

'Come back across. I've left you on that landing eleven years.'

The voice came out lower than its owner used it.

Coralie kept her two fingers where they were, on the pulse.

'It's three nights,' she said. 'Friday, Saturday and last night. I can count three.' She was looking at the mouth. 'And you don't ask. In sixteen years you've never once asked me for anything. I'd have come back for half that sentence. Where did you get eleven?'

Under Lesca's grip the fingers pulled. The other one wanted her hand and her mouth, and for as long as Lesca held she had neither.

Coralie bent and looked into one eye and then into the other, as she looked at the old people at the home when they started being somewhere else. She took her fingers back, and had to pull to get them.

'Who is that?'

She stood up off the bed, with the hand Lesca had held shut inside her other one. The mouth had begun on an answer, and Lesca could not have said which of them it belonged to.

Then Inguma was on Lesca. She did not see it come. It sat on her breastbone with its back to her, and the tiles over it were dry. The clock radio read 06:22. Coralie was coming round the bed in her dressing gown with the telephone in her hand, and Inguma got down.

'Twenty-two minutes,' this Coralie said. 'Lie still. Your eyes were open and I couldn't wake you, so I've rung your mother. Give me that wrist, Madame le maire.'

She took it between a finger and thumb and watched the clock. Lesca let her have it, and kept the hand shut.
