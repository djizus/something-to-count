# N20, chapter 16, comic with dread. It is the whole chapter. It takes in the breakfast of 15b, the café of 15d and the window of 15e, each replayed with Lesca in Maylis's body. Maylis wins her mouth twice, on "Either" and on "Put it in forty-two".

* * *

On Tuesday Lesca went out of the chair with Maylis at six, into a bed with one pillow and rain on the tiles, and Inguma did not come to fetch her.

She lay and waited for it. The clock radio went from six to ten past, and to twenty past. Inguma was counting gravel, stone by stone, and there were a great many loads.

She got up. The legs were hers to move, and so was the left hand. In the right hand, under its bandage, Maylis was awake. Lesca could feel her in the fingers, which shut whenever they were left alone.

A yellow waterproof hung on the back of the kitchen door, with COMMUNE DE PEYREBARTHE across the shoulders. Lesca handed that waterproof in with her keys in 2015. She put it on, and it knew her elbows.

Coralie was at the sink in her tunic, rinsing a bowl. Her hair was wet from the yard. The pocket of the tunic said care assistant. No keys lay by the bowl. In this village the home was not hers to run.

'You're down early,' Coralie said. 'Did you get up at four?'

'When does the director get in?' Lesca said. They were her first words to this woman in daylight, and she said them as she said things. 'I want her in my office at nine. That river is over the line this afternoon and a great deal over it by Friday. The home wants a plan for the minibus before then, in writing, with the names of those who can't walk.'

Coralie turned round with the bowl in her hands.

'Your office?'

'The mairie will do, if she'd sooner.'

'Maylis.' Coralie put the bowl on the rack and came across the kitchen. She laid two fingers on the strapping under the right arm, to see what the breath did. Lesca had slept across a landing from those hands for eleven years. She stood under two fingers and could not have said what her breath did.

'You've a shed,' Coralie said. 'The director comes out from Bayonne and has spoken to you twice, about the hedge. Who is it wants her at nine?'

The shopping pad lay on the table. It said *Tonight*, and under that *soup*, and under that *omelette*, with a box drawn beside each. Both boxes were empty. Every box on that pad was empty, back to the cardboard. Lesca did not leave boxes unticked. She took up the pencil in the left hand, ticked soup, and wrote seven o'clock beside it.

Coralie looked at the tick for some time.

'You ticked it,' she said. 'Sixteen years I've drawn those. What do you want, Maylis? I've a tick. Can I have the rest?'

Lesca had the rest. It was this kitchen, and her name said like that, and a pillow carried back across two landings. She opened Maylis's mouth to give it at length.

'Either,' the mouth said.

It was Maylis's word, and Maylis got there first. Lesca felt the jaw shut on it and hold.

Coralie let her breath go. She was close to laughing.

'There you are,' she said. 'I was wondering who I'd made coffee for.' She took her keys off the hook. 'Leave the tick, will you? I'd like to look at it tonight.'

* * *

At eight Lesca stood on the platform of the old gate with the key on her belt.

In her own village that winch was sold by weight. Here it stood greased, with its handle locked in a box, and on the pier beside it was her father's red line. The river was twenty centimetres under the line at the bottom of the ebb, brown, and rising. On the eighth of December 2014 it was at the line by the afternoon. It was in her own minute.

Behind her the low meadows were grass. Her mother's ponies stood on the rise with their tails to the river, and she counted them, since she could. Out past them was the hump of a tonne, with a dry cellar under it.

She drove back through the Port. The old bank stood over the road, with a patch of clay on its face that was dark with wet. Under it were the four houses she bought for the State in 2016 and took down. Sarthou was clipping his hedge in front of the last. Old Lafitte's shutters were open next door. A line of small clothes hung under the Darrigrands' porch, out of the rain, and somebody in that house was singing to a baby.

She put those to the council as four. She drove past them at walking pace.

She was here to stop a flood, with three days and a mouth.

* * *

Guy Larrère drew up beside her in the square at nine, in the space in front of the mairie, where no stone stood. He lowered his window a few centimetres.

For eleven years Lesca sat across a table from that man and timed him. He never once spoke to her through a gap.

'The signs,' he said. 'Pau wants the count by Friday. Every panel with the commune's name on it, on the form, in the columns. Your father would have had that count in his head. He wouldn't have needed to go round with a board.'

'You have three minutes,' Lesca said.

The wipers went across. On the far side of the square a cup stopped at the café window.

'What did you say?'

Maylis was at the jaw by then, and pulling. Lesca kept it.

'Three minutes on any item. It's the rule, Guy, and it was made for you. You've had one of them on my father. In the two that are left I'll tell you what you ought to be asking me. That river is over the red line on this afternoon's tide. Pau will send you a yellow at eleven, and you'll tell me it stays shut, and that the Port knows it has a river. On Friday night the bank comes through at the last house. I'd spend my two minutes on that.'

The window went down. It went all the way, into the door. In all her years beside that car Maylis never saw it done, and her surprise came through to Lesca whole.

His face was not the face he kept for signs.

'Guy,' he said. 'From the commune's girl, in my own square. They tell me you were at the Castagnet house on Sunday. With flowers. Twenty minutes in the kitchen. He's sat at home every night since, like a dog in a doorway, and nobody can get near the house. You'll keep away from it. That's not advice. And what you've said this morning goes in a file that Hontaube opens in January.'

'A man's wife is no business of the mayor's,' Lesca said. She said it to have the last word. She did not know yet how much of one it was.

Maylis pulled at the jaw the whole time and did not get it.

The window went up, all the way.

* * *

Lesca counted the signs of Peyrebarthe in the rain, on a board, with a pencil.

There was the panel at the mairie door, and the one on the hall. There was the blue plate on the school and the arrow to the cemetery. In her own village each of those would go on saying what it said, since there it was Hontaube that asked to come in. Here they would be unscrewed in January. She was counting, for a man in a car, the places where the name of her commune was to come down.

She wrote each one out in full, with its street, in the script that leaned. At the fourth, the right hand came up off her side in its bandage. It took the pencil out of the left and made a short upright stroke in the first column. It made three more, and put a line through the four. Then it gave the pencil back.

Two women were keeping that count, in two hands, and the form began to show it.

* * *

At noon she went into the café for bread, and stopped inside the door.

Jeannot Castagnet sat at the counter with a glass of white. He wore a day's beard, with rain on the shoulders of his jacket, and he was scratching his ear. Each December she laid a wreath for that man.

Lesca looked at him. She went on looking. Maylis, who since Sunday could not look at him, dragged the eyes down to his boots, and Lesca brought them up again.

Old Lafitte had the score pad turned over on the card table, with Sunday on it to the minute. He was reading it to the owner's wife. He broke off.

'On Sunday she couldn't look at him,' he said. 'Look at her now.' He wrote.

'What?' Jeannot said, to her. 'What is it? Have I something on me?'

'You owe the commune for a tank of diesel,' Lesca said. 'January 2017. It was stopped out of her pay.'

'Out of whose?'

'Mine.'

The card table put its cards down.

'I'll take that in order,' Jeannot said, and turned on his stool, the whole of him. 'One. I never asked to be pulled out. I was waiting for the ground to harden. Two. The ruts are there yet, and I've written. Three. If the commune wants paying for a tow that nobody ordered, it can say so in a letter, registered, and I'll sign for it myself, which is more than he does.'

He enjoyed that. Lesca found that she was smiling at him, in a café, on a Tuesday, with the room looking on.

'That's the pot explained,' said the owner's wife.

'Bronze, was it?' Lafitte said. 'Or yellow? I've been given both.'

'I don't know. I wasn't there.'

'She wasn't there,' Lafitte told the table, and wrote it.

Sarthou sat at the table in the window, in uniform, with a sandwich in its paper.

'I am not hearing a statement,' he said. 'A file was closed on Friday. I retire on the eighteenth without another.' He turned his chair to the wall.

'It isn't a statement. It's a list, and she can have it read.' Lafitte held the pad off. 'Counts her fingers on the hour. Signs for a parcel like a prefect. Paces a bank through four gardens and puts a bench on the gendarme's cabbages. Has the mayor's bollard over with the mower. Orders a part for the winch at the mayor's desk, with the mayor's stamp. A pot of chrysanthemums to this man's wife. And at nine this morning she told the mayor, through his own car window, that he had three minutes. I had that from here. The window went right down.'

'It wants a heading,' said the owner's wife.

'Madame le maire,' said the man who was dealing. 'The desk, the stamp, three minutes. She thinks she's the mayor. Put that.'

It carried, with Jeannot's hand on his glass. Lafitte wrote it across the top and ruled a line under it, and turned the pad round for her.

Lesca read her title, in a pensioner's capitals, over a list of what she had done to another woman's fortnight.

'It takes a small m,' she said.

'She'd know,' said Lafitte, and the table was very happy.

She laid the bread money on the counter. For eleven years she gave one minute each December to a table that knew it by heart. She gave it now to a café hearing it for the first time, with the tenses changed.

'It has rained here since the twenty-fourth of November. This morning the river stood twenty under the red line at the ebb, and it will be over on this tide. On Friday night it will be ninety over, and rising. The bank is coming through at the patch by the last house. When it starts to go, it will have ten minutes in it. Behind it are forty houses, and the home, with forty-two people in their beds.' She turned to Lafitte, who slept under that bank. 'And out on the meadows there is one man.'

'She's changing the subject,' said the owner's wife.

Lafitte wrote none of it down.

'Is that a forecast?' Sarthou said, to the wall. 'I cannot file a forecast.'

Only Jeannot listened, and to one part.

'Over on this tide,' he said. 'Then there's a hand of water at my tonne tonight. Five nights I've sat in.' He got off his stool. 'I asked that mairie for it in twenty-six letters, and I'll not leave it standing there. I'm going down.'

'Go tonight if you must,' Lesca said. 'On Friday you stay in your kitchen.'

'Now she wants him kept in,' the owner's wife said.

'That's so we'd not think it,' said Lafitte, and wrote that down as well.

'I'll go down Friday because you've said it,' Jeannot told her. He stopped beside her on his way to the door. He smelt of the river, and he was breathing.

Lesca found nothing to give him. She had rung his number once.

The jaw moved, and this time she let it.

'Put it in forty-two,' Maylis said.

'It's getting long,' said Jeannot Castagnet, and went out to his van.

'Then he is in a hole in a meadow every night until the eighteenth,' Sarthou said, 'and he cannot write to anybody from there.' He turned his chair back. 'I would leave that house alone, all the same. His staying in of a night has put out more people than you'd think.'

Lafitte turned the pad over and dealt.

* * *

By two the river stood over her father's line, and a yellow envelope from Pau lay in the mayor's tray under a reminder about signs.

Lesca went on with the count. The dyke road was still to do. She had three days, in a village that voted her its mayor for a joke and would not hear her read the river. She stood at the mairie door with the point of the pencil on the panel beside it, to keep her place.

Inguma was on her.

The gravel was counted. Inguma sat on Maylis's breastbone in the rain, in daylight, with its back to the door of the mairie. The square went grey from the edges. The last she had of that village was a pencil held against a sign, and Maylis's hand closing round it to take it back.
