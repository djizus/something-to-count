# N21, chapter 17, comic with dread. It opens the chapter and takes in 16a, with Maylis in Lesca's body. The sandbags of 17a follow, on Wednesday afternoon. Lesca wins the mouth once, too late, and the right hand at the sash.

* * *

On Tuesday night Maylis said the words to the top of the wardrobe and gave Inguma work. It was to count the maize kernels on every card of the loto, each card Marie-Jo printed, played or not. At six on Wednesday Lesca got up from the chair, Maylis held the right-hand lamp and went with her, and nothing came to fetch her back.

The bed was the same bed, with Maylis in the middle of it. The window was white with frost and the tiles were quiet. She breathed in to the bottom, and nothing caught. The right hand had a seam across the palm in place of a weal. Under the seam Lesca was awake, and trying to make a fist.

'Madame le maire is to get up,' Coralie said, from the doorway. She wore a dressing gown and held a bunch of keys. Her pillow was not on this bed. 'Three of your councillors are in your mother's kitchen. They've been there since six and they won't say it to me. You'll eat before you cross that yard. There's bread cut.'

Maylis got up. That voice had told her to do things for sixteen years, though never with a title on the front.

'You didn't argue,' Coralie said. She waited for the argument, and it did not come. 'Then go.'

* * *

Maylis crossed the yard over frozen gravel, in a dark coat that was narrow across the shoulders.

Monsieur Tastet, the first deputy, had the chair by the range, with his hat on his knee. Two more of the mayor's list sat beside him. Each held a bowl of coffee, and none of them got up.

'Sit down, Maylis,' her mother said.

She sat at once. The name fitted. Arlette put the loaf in front of her, and the knife, and watched her cut a slice and eat it.

'It's Saturday,' Tastet said, to his hat. 'The three of us have talked. Madame Castagnet has asked not to be kissed, and that's in print. You laughed in the minute, Madame le maire, and then you stood. We think the prefect should do the speaking. The mayor would stand at the stone in the sash, and leave it there. It would be put about that she had a throat.'

They braced for it. All three served under that mayor from 2015, and not one of them ever got to the end of a request.

Lesca was already answering. Maylis had the jaw, and held it shut on her until it ached.

'So will you speak, or will you stand?' Tastet said. 'It's for you to say which.'

No one had asked Maylis which, about anything, with three men waiting on her answer.

'Either,' she said.

Tastet looked up from his hat.

'Either,' he said. He took a diary out of his coat, and a pencil, and wrote. 'I have that as consent. The mayor is content to stand. Ten past seven, before two members and her mother.'

'The mayor speaks after the prefect,' said the mouth. It was Lesca's voice, lower than Maylis used it, and it got out between her teeth.

'I have Either,' Tastet said, gently. 'It's written, Madame le maire. A second answer doesn't strike out a first. You taught me that.'

The three of them put their bowls in the sink and were across the yard before anything could be amended.

Arlette shut the door on them and stood with her back against it.

'My daughter has never said that word,' she said. 'Not at four years old, when it was an apple or a pear. She says which. Then she says why. Then she tells you which you ought to have had.' She stayed at the door. 'You sat when I told you. You've eaten my bread without being told twice. I'm not asking who's in my kitchen.'

'It's Maylis.'

'I know what you're called,' Arlette said. 'I'm the last that uses it.'

* * *

The square was dry, with every tree lit. Kévin Labat was at the gratings by the church, in the yellow waterproof, raking towards himself.

'You're pulling the leaf into the slot,' Maylis said. 'Give it here.'

He gave it. She did the four gratings left to right, away from the iron, and banged the rake on the kerb to clear the tines.

'That's my job you're doing,' Kévin said. 'Again.'

'It's mine.'

'It was. You gave it me.' He shrugged and took the rake back. The gratings were cleaner than he ever got them. 'Who showed you that?'

'My father.'

Four men stood in the café window with their cups.

A car came round the square while she stood there. The driver lifted two fingers from the wheel to her and drove on. No window came down, and nobody told her how her father would have raked it.

Madame Ducasse got to her feet when the mayor came in. Nobody got up for the commune's girl. The post lay open on the desk, with the pen beside it. Maylis sat down in the chair with arms, and nobody told her to get out of it. She signed the first letter *M. Lesca*, with her father's tall L. She signed the second and the third. Lesca had the right hand and dragged at each one, so that the L leaned. But Maylis's hand knew that name better than the mayor's knew hers, and it stood up.

Madame Ducasse read the three signatures and squared the letters on the blotter.

'I'll hold these until tomorrow, Madame le maire,' she said. 'In case you'd like to sign them.'

The safe was behind the desk. Maylis had the combination, with the rest of Lesca's fortnight. Inside were the seal, the cash box, a medal in its case, a blue notebook, and a flat box. She left the notebook where it lay. Its last page was hers already. On Monday her own hand wrote it out on the back of a form.

The sash was in the flat box, folded in three. It was blue, white and red, with gold tassels, and it was heavier than it looked on Guy Larrère.

She put it on. It went over the right shoulder and down to the left hip, and tied there. Her left hand made the knot. The right hand came across and undid it.

She tied it again. Lesca pulled it loose before the ends were out of her fingers. The two of them stood in the mayor's office, in one coat, with one hand tying a sash and the other untying it, and Madame Ducasse watched from the doorway with the post held against her chest.

'Shall I do that for you?' Madame Ducasse said.

'Please.'

Madame Ducasse tied it, in a knot that Lesca's hand could not get a nail under, and stood back.

'It's Wednesday, Madame le maire.'

'Yes.'

'I only say so.'

* * *

Maylis went down the steps in the sash and round to the front of the stone.

On her first morning she stood on that spot in the rain, on a painted rectangle, and took ten breaths. Now it was granite, shoulder-high, with frost in the letters. Her own name was the lower line, which she knew. She read the upper one.

JEAN CASTAGNET.

The stone carried no verb. It carried the two names, his over hers, as on the grave of a couple.

In her own village he was at his tonne that morning, alive, on a hand of water. She told Kévin, at the loto, that the other one drove into him. The stone did not say what the other one did. Nobody gave it the word.

The tassels knocked at her hip. In her right arm Lesca went still.

'It's Wednesday,' Maïté Castagnet said.

She stood on the kerb in a black coat, with her handbag held in front of her in both hands, and her eyes were on the sash.

'That's for Saturday.'

'I wanted to see how it sat,' Maylis said.

'And how does it sit?'

'Heavier than on him.'

'Than on whom?'

It was no village in which Guy Larrère wore it. Maylis looked at the widow as she looked at the wife on Sunday, and this time the look fitted.

'You laughed on Monday,' Maïté said. 'In his minute. I asked what there was to laugh at in him, and I said I'd be glad of it. I meant that. I've had nothing to laugh at in him for twelve years.'

Maylis could tell her. The letter was hers, and Lesca only borrowed it.

'He wrote to the mairie that he'd never asked to be pulled out. He said he was waiting for the ground to harden. He sent in a bill for the ruts.'

Maïté's mouth moved. It was nearly there. He would have, and nobody living knew it better.

'When was that?'

'January. In 2017.'

The handbag came up a little, against the black coat.

'He drowned in December 2014,' Maïté Castagnet said. 'Madame le maire.'

She went away across the square. From the café window four men watched the mayor of Peyrebarthe stand at the stone in her sash, on a Wednesday, with one of her hands holding the other by the wrist.

* * *

Madame Ducasse undid the knot. Maylis folded the sash in half and in half again, laid it in its box and left the safe open.

It was close on noon. She sat down in the chair with arms and took up the mayor's pen. She wanted one more thing to sign, and she wanted it a great deal. She knew what was on the stone, and she wanted it with the stone in.

The church clock began to strike. Somewhere the last kernel went onto the last card, and Inguma was on her breastbone, in a dry office, with its leaves against the blotter.

Then it was rain on the tiles, and her own bed. Two fingers lay on the inside of her wrist. Coralie sat on the edge of the bed in her tunic, with the telephone in her other hand. The doctor's number was on the screen, dialled, and her thumb was over it.

'Six hours,' Coralie said. 'With your eyes open. I've not gone in. I've rung the home twice to say I'm not coming, and I don't know what I told them.' She kept the two fingers where they were. 'Is it you? And where have you been, Maylis? Don't tell me upstairs.'
