At eleven Kévin Labat, in the commune's yellow waterproof, pushed a lead into the loudspeaker on the mairie steps, and the anthem came out of it in the middle of a line. He pulled the lead out and pushed it in again, and it began at the beginning. The square stood for one verse. In the front row the men who could not stand took off their caps. Sarthou carried the flag up the steps in its sling and stopped behind the stone.

Lesca stood on the cross of white tape, with the stone at her right knee and the two names level with it.

The prefect spoke first. The young man from Pau stood at the foot of the steps with his clipboard and his watch.

'On the night of the eleventh to the twelfth of December 2014,' the prefect read.

'Here,' said Monsieur Daguerre, from the front row.

The prefect looked up. The young man moved the clipboard a little, and the prefect found his line again. He was in his second year in the department. He said that a river put a question to this commune one night, and that the commune answered. He said that the State rebuilt in 2016 what the river tried, and that the State did not forget its dead. There was one, and one was too many. Then he turned from the microphone and held his open hand towards Lesca, where his sheet told him to.

'One employee of this commune, alone at her post, took in the dark the decision that the hour required. She is today its mayor.'

A television camera stood on a tripod under the first plane tree, and it came round to her with the hand. She kept her face as at eleven of these. The prefect finished a few seconds inside his three minutes, and the young man wrote the time down.

Then she had the microphone.

Six thousand people stood from the stage to the plane trees and on down the road to the Port, where a second loudspeaker gave them each word a moment late. The home had the front row, under its rugs. Coralie stood in the second, in her good coat, with her hands on the handles of a wheelchair. Maïté Castagnet stood beside the wreath, in black, with her bag held in front of her. Père Honoré was at the church door. Lesca's mother stood by the church wall, where she stood each year, side on to the stone. Guy Larrère was at the back, under the fourth tree, with his hat on.

The speech was typed on four sheets. It was the speech of 2016, with the year changed.

'It began to rain on the twenty-fourth of November,' she read, 'and it rained for nineteen days.'

The first sheet was the rain. She gave them the ditches running back up into the yards, and the low meadows under water from the sky alone. She gave them the gauge on the pier of the old gate, read at eight each morning. Her voice went out over the square and came back off the church, and came a third time from the dyke road.

The second sheet was the river. On the night of the eleventh it stood ninety over the red line, and the old bank at the Port was coming through. Behind that bank were forty houses, and a home with forty-two people in their beds. Eight of the forty-two sat a few metres from her knees.

'The gate on the flood meadows was opened,' she read, 'and the river went out over three hundred hectares of grass. By first light it had fallen below the doorsteps of the Port.'

She turned to the third sheet. It was Jean Castagnet's, and it began with two sentences.

'Jean Castagnet was at his tonne that night, as on any night of the season.'

The next sentence was six words long. She read it into that microphone each December. She had given it to his widow across a council table, in answer to another question. It was true, word for word, and it was typed.

She looked up from the sheet.

'I knew that he was,' she said. 'When I got to the gate I put my headlamps down the track, and his van was at the end of it. I knew it for his. I rang his telephone, and I let it ring once. Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him.'

The church gave it back to her. The loudspeaker on the dyke road said drowned him when the square had finished hearing it.

She was on her feet, in daylight, in front of all of them, and she had her knees ready to lock. If Inguma had an hour, it was this one. She waited for the weight on her breastbone and the brown leaf under her chin. Nothing came down on her. There was the wind, and the flag moving in its sling, and she could turn her head.

In the front row Monsieur Daguerre held his cap in his lap. One of the Lassalle sisters took hold of the other's hand. Madame Dufau, who could not have told the year they were in, said the year of the flood aloud to her rug. At the end of the row, nearest the stone, Madame Etcheto's lips went on moving. She was looking at the two names.

Coralie did not look down. On Thursday she learned what stood at the end of the track that night, and said that she would know which Lesca did with it. She knew now. Her hands stayed on the handles of the chair.

Behind Lesca, Monsieur Tastet had his own copy out, with a finger under the second line of the third sheet. He turned the sheet over to see whether it went on.

The square made no sound that she could have put a name to. Out on the lake the dredger was working, as on any Saturday. Then, at the plane trees, people turned to the people behind them, who had been too far off, or talking. She watched her sentence go back through six thousand people at a walk, row by row and on down the road to the Port, with here and there a hand going up to a mouth. Along that road telephones rose over the heads, held out towards a stage their owners could not see. Beyond the last loudspeaker a man began to clap, taking her to have finished. Three or four clapped with him, and stopped.

A child asked what the lady had done, and was told.

The camera stayed on her. The man behind it took his eye from the eyepiece and looked at her over the top of it. Kévin Labat stood by the loudspeaker with the lead in his fist, and looked up at her as he had once looked up at a lens. Sarthou held the flag and kept his eyes to the front.

Père Honoré looked at his shoes. Her mother came out from the church wall, as far as where the lettering could be read.

Under the fourth tree the people nearest Guy Larrère turned to look at him. He had moved four times in council that the stone be given a verb, and they wanted to see him get one. He took his hat off. He was looking at Maïté Castagnet, and she was looking at the wreath.

The fourth sheet was the thanks, to the State, to the services, and to the commune of Hontaube for the loan of its pumps. Lesca squared the four sheets and kept them shut.

The young man from Pau looked at his watch. The mayor had eight minutes, and five were gone. He looked at his sheet. It had a line for a mayor who was prevented, and none for a mayor who stood on her cross of tape with nothing more to read. The prefect looked at the young man.

No one came up the steps. No one shouted. Lesca stood where she had been put and waited to be told what she was now, and the square waited for her to tell it what came next. It was her ceremony. She had drawn up the order of it herself, and the next item was in print.

'Madame Castagnet will lay the wreath,' she said into the microphone.

Maïté came up the four steps with the wreath in both hands and her bag on her wrist. She took her time. She crossed the planks in front of Lesca, bent, and set the wreath against the granite under the names. She drew the ribbon straight, so that it could be read.

The prefecture's photographer came to the foot of the stage with his camera raised. He had taken that picture each year.

Lesca kept to the tape. Maïté stood up, a metre from her, and turned her cheek away before it was asked for.
