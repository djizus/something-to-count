# N8, chapter 7, comic with dread. The first argument, on Saturday night. Joins: after 15c, before the flowers of Sunday.

* * *

That night Maylis lay still under Inguma until she was in the chair, and then she did what the forum said in capitals not to do. She stood up.

The warmth along her back stayed where it was. She took a step towards the panel and turned round.

The mayor of Peyrebarthe sat in the chair. She wore Maylis's T-shirt, and she had Maylis's hands on the steel arms, with the right one shut. She sat a great deal straighter than Maylis did, and nothing was wrong with her side.

'Good,' the mayor said. 'You can get up. That saves a week. I've a number of things to tell you, and I had begun to think I should have to do it through your mouth, which is no way to do business, as I found this morning. Sit down.'

'It's the one chair.'

'Then I'll stand.' She stood. They were of a height, and neither of them liked it. 'Which will you have first? What I've come about, or what happened at six?'

'Either.'

'Don't.' The mayor's hand shut harder. 'Not in here. I gave that word up at thirty-one, and I won't have it said to me by my own face.'

'You had her hand.'

'I had yours. She laid two fingers on it, and I took them.'

'With my mouth. Eleven years. I don't know any eleven years.'

'No. You know sixteen, and you've spent them writing Either on a shopping pad.' She let that go by. 'That's for another night. Listen to me, because this is what I've come over for.'

She gave it in order. In her village a stone stood in front of the mairie, with two names cut in it, Jean Castagnet's over theirs. He had drowned in the cellar of his tonne on the night of the eleventh of December 2014, when the Adour stood ninety centimetres over the red line and the gate was opened to save the Port. His nets had been found afterwards under the duckboards, green, folded wet, with a padlock under the third board. It had rained for nineteen days before that night, from the twenty-fourth of November.

'It's rained here since Tuesday,' Maylis said.

'Since the twenty-fourth. I know it has. Your gauge reads what my file reads, day for day. On Friday the eleventh, at night, your river is ninety over that line, and the old bank is coming through at Sarthou's hedge.' The mayor drew breath. 'And it's mine. I carried that night for twelve years. Inguma kept it on my side of this room, and I came in past it on purpose and let it across. That flood is mine. I'm coming over to stop it.'

Maylis went through it on her fingers, as she priced a job.

'I did the rib on the Monday. It sat on me that night. It rained on the Tuesday. When did you come in on purpose?'

The mayor could have declined to answer. She could not give a wrong day.

'Thursday.'

'Then it had rained two days before you did anything.'

Behind the mayor the two lamps burned, the left and the right.

'Then it's the winch,' the mayor said. 'It stuck, and put you on your back in that bed. They were written to three times. It's in the minutes.'

'It's always in the minutes.'

'I want somebody found to blame.'

'We'd a dry winter here in 2014,' Maylis said. 'I cut verges in December. Nobody was to blame for a dry one.'

She had one more, and it was the short kind.

'There's no word on your stone? Between him and us?'

'The council sat on it three times.'

'What would the word be?'

The mayor declined that. Maylis saw how it was done. She did the same herself, with a shorter word.

The door of the box was on their left, with its rubber seal and no handle. It opened. Inguma came in over the sill with the sound of a sack dragged, crossed the steel floor between them with a back to each, and got up into the empty chair in one movement, as a dog gets up.

Maylis lay on her back under a paper lampshade, with rain on the tiles. She had a date now, and a drowned man, and nobody whose job it was to be told.
