She unlocked the box under the rail and took out the handle. A council had taken note of that winch in 2011, and she meant to see what it had taken note of.

She fitted the handle to the shaft and stood square. The cracked rib let her use the left shoulder and not the right. She turned, counting by the click of the pawl, as she had counted for seven winters.

At the nineteenth the handle stopped.

She got the left shoulder under it and drove with her legs. It gave all at once, and the pawl missed its tooth.

The right hand let go.

She had not told it to. That give had put its owner into the rail a week before, and its owner was not going there twice.

The gate dropped on its chain. The handle ran back. Lesca snatched at it with the hand that had left it, and it went round four times through the fist before she had it stopped.

She opened the hand in the rain. The skin was off in one line, from the base of the forefinger to the heel.

She knew that line. Her father had worn it on that hand, from the winter the pawl let go on him. She had worn it since the night of the flood. Now she had put it on a woman who had turned nothing in her life but what she was told to.

The fingers closed over it, and Lesca had not closed them.

She let the gate down left-handed, a click at a time, and locked the handle in its box.
