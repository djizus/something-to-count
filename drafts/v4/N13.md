# N13, chapter 10, comic. Joins: after 17c as far as the letters, before the stone. It takes the correspondence and "Thank you, Guy" from 08d.

* * *

At half past six Maylis took the chair of the council of Peyrebarthe, since nobody would sit down until she had.

There were fourteen round the table, and a register, and at her right hand a white kitchen timer. Madame Ducasse read the correspondence. The prefecture had written about the twelfth, and the firm at Anglet about the bull.

'And Castagnet's letter,' Maylis said. In her own mairie it had lain on top of the tray for a week. 'Has that had an answer?'

Madame Ducasse held her pen where it was.

'Not since 2014, Madame le maire.'

Inside the jaw Lesca said something short.

'On that correspondence,' Guy Larrère said, and got to his feet. He had a black ledger under his hand. 'I'll be brief.'

Every head at the table turned to the timer.

No member of that council spoke for more than three minutes, and the chair set the bell. Maylis knew the rule. It had come with the coat. The right hand, which was Lesca's that evening as well as hers, went to the timer and took hold of the dial.

Maylis pinned the wrist with her left.

She could not do it. For eighteen years that man had talked down at her through the top of a car window, about her father, with the engine running, and she had stood in the rain with a rake until he was done. Nobody had ever told her that he could be stopped. The right hand got the dial round a centimetre. The left turned it back.

So he spoke.

He gave them Jean Castagnet's twelve letters, by number, and the replies he had signed to eleven of them. He gave them the association's care of the gates since 1962. He opened the ledger at a ribbon and read out the manoeuvres of 1978 and of 1996, and came to a line that said order of the president, by telephone, keep shut, with nothing written under it. He said that a gate was a door in a bank. At four minutes he glanced at the timer, and went on with more confidence. At six he said hydraulic and finished the sentence it was in, which nobody at that table had heard him do. Monsieur Tastet looked at the chair. Madame Ducasse looked at the chair's two hands, one of which was sitting on the other.

At nine minutes he began to slow. He had been saving it since 2015, and there was less of it than he had thought. He said for the second time that a gate was a door in a bank. He turned a page of the ledger and turned it back.

At eleven he stopped. He stood with his mouth a little open and looked at the white timer, which had not moved. Then he looked at her.

'That's all,' he said, and sat down. He did not seem to know what came next.

'Thank you,' Maylis said. It was what she said at the car window when the glass went up.

'Guy,' the mouth added, lower.

He looked round the table to see who had heard it.

'I ask that that be minuted. The eleven minutes, and the thanks. I never thought to see either.'

'In what words?' said Madame Ducasse.

'In hers.'

Madame Ducasse wrote that the chair had thanked Monsieur Larrère. Monsieur Tastet wrote something in a diary of his own.
