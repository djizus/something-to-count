That evening Coralie put a plate in front of Lesca and stayed on her feet.

'On Wednesday I told Madame le maire to get up, from the doorway,' she said, 'and she got up. I told her she'd eat before she crossed the yard, and she ate. No motion. No amendment. In eleven years I haven't had that.' She set the bread down. 'I'd keep that one in the house. You can tell her so, wherever it is you tell her things. Madame le maire is to finish what's on the plate.'

Lesca finished it. She did it to show that she could, and Coralie was not taken in.

The oven was behind Coralie, with a cloth over its rail. The last time she had opened that door a finger's width and bent to it had been in January 2015. She had done the prefect's office on the telephone, asking through the gap whether Madame Lesca owned a plain coat. She had done it well. Lesca had sat at this table with a tea towel round her hand, a month after the gate, and had not been able to laugh. Coralie had shut the oven on him. In February she had carried her pillow across the landing, and she had done nobody's voice in that kitchen since.

'Eat,' Coralie said.
