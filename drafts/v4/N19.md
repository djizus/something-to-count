At nine minutes to three on Tuesday Lesca was on her feet beside the chair when the other one stood up out of it and turned round.

'You laughed in his minute,' Lesca said. 'With my mouth, at the head of my table, and his widow went down the stairs. It's in the register as a fact of the sitting, by six votes to one. I have stood for that minute each December and it has not once had a noise in it.'

'He's alive where I am. He sent a bill.'

'For what?'

'Ruts. I towed him out, and he billed the commune for the ruts.'

Lesca had that laugh herself, behind the nose, and shut it off.

'He goes down on Friday because you told him not to,' the other one said. 'With my mouth, in his own café. He'd sat in for five nights.'

'Yes. I forbade it on the thirtieth, in front of a full table, and he said why he was going as he went. That one is mine. I can date it.'

'I want your side. The desk, the timer, people getting up when I come in. I've read the stone, both lines. I want it with the stone in.'

'You've no idea what you'd be taking.'

'Then tell me. What's in the stone?'

'You've had the date and the sum.'

'That's what's on it.'

Lesca did not answer. In that room she could not have got a lie as far as her teeth, and she kept them shut on the rest.

The other one waited, with her weight on the left foot. Then her eyes went past Lesca's shoulder.

They had not turned round, and Inguma was in the chair. Neither had heard the door. It filled the seat from arm to arm, and cold came off the plate.

Behind the other one the steel wall had gone. Waxed boards ran out under strip lights to three rows of tables, with a head bent over a card at each place, and a voice calling numbers. One chair stood empty, at the near end.

The other one had seen it. She looked at that chair as at a bench in the shade.

Then Lesca had a lampshade over her, and dry tiles.
