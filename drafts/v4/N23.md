She was not in her bed.

She stood barefoot on waxed boards, under strip lights. Behind her the steel room stood open on its fourth side. Inguma sat in the chair in there with its back to her, and nobody was on it.

In front of her were the tables. They ran in three rows under the lights and on past where the back wall of the village hall should have been, and she could see no end to them. At each place lay a card and a saucer of maize kernels. At each place somebody sat with their head down, and each of them was a little more than one, like a photograph of a person who had moved. The hall smelled of floor wax and of the urn.

One chair stood empty, at the near end of the third row, with a card laid at its place.

'Lesca, Maylis,' the voice called from the far end, as it gave the numbers, whole and then in parts. 'Lesca. Maylis.'

It was reading her off the card. No head came up. They had their kernels ready.

She saw what the chair was. Nothing came to that hall at nine minutes to three. It had no stone in it, and no typed sheet with a sentence on it that was true word for word. A woman who sat down there was nobody's to fetch. She would have the quiet for good, with a card in front of her, and no morning to get up to.

Her knees had begun to bend for it.

'The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies,' she said, in the voice she kept for the prefecture. 'She has a commemoration at eleven and a third sheet to put right, and she will not be taking the place.'

'Lesca. Maylis.'

'It can go in the minutes as apologies received.'

She walked. A hall with no walls had no door, so she took the aisle between the second row and the third. She passed Marie-Jo's coat, twice over. She passed two women who were Coralie, one in a tunic and one with keys beside her saucer, and neither lifted her head. She passed a man in two jackets with a card in each hand, and there was one of him and no more. She stopped at none of them. The rows went on.

Her mother sat where the second row should have ended. She was seventy, in her coat, and in the next chair she was thirty, and both were pushing kernels onto a card with one finger and saying names. They were the ponies' names. Lesca stood behind the two chairs and heard them as far as the old mare.

The eleventh kernel went down, and both women sat back.

A long way behind her there was the sound of a sack let down off a trailer, and then the dry noise on the boards, coming. She kept her face to the rows. It was on her breastbone where she stood, with the leaf under her chin, and the strip lights went out from the edges.

Behind her, in the hall, a chair took somebody's weight.
