The prefect did not come back to the microphone. He went down the steps at the side of the stage, where the trestles were bare, and his driver had the door of the grey car open before he reached it. At eleven he had shaken her hand, and he left without it.

The young man from Pau was left on the planks with his clipboard. He turned a sheet over and turned it back. Then he came as far as the cross of white tape and stopped with his shoes short of it.

'I've nothing on the sheet for this,' he said. 'I'm asked to ask. Whether Monsieur le préfet might have the case back, until things are clearer.'

She wore the medal one day in the year, on the lapel of her good coat, since the prefecture liked it on camera. The case was in her pocket.

'It was given by decree, and it isn't taken back by clipboard,' Lesca said. 'He'll need a decree to keep it. He can hold it until he has one. You can tell him the mayor said so, and that she'll have a receipt.'

'I've no form for a receipt.'

'Then write one. Received from the mayor of Peyrebarthe, one medal in its case, with the date and your name.'

He wrote it on the back of his plan of the chairs. She unpinned the medal, laid it in the case and held the case out to him over the tape, and he gave her the plan with his other hand.

A lieutenant of gendarmes had come up the steps behind him, with her cap under her arm. She had been sent that morning for the traffic.

'Madame Lesca. You've made a statement before witnesses concerning a death. Bayonne will take it from you on Monday at nine. Bring the telephone you had that night, if it exists.'

'It's in a drawer. I'll be there at a quarter to.'

'Nine,' the lieutenant said. She looked at the wreath, and at the two names over it. 'You'll stay in the department.'

'I've a commune to run.'

'Until Tuesday,' said Monsieur Tastet.

He had waited at the back of the stage with his hat in his hands. He put it on now, in front of her, and held out a sheet folded in three. He was looking at her face.

'Seven names. Mine's the first. For an extraordinary sitting on Tuesday at six, on one item, and the item is the mayor. It's in writing, with the item stated and the signatures under it. You told me a thing asked by word of mouth hasn't been asked.'

She read it. It was correct. In April 2015 seven had got up from that table over Guy Larrère, and she had been voted into the room they emptied.

'You've left out the place.'

'The council room.'

'Then write it in. I can't sign a summons to nowhere.'

She gave him her pen. He wrote it in against the granite, and she signed under the seven, with the stroke that came back and crossed itself.

The square was going away down the road to the Port and its bar, more quietly than in other years, and it looked back as it went. The camera was being folded. Under the fourth tree there was nobody.

Marie-Jo Labat came up the four steps. She had stood in the fourth row. Since the night of the carrier bag she had spoken to the mayor at sittings, and to the wall.

'Maylis,' she said.

Lesca waited for what came with it.

'There are two hundred chairs in this square. I counted them out of the hall at seven, and Kévin's on the bank with the loudspeakers. Somebody has to stack them.' She looked at the sash. 'You can't do it in that.'

Lesca took the sash off. She folded it in three along the white and put it in her coat pocket, on top of the receipt. Then she went down the steps and began at the church end, six chairs to a stack.

The home was loading. Nadine brought the wheelchairs past her to the minibus one at a time, and Madame Etcheto's was the last of the first lot. The old woman put her hand on the wheel and stopped it.

'It never came last night,' she said. 'I said them, and I lay there for it till it was light. Where was it?'
