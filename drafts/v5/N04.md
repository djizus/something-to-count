At two Lesca took her father's watch out of the drawer and drove down to the old gate.

The gate had a motor on it now, in a green box. The dyke road ran on past it along the shore of the gravel lake, which lay where the low meadows had been. A dredger worked where her mother's ponies had stood. The track out to the tonnes had been the company's haul road since the lease, and it kept to the old line.

She set the watch at the gate and walked. She held herself to the pace of a woman with a cracked rib and a torch, in rain, on ruts. Past halfway the old track had dipped, and the company had laid a culvert under the place.

A man in a hard hat came down from the weighbridge to meet her, with a board under his arm.

'Mairie?' he said. 'Then tell him he's had my tickets. Every load since we started digging, by email. And tell him I don't open a weighbridge for a clerk with a stopwatch.'

'Tell whom?'

'Your mayor. Monsieur Lesca. I've had him on the phone twice.'

'You had her. She wanted a figure, and she has it.'

He looked at the watch and at her good coat, and wrote something on the board.

'So what's she timing?'

'A walk. There was a duck hide at the end of this road.'

'There's a lake at the end of this road.'

She went on to it. The haul road stopped at a rail, with a lifebelt on a post. Out past the rail the dredger swung on its chain over the place. She stopped the watch.

A kilometre had taken twenty-five minutes. For twelve years she had reckoned that figure, and had said it to one priest. Now she had walked it.

By her file the river over there had stood a hand under the line that morning. In four days a bank would have ten minutes left in it. A woman who set out from that gate on foot would be standing here, at a hatch, when the bank went.

She would have liked not to have a twelfth of December at all.

On the night she would have to drive it.
