Before six on Saturday Inguma set Lesca down in the steel room, where the two women shared one chair and, once on their feet, could see each other and could not lie. Then it went out by the door.

It went over the sill with its dry noise, and the door shut behind it on the rubber seal. The chair stood empty. The wall on the right was steel again, riveted, from the floor up.

Maylis stood against that wall. Her waterproof was dark to the ribs and ran onto the floor, and silt lay in the turn of the cuffs.

Lesca, for once, waited to be spoken to.

'My hand was on the gate key,' Maylis said. 'You said the tonne first, with my mouth, and I went. So here's what it came to. The cellar was dry. He had a stove lit down there, and I pulled him out by the collar. The bank held sixteen minutes. You gave it ten, and so did I. I'd have been back at the handle in time, but my own van was sat across his track, in the dip, where he'd told me it would be. Forty-one came out of the home. Madame Etcheto didn't.'

Lesca had gone over to stop a drowning, and she had moved it from a cellar to a bed. She was ashamed, and she had no minute to put it in.

'I had your arm,' she said. 'I let go of it to say those words. They were mine.'

'I said both. That was mine. So was the van.' Maylis wiped her face with a wet sleeve. 'So you were right, that night. Turned first, it keeps them all. The forty houses, the home, the one in room twelve. I've stood in that room now. I'd sign your sum.'

'No.'

'It's your own sum.'

'I was right about the houses. I can show you that on a file, to the centimetre, and I've shown it to a council each December since. I rang once. I had his van in my headlamps and his number in my hand, and I let it ring once and put the telephone in my pocket. No bank made me do that. Don't hand me the houses for it. I was handed a medal for the houses.'

Maylis weighed that as she would have weighed a bale, with both hands.

'Once,' she said. 'I let it go to nine. He answered on the ninth.'

Lesca had nothing to set against that, and set nothing.

Behind the steel a voice called a number, whole and then in its parts.

'Twelve. One, two.'

A chair was pulled in to a table.

They both knew whose it was. Neither of them looked at the chair they had fought over, and neither sat. The door stayed shut, and Inguma did not come back.
