Coralie came in at nine, to a kitchen where Maylis had the kettle on. She had been forty hours in her tunic, and it had dried on her twice.

'Larrère came down to the Port on foot,' Maylis said. 'His car's in the silt at the crossroads. He's dated my letter, and Sarthou wants him at the brigade at two.'

Coralie looked at the oven. She went to it and opened the door the width of two fingers, and bent to the gap.

'I'll be brief,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the neck. 'The—'

She stayed bent for a moment, with her hand on the door. Then she shut it and sat down at the kitchen table in her coat. The shopping pad lay on the table, with a week of empty boxes on it, and under them the one she had drawn for Friday night.

'Room twelve,' she said. 'I'll say what it is to me, and then I won't again. I've stood at that door at lights-out since I started there, while she said her words to the wardrobe, and her care plan had me down as not to join in. On Friday at ten she wouldn't sleep in a chair, and I walked her back to it on my arm. At four I shut her eyes. I'm not telling you so that you'll have less of it. You had the key.'

'I had the key.'

'Castagnet's told the Port where you went with it.' Coralie laid both hands flat on the oilcloth. 'Last Saturday somebody stood on that landing in your coat and asked me back across. She answered whatever I put to her. I told her what I wanted, which I'd told nobody, and she said take it. I'd have had her. I said so to her face. Then I sent her down the stairs and shut my door, since it was your house, and you were in that hand and couldn't get a word out. I chose you, Maylis, over somebody who asked.'

'I couldn't get the mouth.'

'I know you couldn't. I had two fingers on you. You had it back by breakfast.'

'I asked on Friday. At the back door.'

'You did, and I said seven. At seven I was in a hall counting heads, and you were in it with me, and nobody was in the house.' Her voice held. 'And before seven I'd asked you one thing, down a telephone, with the water at my back door. The gate, or Castagnet. You said both. I know that word. I've cooked it. That was Either, with the river in it.'

Maylis had no shorter word for it than hers.

Coralie took an envelope out of her tunic pocket, slit along the top, and laid it beside the pad.

'Bayonne's written. The geriatric floor, on nights. I start in January.'

'Stay.'

It was out before Maylis had weighed it.

'Stay is what you say to keep a thing as it is,' Coralie said. 'I didn't ask what you'd keep. I've asked what you want, and that's another question, and you know it is.' She turned the envelope round on the oilcloth. 'I can answer mine. I want that floor. I've wanted it since March, when they told us the home would shut. A floor and a night of my own to run, where I say who's turned and who's left to sleep. I filled it in and showed it to nobody, and I posted it for me.'

'It's twenty minutes. You could go in from here.'

'Don't give me the measurement. I could. I'm not going to.'

She went up. Maylis followed her as far as the bedroom door, and found that she had brought the pad.

Coralie packed one bag, on the bed, out of the chest of drawers. Three tunics went in, and the good coat, folded, and the clock she woke to. Maylis stood in the doorway holding the pad in both hands, and priced the bag as it filled. It came to less than a morning's load for the tip.

'I've asked you one question for sixteen years and drawn you boxes for it,' Coralie said, with her back turned. 'You've a week of them in your hands. I'll ask it once more, with no box. What do you want, Maylis?'

'She's to be buried here on Tuesday. I want to dig it myself.'

Coralie shut the bag.

'Then you'll dig it. With that side. I'll strap it first.'

She carried the bag down and stood it by the front door. In the kitchen she fetched the box she kept from the home and strapped the rib where Maylis sat, three turns of the wide bandage, pulled tight. She tucked the end in, and her hand stayed on it a little longer than a bandage needs.

Then she went up. Maylis heard her cross the landing to the box room and come out of it. She heard her on each stair coming down, all eleven, slower than she had gone up. She heard the passage, and the front door, and the car door shut on a pillow.
