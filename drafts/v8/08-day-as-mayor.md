The square was dry, and every tree in it was lit. Kévin Labat was at the gratings by the church in the commune's yellow waterproof, raking towards himself.

'You're pulling the leaf into the slot,' Maylis said. 'Give it here.'

He gave it. She did the four gratings in Lesca's good coat, left to right, away from the iron, and banged the rake on the kerb to clear the tines.

'That's my job you're doing, Madame le maire.'

'It's mine.'

'It was. You gave it to me.' He took the rake back and studied the gratings, which were cleaner than he got them. 'Who showed you that?'

'My father.'

Along the café window the cups had stopped halfway. A car came round the square, and its driver lifted two fingers off the wheel to her. No window came down.

In the mairie Madame Ducasse got to her feet when Maylis came in. At home nobody got up for the commune's girl. The day's post lay open on the desk, and the mayor's pen beside it. Maylis sat down in the chair with arms and signed the first letter *M. Lesca*, with her father's tall L. Lesca was in the right hand and dragged at the pen, so that the L leaned. Maylis had made that L since school. She set it upright on the second letter, and on the third.

Madame Ducasse read the three signatures and squared the letters on the blotter.

'I'll hold these until tomorrow, Madame le maire. In case you'd like to sign them.'

Maylis asked her for the flood service at the prefecture. At home that number was a recording. Here a young man answered at the second ring, and his voice changed when he heard which mairie was calling.

'Madame le maire. What can we do for you?'

'A hand gate on flood meadows, and the river coming up. Who can have it raised by day, before the river's over the line?'

'That's your own rule, Madame le maire. We've had it since your flood. The president of the landowners orders it, in writing. Or the prefect does, and his order goes down through the mayor.'

'And the keyholder?'

'She turns it. She doesn't decide it. That was the point of the rule.'

Maylis put the receiver down. Over here the president and the mayor were two people. At home they were one man in a car, and he kept a letter of hers in a folder.

Madame Ducasse fetched a small case from the safe and laid it open on the blotter, on a yellow cloth. A medal lay in it, on a ribbon of blue, white and red.

Maylis was looking past the medal, at the telephone that was answered at the second ring.

'I want that,' she said.

'You have it, Madame le maire,' said Madame Ducasse, with her eyes on the medal. 'It's in the inventory.'
