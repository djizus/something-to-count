At the interval the hall came to the bar and queued three deep along the trestle.

Lesca had the urn, the left hand and the mouth. Maylis had the right hand, and Maylis knew what a beer cost. So Lesca drew each order and said something with it, and Maylis took the money and gave the change, and for ten minutes the bar of the loto was better run than anyone in the hall could remember.

Père Honoré asked for a glass of water.

'I have nine on a good Sunday,' he said. 'This is the most I have had under one roof this year, and the roof does not leak.'

'They've come for a ham, Father,' Lesca said, and Maylis slid him his water before she could go on to the roof.

Jeannot Castagnet came with his cards under his arm, so that nobody could read them. Lesca had watched him since the first number, his ears and his hands and the back of his neck where the collar rubbed. Maylis set his beer down with the right hand and left the hand flat on the trestle between them.

'You have your letter back, and your complaint,' he said. 'What more is there on me to see?'

'Nothing,' Maylis said, before Lesca had the mouth open.

'You're breathing,' Lesca said after her, with the same mouth, a little lower.

'I'm what?'

'I said it was him or the wife,' the owner's wife told the queue. 'It's him.'

Old Lafitte, behind Jeannot, turned the score pad to the side headed *Madame le maire* and wrote.

Marie-Jo came round the trestle for the bar tin. She let nobody else count it. She tipped it out and built the coins into piles in front of the queue.

'You'd be on the council now, you know,' she said, counting. 'If you'd answered me in 2020. Third on my list, against Larrère. The lists closed at six, and I was in the sub-prefecture at ten to, with a gap at number three. I'd rung you at four and at five. We lost by thirty-eight votes.'

'I'd have stood,' Lesca said.

She said it in Maylis's voice, across the coins, and the queue heard it. Marie-Jo stopped with a pile in her hand.

'I'd have said yes before you had the book shut. Third on the list, and the key handed in the same week. Half the Port votes for whoever keeps its gate. You'd be mayor, and I'd have your accounts in order by now.'

'You were on the mower. I could hear it from the hall.'

'She was.'

'Who was?' said Lafitte.

Maylis got the mouth, and it was hers for three words.

'I was thinking,' she said.

'That's two answers,' Marie-Jo said. 'I waited six years for one.' She tipped the pile into the tin uncounted, which nobody in that hall had seen her do.

'Then it wants reading,' Lafitte said. He held the pad off at arm's length, for the queue. 'Tonight. Has not taken her eyes off Castagnet since the first number. Told him he was breathing. Would have gone on a list against Larrère, and says so with him sat in the hall.'

Coralie had come up the hall with her tray. She waited at the end of the queue until he had read it.

'Coffees for my table,' she said. 'Four with nothing in.' Her eyes went to Jeannot, who was studying his cards, and to the pad, and to the face behind the urn. 'Marie-Jo rang me last Sunday about a pot of flowers. I didn't ask you. Am I to ask now, with the pad out?'

'No,' Lesca said. 'There's nothing in it for you to mind. I watch that man because I can't believe my luck in him. The rest is for the house. I have one thing to say to you about Friday the eleventh, and another about that landing.'

'Can't believe her luck in him,' Lafitte said, writing.

Lesca filled the cups with the left hand. Maylis set them on the tray with the right, the four with nothing in and then the rest, and got one word past her.

'Coffees,' Maylis said, in her own voice, which was the higher.

Coralie's eyes went from the right hand to the mouth.

'I'll have the rest at home,' she said, 'from whichever of you is in.'
