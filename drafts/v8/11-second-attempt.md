Old Lafitte tore a leaf off the score pad and wrote on the back of it at the card table, while the café watched over his shoulder. *The Port asks that the gate be raised on Friday the eleventh, by day, on the ebb.* He signed it. Madame Darrigrand signed it, with the baby on her arm. Sarthou read it twice and signed as the last house in the Port. He said that the brigade had not seen it.

They carried it up to the mairie before it shut, and Maylis went up behind them. Lafitte laid it on the counter, card scores down.

Madame Ducasse turned the leaf over, and turned it back.

'It's received,' she said. 'I've no column for which side.'

Guy Larrère came out for his post and read it where it lay.

'It's the association's gate,' he said. 'The association will view it. Tomorrow at nine.'

On Wednesday the same six men gathered on the dyke at the same winch, and the president presided through the top of his car window. The river lay along the red line. Jeannot Castagnet had walked up from his van to hear.

'The keyholder will say what she knows,' Larrère said.

'It comes through at the patch on Friday night. At two that afternoon the tide's on the ebb, and there's nobody on the meadows. Raise it then.'

'On what information?'

She had it from a woman who had turned that winch in the dark, in a village she could show to none of them.

'None I can give.'

'The keyholder has no information,' Larrère said, and the member who kept the book wrote that down. 'Then I put it. Above the line, the gate stays shut. Those in favour.'

Six hands came out from under the umbrellas. A seventh went up behind them, in the sleeve of a wader jacket.

'You've no vote, Castagnet,' the oldest member said.

'I've a tonne out there, and a hand of water on it. Shut keeps the river out of my cellar. Count me or don't.'

'The hunters are heard,' Larrère said.

The book had it as carried. Jeannot's hand was the last one down, and he was the one man who would be out on those meadows on Friday night.
