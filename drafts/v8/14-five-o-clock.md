Maylis got out. She unlocked the platform with the key on her belt, and after it the box under the rail. The handle lay in the box on a rag. She lifted it out with her left hand and fitted it on the shaft, and it went home with a knock.

A hundred metres back along the crown a car sat with its engine running and its window up. She knew the car.

She put her weight on the handle. The chain came up out of the water a link at a time until it was taut, and the pawl dropped into its first tooth with a click. The gate had not moved yet. It needed eighteen turns more, and her shoulder for the last of them, and it would be up in daylight, on the ebb, while the one man who went out on those meadows was on the dyke behind her.

A van door shut.

Jeannot Castagnet came up the steps onto the planks in his waders.

'That's my tonne you'd be filling,' he said. 'I waited a fortnight for a hand of water on it, and you'd put the river in the cellar to the hatch. You were on this dyke on Wednesday. You heard it voted, and my hand was up.'

'It comes through tonight.'

'So you say. He says shut.' He tipped his chin along the crown at the car. 'I've not been on that man's side of anything in my life, and I'm on it today, so I'll know what I'm beside. It's your hand on it. Which is it to be? Shut, or open?'

The river went by under the planks.

'Either,' Maylis said.

He waited for the rest of it, and there was none.

'Then it's shut,' Jeannot said. 'That's how it was left.'

She lifted the handle off the shaft. The pawl let go of its one tooth, and the chain went slack into the water. She laid the handle on its rag and locked the box.

The car on the crown turned round in three goes and drove up towards the terrace. Its lamps left the road at the last four houses and went in behind them, by the back lane.

Jeannot got into his van, and she went to his window.
