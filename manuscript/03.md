# 3

Lesca was lying on her back in a bed that wasn't hers, listening to the rain on the roof. The room could have been her own. The paper lampshade: hers. The top of the wardrobe: hers. The clock radio, 06:00 in red: hers. The rain: not hers. No rain had fallen on her own roof for two weeks. Pillows: two. At home she had one. On the back of the door hung a yellow raincoat with COMMUNE DE PEYREBARTHE across its shoulders. She had handed that coat in with her keys when she became mayor.

She breathed in, and a rib caught on the right side. There was nothing wrong with her own ribs.

A minute ago she had been asleep in a steel chair, with somebody warm sitting in it beside her. Then an alarm had rung a long way off. The warmth had got up to go. Lesca had stayed sitting.

Coralie was lying between her and the wall, warm from the bed, with her hair flat on one side. She reached across Lesca to stop the alarm. At home that alarm rang on the other side of the landing.

Coralie sat up, lifted the right wrist off the blanket, put two fingers on the inside of it and watched the clock radio.

'Your pulse is 130,' she said. 'You were lying there with your eyes open again. What is it this time?'

An answer went past Lesca on its way to the mouth. It was about the rib, and it wasn't hers. Maylis was in here too, and she was about to say it.

Lesca got in first. She told the left hand to move and it moved. She brought it across and closed it over Coralie's fingers.

They were warm, and rough at the tips from the soap at work. Under Coralie's fingers the right wrist began to pull away. Lesca wasn't the one pulling. Maylis was. Lesca held on with the left hand while the pull went up the right arm and into the cracked rib.

'Maylis?' Coralie said.

Lesca told the mouth to open. It was stiff, but it was hers to use.

'Come back across. I've left you on that landing eleven years.'

The voice came out low.

Coralie kept her fingers where they were, on the pulse.

'Across what?' she said. 'I'm here. I'm in the bed.' She was looking at the mouth. 'And you don't ask. You've never once asked me for anything. I'd have come for half that sentence, from the far end of the Port. Where did you get eleven?'

She bent down and looked into one eye, then into the other. Then she took her fingers back. She had to pull to get them.

'Who is that?'

She got out of the bed and stood beside it, holding her fingers in her other hand.

Lesca opened the mouth to tell her, but the jaw was pulled out of her hold. Maylis had thrown everything she had at it.

'The rib,' the mouth said, in a voice higher than Lesca's.

Two words were all Maylis could get out before Lesca had the jaw back. By then the jaw was no use to Lesca. The room had gone cold. The thing was on the chest with its back to her, just as it had been on her own chest every night for twelve years. It took her home.

She was in her own bed, under a dry roof. Clock radio: 06:20. Pillows: one. Coralie was coming around the bed in her dressing gown with the phone in her hand. The thing got down.

'Twenty minutes,' this Coralie said. 'Lie still. Your eyes were open and I couldn't wake you, so I've rung your mother. Give me that wrist, Madame le maire.'

She took it between a finger and thumb and watched the clock. Lesca let her have it.

'It's 90, and coming down.' She laid the wrist back on the blanket. In this village she ran the care home in the Port.

She was at the door when Lesca said her name.

'Madame le maire?'

Lesca had the sentence ready. This was the woman it was for.

'Nothing. Go back to bed.'

The spare room was four steps away, across the landing. Coralie had carried her pillow over there in the February after the flood, and the pillow had stayed. The door shut.

Lesca was lying in the middle of the bed with her left hand closed, as if those fingers were still in it.

In June 2014 the woman from the university had asked her whether, somewhere, she also saw the other lamp. Somewhere was another Peyrebarthe.

So she could cross.

* * *

There was a white frost on the square that morning. Lesca left her car by the church and crossed on foot. She had no parking space, because the one in front of the town hall door had been given to the stone.

The stone faced the square, so most days she walked past the back of it. That Saturday she went around to the front. The top line was JEAN CASTAGNET, who had been Jean in the town hall's files and Jeannot at the café. Under him, in the same capitals, was MAYLIS LESCA. Under that was the date. There was frost in the bottom of each letter.

He had drowned on the night of the flood. In his whole life he had sent the town hall twelve letters. But something had come home with her from that bed, the way mud comes home on a boot. She remembered signing for a registered letter from him on Tuesday, in the rain, with a rake in her other hand. It was his forty-first. Lesca hadn't held a rake since she handed in the coat.

Over there he was alive and still writing.

She didn't trust it. She had wanted him alive for twelve years, and she had nothing to check it against.

She stood in front of the top line with her keys in her hand and read his name through to the last letter before she went in.

In the council room the committee for the twelfth commemoration of the flood had got as far as Larrère, who had asked to speak. Marie-Jo Labat turned a white kitchen timer to three and stood it in front of him. Any member could speak for three minutes on any item. Lesca had made that rule in her first month as mayor. It was for Larrère, who had been mayor before her.

Larrère used his three minutes to explain floodgates to Lesca across the table.

'A gate is a door in a bank, Madame le maire. It is not a tap. You don't turn it on because the sink's full.'

The timer rang while he was in the middle of the word hydraulic. He finished the word and laid a hand flat on the table.

Lesca answered him, as she did at that table every year.

'A door in a bank is opened from the platform, Guy, and I was the one on the platform. The bank had ten minutes left. Behind it were forty houses, and the care home, with forty-two people in their beds. Out on the fields there was one man, at his duck hut, and no one came to tell him. I opened it. Behind the bank, nobody even woke up.'

It took her half a minute. Nobody at that table had ever asked her a question about it. Maïté Castagnet, Jean's widow, held her handbag in front of her with both hands and looked at the radiator.

The last item was the stone. Marie-Jo stood the timer in front of Larrère again.

'I propose that the stone be given a verb,' he said. 'It has a man's name and a woman's name and nothing between them. A stranger reads it as a married couple. I propose Saved.'

'Saved who?' Marie-Jo said. She hadn't spoken to Lesca directly since 2018, so she said it to the wall above Lesca's head. 'Castagnet, Saved, Lesca. It reads as if he was saved, by her.'

'Opened.'

'Opened what? Castagnet, Opened. It reads as if he was opened.'

'I have a verb,' Maïté said.

Every head turned. At that table she had never said anything except that she abstained. She opened her mouth to say the verb.

The timer rang.

'That's the member's time,' Marie-Jo said, to the wall. 'It goes to the vote.'

Larrère's verb lost the vote, as it had every time before. Lesca voted against it. Her left hand went up still closed. She had to open it to be counted.

Maïté waited until the others had their coats on.

'Did you know he was out?' she said.

'No one came to tell him,' Lesca said.

'Yes. You said— At the table, you said that.' Maïté did up the top button of her coat. 'There's a tile gone over the back bedroom. Right over the wardrobe.'

'I'll come at 2, while there's light.'

* * *

The Castagnet house stood on the edge of the high ground above the Port. Lesca had looked after its roof since the January after the flood, though no council vote had ever asked her to.

That afternoon Maïté stood in her black coat and held the bottom of the ladder while Lesca set the tile back in its row and cleared the leaves out of the gutter along the roof. She was nearly down again when Maïté spoke.

'It was raining by now, that year. It started in the night, and I had his rubber boots drying by the stove every evening after that.'

'On the 24th. It's in the file.'

Lesca stopped on the ladder. It was white with frost under her hands. Peyrebarthe had been dry for two weeks. But that morning she had been in a bed with rain falling on the roof. And on Tuesday, over there, the ink had run on the card she signed.

Tuesday had been the 24th.

She came down the last steps, laid the ladder along the wall, drove to the town hall and let herself in. She took the flood file for 2014 down from the cupboard and opened it on her desk at the pages where the height of the river was written down.

She had been the keyholder that winter. Every day she had gone down to the old gate, where her father had painted a red line on the pillar at the height of the doorsteps in the Port. She had read the river against that line and written down what she saw, one line a day. Now she read those lines with a finger under each.

*24 November 2014. Under the line. Rain since the night.*

*25 November 2014. Under the line. Rain.*

*26 November 2014. Under the line. Rain.*

*27 November 2014. Under the line. Rain.*

*28 November 2014. Under the line. Rain.*

Today was the 28th.

Ever since she stood at the stone that morning, she had taken the other village for the lucky one. Its river had stayed behind its bank in 2014, so its Castagnet had lived. But the rain over there had started in the night before the 24th, exactly as it had in her file. Four lines later it was still falling.

That village hadn't had its flood yet.

She turned the page and went on down the dates. On 8 December 2014 the river had reached the line. Three lines later she came to the last one in her own handwriting.

*11 December 2014, 22:00. Over the line. Rising.*

Under that the file was typed, by the gendarmes.

*First light. CASTAGNET Jean recovered from the cellar of his duck hut. Trapdoor locked on the inside. Watch stopped at 03:11.*

This year the 11th fell on a Friday, thirteen days away. That night the river over there would be at the top of the bank, with Castagnet out at his hut. Larrère would ring Maylis, as he had rung Lesca, and tell her to keep the gate shut. Maylis had the key, but only Lesca had the date.

She couldn't get Jean Castagnet out of his cellar. But over there he was still above ground, and she wanted to keep him there.

She took a sheet of the town hall's paper from the drawer. When something had to be decided, the only form she had for it was a motion, the kind the council voted on. So she wrote the heading for one. That morning she had stayed sitting, and she had woken up over there with a mouth. A mouth could tell that village what was coming, during office hours.

She wrote the motion under the heading.

*That the mayor remain seated.*

It didn't say for how long. That morning the thing had come for her after twenty minutes. She wanted a day.

* * *

When it got dark Lesca crossed the yard to her mother's kitchen and asked her question before she sat down.

'The thing that sits on a sleeper. What did your mother give it to count, and how long did that keep it off her?'

Arlette was at the stove. 'My mother had words for everything. She had some for the stove, and a whole set for the priest that she only used after he'd gone.' She turned the potatoes. 'For Inguma she had the grass on the village field. She gave it that, blade by blade, and it was counting till daylight. Madame Etcheto, down in the Port, gives it the stars. Coralie tells me the old woman rings for the night staff at 3. Stars, in December, under cloud. It needs more than that. A great many things, and small ones.'

'I want a day. A whole one, and a Monday. On a Sunday the town hall is shut, and there'd be nobody to tell.'

Arlette put the pan down.

'Nobody gives it that much just to sleep, Maylis.'

Her mother had never called her anything else.

* * *

When Lesca came in, Coralie had the soup on and two places laid at the kitchen table.

'Madame le maire will sit down,' she said, 'and eat it while it's hot.'

'I'll have it here. I've got the minutes of this morning's meeting to read.'

'It's soup. It doesn't need a vote.'

Lesca ate it standing at the stove, with the minutes open beside the pan.

At 10 they went up. At the top of the stairs there were two bedroom doors, with the landing between them.

'Good night, Madame le maire,' Coralie said, and shut hers.

Lesca left her own door open and got into the middle of the bed. From there she could see the light under Coralie's door. That morning she had held Coralie's fingers, in Maylis's hand. Before that she hadn't held them since a February eleven years ago.

When the light went out she got up, went down to the kitchen and unfolded the sheet of paper on the table.

*That the mayor remain seated.*

A motion is supposed to say what it is for. This one kept that back. She had sent Larrère's motions back down the table for less.

She changed the full stop to a comma and went on in the same ink to the end of the line.

*That the mayor remain seated, and take the other one's bed, and Coralie Mounet in it.*
