# 13

Lesca was in the chair in the steel room, with Maylis beside her. Maylis got up first, and Lesca stood up after her.

Both lamps on the panel were lit. The wall on Lesca's right was gone again. Where it had been she could see the lit hall and its rows of tables. One chair in the hall was empty, with a card laid in front of it, and the name on the card was hers.

Maylis faced her in the yellow raincoat, with rain on its shoulders and mud up to the ankles of her boots. Her right forearm was pressed across her side. The palm of her right hand was bandaged.

Lesca opened her own right hand and held it out between them. The scar ran across the palm, white, from the base of the forefinger to the bottom of the palm.

Maylis looked at it for a long time.

'You rang once,' Maylis said.

It was Lesca's own voice. It sounded the way the town hall's answering machine played it back to her.

Lesca had answers. For twelve years she had given them to the prefect, to her council and to Maïté. Every one of them was true. She closed her hand and didn't give any of them.

'I asked you not to come,' Maylis said. 'You came, and you brought the river with you. But my bank's still standing. If I get you out of me now, it may hold. You're not staying.'

'I've come to stay,' Lesca said.

Far off, the phone had rung until it stopped. Now it started again, and Maylis turned her head towards the sound.

Lesca sat back down in the chair.

The phone woke Maylis in the van, behind the steering wheel, and Lesca woke there with her.

The van was parked on top of the bank, facing the floodgate. Rain was running down the windscreen. Ahead, under the lamp on the platform, the handle was in place, with the chain pulled tight. The clock on the dashboard said 02:52. The phone was ringing in the left hand.

One ring.

The right hand moved before anything else did. It was Maylis's. It took hold of the steering wheel at the top.

Lesca had the rest: the legs, the left hand and the mouth. She dropped the phone in the lap, reached across with the left hand and turned the key. The engine started. She put the lights on.

Two.

Lesca put the van in first gear with the left hand, got the hand back on the wheel and let the van roll along the top of the bank. The lamp and the handle went by the side window. Then they were behind her.

Three.

Where the track began Lesca turned the wheel. The right hand pulled it the other way, with the cracked rib behind it, and lost. The headlights swung out across the flood fields and down the wheel tracks. At the far end a round reflector shone red.

Four.

Lesca kept the foot down. The right hand still had the top of the wheel and was hanging its whole weight on it, so she drove against the weight.

Five.

The right hand let go of the wheel and covered the eyes. Lesca kept the foot down, blind. She took the right wrist in the left hand and dragged it off the face. By then the front of the van had swung away from the track, towards the drop, so she let go of the wrist to catch the wheel.

That freed the right hand. It pulled the handbrake up as far as it would go.

The engine struggled and died. The van stopped where the track left the bank, with its front wheels on the slope. The stop threw the body forward onto the steering wheel, and the horn sounded once across the fields.

Six.

'Let go of it,' Lesca said, with the mouth. 'I left a man at the end of that track. I can get this one out. I know where the dip is. Let me drive.'

She went for the handbrake with the left hand. The right hand was locked on it, cut palm and all. She pulled at its fingers. The hand on the brake was the weaker, and it had got there first.

Maylis took the jaw from her for three words.

'You're the water.'

Seven.

On Sunday night, standing in the steel room, Lesca had tried to call it the tide, and the word hadn't come. She had the jaw back now and nothing to put in it. Maylis took it again.

'Inguma, enauk hire beldur.'

Lesca waited for Either. It didn't come.

'You'll count the fingers of my right hand,' Maylis said. 'Then you'll take her home.'

There were five of them, locked on the brake. It was a short count.

Something heavy landed on the roof of the van. The metal bent in and sprang back. Then Inguma was inside. Its leaves went over the knuckles of the right hand on the brake, one at a time. After the fifth it was on the chest, with its back to her and its leaves against the windscreen.

Eight.

Lesca knew what came next. First the weight, then her own bed, with one pillow on it and a dry roof over it.

She wasn't going. She got the left hand onto the steering wheel, under the leaves, and locked the arm. For twelve years she had gone wherever that weight took her. The cold went through the raincoat and into the cracked rib, and the arm began to shake. She kept it where it was.

She knew what that cost. She had seen the card with her name on it.

Rain was loud on the roof of the van. She had crossed for that sound, and for a bed with two pillows. She held on to the wheel for the whole of that ring.

Then she saw what she was holding. It was the wheel of a van with a dead engine and its brake on. The hand on the brake was the hand that had held the stair post against her. It wasn't going to open while she was in this body, and she couldn't open it with one hand of her own. The clock on the dashboard said 02:53. At the end of the headlights the reflector was red, and it was getting no nearer.

She could stay sitting in this van until the river came over it. As long as she held the wheel, nobody was going down that track at all.

'I'm letting go,' Lesca said, with the mouth.

She used it for five more words and heard them come out in Maylis's voice.

'Go to the hut first.'

Then there was no steering wheel, and no rain.

* * *

Lesca was standing barefoot on a waxed wooden floor, under strip lights. Inguma hadn't taken her home. She had held on one ring too long for that. It had put her down where her card was.

Behind her was the steel room, open on its fourth side. The chair in it was empty.

In front of her the tables ran in three rows under the lights, out past the place where the back wall of the village hall should have been and on further than she could see. At every place there was a bingo card and a small dish of corn, and one of the village's dead sat over them with their head down. Nearest were the ones she had known. After them came the ones her father had buried, and after those the ones nobody living had ever met. The hall smelled of floor wax and of the coffee pot. On the edge of the stage there was a duck in white paper, with its feet sticking out.

'Line,' said a woman in the first row. She put her hand up.

The voice at the far end read her numbers back to her, each one whole and then in parts. She was one short. She took her hand down.

One chair was empty, with a card laid in front of it.

'Lesca, Maylis,' the voice called. 'Lesca. Maylis.'

Nobody else looked up. They had their grains of corn ready.

The place was in the second row. The chair next to it was taken. Jean Castagnet sat there in two jackets, with a card in each hand. On the other side a woman lifted her handbag off the empty chair and put it on her knees.

Lesca walked over to him and stopped at his shoulder. He didn't look up from his numbers.

'I saw your van,' she said. 'I rang once.'

He put a grain of corn on a card.

She waited for him to look up. He put a grain on his second card.

'Lesca. Maylis.'

Her hand was on the back of the empty chair. Her knees bent and she sat down.

The voice called a number, whole and then in parts. It was on her card, in the top row. She had a grain of corn between her finger and thumb and was holding it over the square. She put it back in the dish.

'The mayor of Peyrebarthe sends her apologies,' she said, in the voice she used for the prefecture. 'She has a ceremony at 11, and she will not be taking this seat.'

'Lesca. Maylis.'

'You can put that in the minutes as apologies received.'

She tried to stand, but her hand stayed on the seat of the chair. The fingers had locked on the wood. She opened them with the other hand.

Then she stood up straight and walked. A hall with no walls had no door, so she took the aisle between the second row and the third. A man in the third row had his arm around his card so that his neighbour couldn't copy it. Her father was sitting at the end of the second row, with his watch on the paper tablecloth beside his card. He didn't look up from his numbers. She didn't stop. Behind her the voice went on calling her name.

A long way behind her the dry sound started on the wooden floor. She kept her face turned towards the rows. Then Inguma was on her chest, where she stood, with its leaves under her chin. The strip lights went out, starting from the edges.
