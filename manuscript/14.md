# 14

It was 2:53 on Saturday morning. Maylis had her body back. She also had five words in her mouth that she hadn't put there.

'Go to the hut first.'

The voice had been her own. Lesca had gone out of her the way warmth goes out of a chair, and those words were what she had left behind. Maylis's left hand was open on the steering wheel. The engine had died. The phone was ringing in her lap. Eight rings so far.

Her right hand was still locked on the handbrake, though nobody was driving her now.

Maylis took her hand off the brake.

The screen said *Castagnet, house*.

She answered.

'You're at the gate,' Larrère said. 'Good. Pau says the river is ninety centimetres over the line and rising. The gate stays shut. You'll stand by it and touch nothing until I ring again. That's an order from the president.'

Behind his voice she could hear a clock ticking, close to the phone, the kind that hangs in a kitchen.

'Castagnet's in his duck hut,' she said.

'He's in his hut, yes. He stays dry as long as you do what you're told.'

He hung up.

She started the engine, backed up onto the bank and drove back past the gate, towards the Port. Two hundred metres beyond the gate, where the road came up, she got out with the torch.

The patch had opened. Water was running down the face of the bank in a sheet as wide as a door. It crossed the road, went over Sarthou's sandbags and on through his hedge. When she put her hand flat on the clay beside the water, the clay moved under her palm.

Lesca was gone. The water kept coming.

Maylis had built that patch herself in April, one wheelbarrow at a time. At 2:55 she gave it ten more minutes.

Jeannot had told her not to bring the van down, and at dusk she had waded through the dip herself. She took the van anyway. In the van she could still have both, Jeannot and the gate. She worked it out as a job. Three minutes to get there. Two to get him up the ladder, if he climbed. Three back. That made eight, and then the handle, with her shoulder under it at the nineteenth turn. Every job on that bank had taken her longer than the time she gave it.

She rang Coralie as she drove.

'The bank's going at the patch. You've got ten minutes. Start moving them now, and don't wait for a siren.'

'Where are you?' Coralie said.

'On the bank. Jeannot's in his hut, with the trapdoor locked. If I open the gate the river's on him in four minutes.'

'Then which do you want?' Behind Coralie somebody was calling a name. 'The gate, or Castagnet? Say which, Maylis. For once.'

'Both.'

The line stayed open, but Coralie said nothing.

'Start at the bank end,' Maylis said, and dropped the phone on the seat.

The lamp over the platform came up beside her. Under it was the handle, already fitted, with the chain pulled tight. She let the van slow down until the handle was level with her eyes. For three breaths she kept her foot off the pedal.

She drove past.

The van went down off the bank onto the track. The phone on the seat said 02:57. She drove in second gear through wheel tracks full of water, and the rib hurt every time the steering wheel pulled. Rain blew into the headlights. At the far end of their light was the red reflector.

Six hundred metres along, the track dipped. The front of the van went down and stayed down, and the back wheels dug in. The van sat on its belly across the wheel tracks and wouldn't move.

The phone on the seat began to ring again. The screen said *Castagnet, house*.

She got out into water that came over her boots. The gate was six hundred metres behind her. The red reflector was four hundred metres in front. The bank had six minutes left.

She could be at the handle inside those six minutes, and she knew what they would buy. The river would go out over the fields and stop coming through the patch. The bank end of the care home would stay dry. She had just sent Coralie to the bank end.

She also knew what an open gate did four minutes later at the other end of this track. Down there Jeannot was sitting on a dry floor, under a trapdoor he had locked himself. He wouldn't hear the water until it was over him.

The lamp over the gate was behind her, small and white in the rain. She took a step back towards it. Then a second, and a third.

Under that lamp was the bank. Under the bank was Coralie, who had said 7, and told her to be in the house. Coralie, who had asked her to say which, for once.

If she turned that handle, Jeannot drowned and her hand had done it. If she went on, whatever the river did behind her was the river's doing.

She wanted it not to be her.

Maylis turned her back on the lamp and went on.

She hadn't run since she cracked the rib, and it let her have a third of each breath. Mud to the ankle at every step. The torch in her left hand, her right arm pressed across her side. She counted her steps. The reflector grew in front of her until, at four hundred steps, it was the size of a plate. She passed Jeannot's van. Then her legs found the low roof of the hut.

The trapdoor was shut. She beat on it with the end of the torch.

The trapdoor lifted. A lamp came up under it, then Jeannot's face. Behind his shoulder she could see a concrete room, with a stove burning and a flask on a shelf. The floor was dry.

'I told you not to knock,' he said.

'Come up. I'm opening the gate.'

'You're what?'

'The bank's going at the Port,' Maylis said. 'I'm opening the gate. You've got four minutes after I do, and then this is under water.'

'It's shut. Larrère told you shut. Look at that floor. There isn't a centimetre of water on it. I've got three hundred ducks sitting out there, and you come down here in the middle of the night like your father never—'

She grabbed the collars of both his jackets in her left hand and pulled. He was fifty-eight and heavy. He had the ladder under his boots and didn't use it to help her. Something gave in her side. She heard it go. He came up over the edge of the trapdoor onto his knees in the mud, swearing, and she didn't let go until he was standing.

'Little Lesca,' Jeannot said. 'You've gone mad.'

'Drive.'

He looked at her in the light of his own lamp. Then he drove.

His van went back along the track faster than she had come down it. He knew every wheel track. At the dip his headlights picked up the front of the village van. It was sunk across the track from edge to edge, with its door hanging open.

He stopped a metre short of it.

'I told you,' he said. 'Not in that van.'

There was no way around it, with the fields under water on both sides. She was out of his van before he had the handbrake on. She took her phone off the seat of the village van as she went past.

Six hundred metres to go. She ran. He came after her and fell behind. Ahead of her was the lamp over the platform, with the handle under it.

She was two hundred metres short of the bank when it broke, at 3:11.

It sounded like a truck tipping out a load of gravel a long way off, except that the sound didn't stop. The street lamps of the Port went out one after another, starting at the bank end.

Maylis had given the bank ten minutes, and it had held for sixteen.

A siren started up at Sarthou's house, the kind you turn by hand.

She climbed onto the top of the bank and pointed the torch at the pillar. Eighty centimetres over the line, and falling as she watched. The river was going down by itself. It was going out through a gap as wide as a house, into the Port.

The handle was still on, where she had left it at 11. She hadn't turned it one centimetre. And Coralie was down there, under the gap.

'Open it now,' Jeannot said behind her. He was out of breath. 'It'll take the weight off.'

She was already running along the top of the bank, towards the lights that were left.

After twenty metres she turned around, unhooked the key from her belt and threw it back to him.

Jeannot caught it against his chest with both hands.

'That's for the padlock,' she shouted. 'The handle's on. It sticks at nineteen. Get your shoulder under it.'

She ran. The torch showed her the gravel one step at a time. The river was on her left, close and loud. On her right, down below, there were no street lamps any more. The only light came from further along. It was low down, white and moving.

After two hundred metres the top of the bank ended, at the place where the road had come up from the Port. The water went through the gap in a smooth brown slope and broke at the bottom on what was left of Sarthou's hedge.

She went down the land side of the bank on her heels, through somebody's rows of beans, into the water. It came up above her knees.

It was colder than the rain. It was moving away from the gap and down the street, pulling at the backs of her knees. Torch up. Right arm across her side. Whatever had given in her side at the trapdoor now let her have a quarter of a breath at a time, so she breathed fast.

Sarthou's front door stood open. The river went in through the door and came out through the kitchen window, carrying a chair. The sandbags were gone from his step. He had laid them properly. One of them rolled against her leg in the road.

Sarthou himself was in the street in his cape, up to his waist in water, coming up from the lower houses. He was holding a child above his head, wrapped in a blanket. It was the Darrigrands' baby, screaming.

'Number thirty-eight is out,' he said. 'They are on the church road with the other child. I have done every door down to thirty-one.' He was out of breath too. 'Bordenave is at his upstairs window. He says that he is dry up there and that he is staying.'

'The care home.'

'The water went in at that end first. I saw it go.' He lifted the child higher. '3:11. I looked at my watch. That is for the file.'

He turned and went back down the street into the dark, with the baby over his head, shouting the number of the next house.

The care home was the last building before the gap. Its long side faced the street and its short end stood under the bank. That end was dark. The water from the gap was breaking against it as high as the windowsills. The last room at that end was room twelve. At 10 Coralie had walked Madame Etcheto back to it.

At the other end the front yard was lit up. Two vehicles had their headlights pointed at the glass doors.

Maylis walked towards the yard through the water, counting. A rubbish bin floated past on its side. Something soft touched her knee and was gone. After sixty steps the road dipped and the water was at her hips. After a hundred she had the gatepost of the yard in her left hand.

The minibus was parked with its nose to the doors and water over its step. Beside it Kévin had driven his van in, opened the rear doors and put two mattresses on the floor. His two blue cool boxes stood on the pavement at the top of the yard, on the high side, where the water hadn't reached. The pump was still running off its battery.

Kévin was at the back of the van in his leather jacket, in water above his knees. He was carrying a man in his arms the way you carry a child. He put him down on a mattress, beside a woman who was already lying there, and turned around for the next one.

'How many have gone up?' Maylis said.

He shrugged. 'I've done one trip. Five people. Bixente's done one.' He nodded at the minibus. 'Ask Nadine. I only lift them.'

Inside the glass doors the corridor ran back the whole length of the building. The water in it was halfway to the knees. Wheelchairs stood along one side, each with its strip of tape. In more than half of them somebody was sitting with a blanket around their shoulders and their bare feet up on the footrests, out of the water. They were quiet. They were looking at the headlights.

Nadine was in the doorway with a torch in her fist. Around her neck, on its cord, hung Coralie's list. She read a name off it and took hold of the wheelchair that went with the name.

'Madame Hirigoyen. Frame.'

'Where's Coralie?' Maylis said.

'Down there.' Nadine pointed the torch along the corridor without turning her head. 'You told her the bank end. She hung this on me and went. She sent nine, ten and eleven up the ramp to me in their chairs, and she went back down for twelve. Then the lights went out.'

'And she hasn't come up.'

'I've shouted. You can't hear anything from down there, only water.' Nadine swung the next chair around by one handle and left it in front of Maylis. 'She said the left side, front to back, and they go in the van or the minibus, whichever has room. She said not to stop for anything. She said it twice.'

She went off through the water with Madame Hirigoyen.

Maylis had the handle of the chair in her left hand. Monsieur Daguerre was sitting in it in his pyjamas, with his cap on and both hands on the arms.

'Here,' he said.

'I know you are.'

Beyond the last wheelchair the fire door stood open. The old wing behind it was dark. Coralie was somewhere behind that door. Maylis wanted to let go of the chair and run to it. But between her and the door sat a row of people who couldn't walk, and she had one of them in her hand.

She pulled him out backwards over the doorstep. The chair dropped into the yard and the water came up to the middle of the wheels. With only one arm to push with, she put her left hand on the left handle and her hip against the back of the seat. The chair went sideways. It kept pulling to the right, and every time she straightened it she lost her quarter of a breath and had to find it again. The headlights were in her eyes. It was fourteen metres to the back of the van. She counted them.

Kévin stood in the water at the back of the van and bent over the chair. He got one arm under the old man's knees and one behind his back, lifted him and put him in the van. The cap stayed on.

She took the empty chair back through the water and stood it against the wall. At the end of the corridor the fire door was still open. Nobody had come through it. She took the next one.

The next one was a woman she knew by sight from bingo night, who had marked her card with her tongue between her teeth. After her came one of the Lassalle sisters, who wouldn't let go of her sister's hand, so Nadine brought the second chair up beside the first and the two went across the yard side by side, with Nadine on one and Maylis on the other. Then there was a man who weighed nothing.

Each time she came back in, she looked down the corridor before she looked for a chair. Once she shouted Coralie's name down it. The people in the blankets turned their heads towards the fire door with her. When nobody came through it, they turned back to the headlights.

The next man asked her at every metre whether it was Thursday.

'It's Saturday.'

'Then they'll want the bins out,' he said.

She was back at the doorstep with his empty chair when Coralie came up through the fire door.

She came up the ramp on the rail, hand over hand, with no torch. She was wet to the collar and had lost a shoe. At the top she stopped, looked along the corridor at the chairs that still had people in them, and counted them.

Maylis's hand had opened. The empty chair went over on its side in the water.

Coralie walked up the corridor past the row. She took the torch out of Nadine's fist, lifted the list from around Nadine's neck and hung it around her own. Then she saw Maylis on the doorstep. She looked at Maylis's side, at the arm held across it, at the water running out of her raincoat. Coralie's mouth opened and shut again.

'Castagnet?'

'He's at the gate,' Maylis said. 'He's out. He was dry.'

'Don't tell me what he was.'

'Twelve,' Maylis said.

'It won't open. The water's pushing on the door from inside the room. I had my shoulder to it, and then my back, and then the torch went and I couldn't—' Coralie looked down at the names. 'I've got a corridor full of people I can reach, and one I can't. Pick that chair up.'

'Did she answer you? Through the door?'

Coralie read out the next name.

Maylis stood the chair on its wheels and went in for the next one.

Bixente was filling the minibus from its other door. It had twelve seats, but he didn't stop at twelve. When it was full he shut the door and sounded the horn once. Coralie drew a line through a block of names with the pencil that hung from the list.

'Seven minutes up the hill, in the dry,' he called.

'Don't give me the minutes. Go.'

The minibus backed out of the yard, pushing a wave in front of it, and turned up the hill. Its headlights left the glass doors.

Maylis leaned against the wall inside the doors. Her legs had stopped telling her where the floor was. When she took the phone out of her chest pocket to see the time, the card from the steel room came out with it. She pushed the card back in. The screen was wet and she had to read it twice. It said 03:31 both times.

The water in the corridor was up to her knees now. And the old wing was lower still, at the bottom of its ramp.

'It's still coming up,' she said.

'I can see where it is,' Coralie said. 'I made a mark on the door frame.' She didn't look at the mark. 'Next. Monsieur Hourcade. Carried.'

There was nobody to carry him. Kévin was at the van and Nadine had a chair.

Jeannot came in from the street, walking through water that broke against his chest. He had no lamp. He came through the yard into the headlights with his arms out for balance and his mouth open.

He reached the doorstep and held on to the door.

'It's up,' he said. 'Your gate. I counted the clicks, the way you do, and at nineteen it stopped. I got my shoulder under it and it went, and I've done something to my knee.' He got his breath back. '3:20, it went up. The river's on my hut now, and it's still coming in here.'

He held the key out to her on his palm.

She looked at it. Until tonight it had never been off her belt.

'I put that handle in letter thirty,' Jeannot said.

'I wrote to them too.'

'I know you did. They took note.' He pushed his palm at her. 'It's no use to anybody now. Have it back.'

She hooked it on her belt.

'There's a man in there who has to be carried,' she said. 'Hourcade. Halfway down, on the left.'

Jeannot looked down the corridor at the row of faces in the light. He looked for two breaths.

'I was at school with his son,' he said.

He went in. When he came out he had Monsieur Hourcade on his back, with the old man's arms around his neck and his own hands locked under the thin knees. He carried him across the yard in his rubber boots.

They kept going. Maylis pushed the chairs and Jeannot took the ones who couldn't sit. Nadine brought them up from the back, Coralie read out the names and Kévin lifted.

When the van had its five, Kévin shut the doors on them and drove out of the yard. While he was gone the only light in the corridor was Coralie's torch, lying on a windowsill. The chairs waited in a row in the dark, with the water moving past their footrests. Maylis stood with her hand on the back of one. The woman in it had taken hold of her sleeve and didn't let go.

Nobody spoke. Out in the street something big floated by, knocked against the wall and went on. No sound came from the dark end of the corridor.

Then Kévin's headlights came down the hill again and across the glass.

He drove up to the doorstep and got out. He took the next one from Jeannot and the one after from Maylis. There were seven chairs left with people in them, and two mattresses. He looked at the seven, then at the floor of the van.

'They'll have to sit up against each other,' Kévin said. 'It's that or another trip.'

'There isn't another trip,' Coralie said.

At 4 in the morning he was lifting the fifth when the phone went off in his shirt pocket. It didn't ring. It buzzed twice against his chest.

Kévin stood in the water with a woman's wheelchair half up on the back of his van. He put it down level. Then he held his left hand out in front of him, in the light his headlights threw back off the glass, with the fingers spread. He touched each finger with the forefinger of his right hand.

Maylis watched him do it. Her own hand was on a chair.

'Five,' he said.

He looked at the hand a moment longer. Then he put it under the woman's knees and lifted her in.

* * *

By 4:10 the corridor was empty from the front doors to the fire door, except for one chair. Past the fire door a ramp went down into the old wing, which ran on to the end of the building, under the bank. Its lights had gone out with the street lamps.

Coralie stood at the top of the ramp with the list in her hand. Every name on it had a line through it except one. She put the torch between her teeth and took hold of the rail.

Maylis had Madame Poey's chair by its handle. It was the last chair.

Kévin came in over the doorstep. He looked at Coralie going down the ramp, then at Maylis, and took the chair from her without asking.

'I've got her,' he said. 'Go on.'

Maylis went down the ramp after the torch.

At the bottom the water came up to her waist, then to her ribs. It pressed against the place that had given in her side and held it, and for the first time since the trapdoor she got half a breath. The water in here was black and still. It smelled of the river and of drains. Ahead of her Coralie's torch moved along the wall at the height of the door handles, from one room number to the next.

Nine was open. The bed had been stripped down to the mattress, which had floated off the frame and was now lying across the room. By the window a walking frame stood in the water with a dressing gown hung over it.

Ten was open. It had two beds, pushed together. The blankets hung down into the water from both.

Eleven was open. Over the bed stood the machine they used for lifting a resident out of it. Its straps hung down, turning a little.

Maylis walked with her left hand on the wall and her boots full of water. She had stood in this corridor once before, with Coralie, outside the last door on the left.

The torch had stopped.

Twelve was shut. The number was on the door in metal. Under it was a card in a holder, with a name written in Coralie's capital letters.

Coralie took the torch out of her mouth and put her shoulder to the door. This time it opened. The water on the other side was level with the water on this side.

She went in. Maylis went as far as the door frame and held on to it.

The window was gone. The whole of it had come in, frame and glass, and was now under the water. Through the hole Maylis could see the foot of the bank, a metre away, with clay running down it. The curtain had been carried across the room and wrapped around the leg of the chair. On the chair hung a coat, buttoned up and wet to the collar.

The bed had been wound up as high as it went. The water was level with the mattress.

It had been higher. It had left a line on the wallpaper a metre above the pillow. It had left mud on the shelf, and on the photographs on the shelf.

Madame Etcheto was in the bed. She was on her back with her hands outside the blanket, as she always was when she said her words. Her handbag was under one hand. Her eyes were open. There was mud at the corner of her mouth.

Coralie laid the torch on the pillow. She took the nearer wrist, put two fingers on the inside of it and looked at her watch. She held it for the full half minute.

Then she laid the hand back on the blanket and closed the eyes with her thumb.

She took the pencil on its cord and drew a line through the last name on the list.

The phone in Maylis's pocket said 04:16. She stood in the doorway with the river up to her ribs and looked at the top of the wardrobe. There was a hatbox on it. Nothing else.

* * *

In the village hall the strip lights were on. Somebody had set out chairs along the folding tables in three rows and switched on the coffee pot.

The old people sat in their blankets with their heads down while Coralie went along the rows with the list. She touched each one on the shoulder, said a number, looked at the list and went on. Maylis stood by the door in her wet clothes, with her arm across her side. Jeannot stood next to her in his rubber boots, dripping onto the waxed floor.

'Thirty-nine,' Coralie said. 'Forty.'

She came to the end of the third row. A man sat in the second chair from the end. She touched him.

The last chair was empty.

'Forty-one,' Coralie said.
