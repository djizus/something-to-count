# 15

Inguma didn't put Lesca down. It held her in the dark with its leaves under her chin. Behind her, in the hall of the dead, the voice went on calling her name.

'Lesca, Maylis. Lesca. Maylis.'

Then the voice called a number, first whole and then in parts.

'Twelve. One, two.'

Somebody pulled a chair in to a table.

It was nearly 6 on Saturday morning when Inguma put her down.

It had taken her home, to her own body. But the body wasn't in her bed. It was on a stretcher against the wall of a corridor, in the hospital in Bayonne. The strip lights above her now were the hospital's. The two fingers on the inside of her wrist belonged to her own Coralie, the one who slept across the landing.

'I came in at 2:55,' Coralie said. 'Your eyes were open, so I rang, the way I said I would. Bayonne has had you for two hours and found nothing. I could have told them that.' She kept her fingers where they were. 'Lie still, Madame le maire. They'll bring a form that says you're leaving against advice, and you'll sign it. I've got the car. You're due in the square at 11.'

Lesca signed it lying down. They drove home with the heater on.

'I rang your mother from the corridor,' Coralie said at the wheel. 'She's had her table laid since 7.'

They reached the village by 9.

* * *

Lesca stood on the cross of white tape with the sash over her good coat. The stone was beside her. The two names on it were level with her right knee.

Below her the care home had the front row. Coralie had brought the residents up in two groups, put their chairs on her strips of tape and given each of them a blanket. Madame Etcheto sat at the end nearest the stone with her handbag on her knees. Her lips were moving. Behind the front row the square was full as far as the church wall, and people were still coming up the road from the bank. It was more people than had ever voted for Lesca.

Sarthou stood at the bottom of the steps in his uniform, holding the flag upright in its shoulder strap. In this village he had retired, and this was the one day a year he came back to the square.

At 11 Kévin, in the village's yellow raincoat, pushed a cable into the loudspeaker on the town hall steps. The national anthem came out in the middle of a line. He pulled the cable out and pushed it in again, and this time the anthem started from the beginning. The square stood for one verse. In the front row the men who couldn't stand took off their caps. Then Sarthou carried the flag up the steps and stopped behind the stone.

The prefect spoke first. The young man from Pau waited at the bottom of the steps with his clipboard and his watch.

'On the night of 11 to 12 December 2014,' the prefect read.

'Here,' said Monsieur Daguerre, from the front row.

The prefect looked up, then found his line again. It was his second year in the job. He had brought last year's speech. He said that one night a river had asked this village a question, and the village had answered. He said that the State had rebuilt what the river had tried to break, and that the State did not forget its dead. There had been one death, and one was too many. Then, at the place where his sheet told him to, he turned from the microphone and held out his open hand towards Lesca.

'One employee of this village, alone at her post, took in the dark the decision that the hour required. Today she is its mayor.'

A television camera stood on its stand under the first plane tree. When the hand turned to her, the camera did too. She kept her face the way she had kept it at all of these ceremonies. The prefect finished inside his time.

Then the microphone was hers.

The crowd stretched from the stage to the plane trees and on down the road to the Port, where a second loudspeaker gave them each word a moment late. Coralie was in the second row in her good coat, with her hands on the handles of a wheelchair. Maïté stood beside the flowers, in black, holding her bag in front of her. At the foot of the steps, a metre from the stone, stood Larrère, with his association's book under his arm. He kept his hat on.

The speech was typed on four sheets. She had written it in 2015.

'It began to rain on 24 November,' she read, 'and it rained for eighteen days.'

The first sheet was about the rain. She told them about the drains running backwards into the yards. She told them about the fields, under water that had all come from the sky, and about the red line on the pillar of the old gate, which she had read at 8 every morning. Her voice went out over the square, came back off the church and came a third time from the road to the bank.

The second sheet was about the river. On the night of the 11th it stood ninety centimetres over the red line. Water was coming through the old bank at the Port. Behind that bank were forty houses and a care home with forty-two people in their beds. Eight of the forty-two were sitting a few metres from her knees.

'The gate on the flood fields was opened,' she read, 'and the river went out over three hundred hectares of grass. By first light it had fallen below the doorsteps of the Port.'

She turned to the third sheet, which was about Jean Castagnet. It began with two sentences.

'Jean Castagnet was at his duck hut that night, as he was on any night of the season.'

The next sentence was six words long. *No one came to tell him.* She had read it into that microphone every December. She had said it to Maïté across a council table, when Maïté had asked her a different question. Every word of it was true, and it was typed.

She looked up from the sheet.

'I knew he was at his hut,' she said. 'When I got to the gate I pointed my headlights down the track, and his van was at the end of it. I knew it was his. I rang his phone, and I let it ring once. Then I opened the gate, and I drowned him.'

The church gave it back to her. The loudspeaker on the bank road said 'drowned him' after the square had finished hearing it.

She was on her feet, in daylight, in front of all of them. She got her knees ready to lock. If Inguma was ever going to come for her by day, it would come now. She waited for the weight on her chest and the brown leaves under her chin, but nothing came down on her. There was the wind. The flag moved on its pole. She could turn her head.

In the front row Monsieur Daguerre held his cap in his lap. One of the Lassalle sisters took hold of the other's hand. Madame Poey, who didn't know what year it was, said the year of the flood out loud to her blanket. At the end of the row, nearest the stone, Madame Etcheto looked at the two names while her lips went on moving.

Coralie didn't look down. Her hands stayed on the handles of the wheelchair.

Lesca turned her head. Behind her the deputy mayor had his own copy of the speech out, with a finger under the second line of the third sheet. He turned the sheet over to see whether there was more on the back.

Nobody in the square made a sound. Out on the lake the gravel company's machine was working. Then, at the plane trees, people turned to the people behind them, who had been too far away to hear or had been talking. She watched her sentence travel back through the square and down the road, row by row, at walking pace. Here and there a hand went up to a mouth. Along the road phones rose above the heads, held out towards a stage their owners couldn't see. Beyond the last loudspeaker a man thought she had finished and began to clap. Three or four people clapped with him, then stopped.

A child asked what the lady had done. Somebody told the child.

The camera stayed on her, but the man behind it took his eye away and looked at her over the top of it. Kévin was by the loudspeaker with the cable in his fist. Sarthou held the flag and kept his eyes to the front.

The priest looked at his shoes. Her mother left the church wall and walked forward until she was close enough to read the letters on the stone.

At the foot of the steps Larrère had his book open in his hands. He shut it. When the people nearest him turned to look at him, he took his hat off. He was looking at Maïté. She was looking at the flowers.

The fourth sheet was the thanks: to the State, to the emergency services and to the village of Hontaube for lending its pumps. Lesca put the four sheets back together and left the fourth unread.

The young man from Pau looked at his watch. The mayor still had time left.

Nobody came up the steps. Nobody shouted. Lesca stood where she had been put. The square was waiting for her to tell it what came next. It was her ceremony. She had written the order of it herself, and the next item was printed on the programme.

'Madame Castagnet will lay the flowers,' she said into the microphone.

Maïté came up the steps with the flowers in both hands and her bag on her wrist. She didn't hurry. She crossed the stage in front of Lesca, bent down and set the flowers against the granite, under the names. Then she pulled the ribbon straight so that it could be read.

The prefect's photographer came to the front of the stage with his camera raised.

Lesca stayed on the tape. Maïté stood up, a metre from her, and turned her cheek away before anyone could ask for it.

The prefect didn't come back to the microphone. He went down the steps at the side of the stage, where his driver had the door of the grey car open before he reached it. At 11 he had shaken Lesca's hand. He left without shaking it again.

That left the young man from Pau on the stage with his clipboard. He turned a sheet over and turned it back. Then he walked up to the cross of white tape and stopped with his shoes just short of it.

'I've got nothing on the sheet for this,' he said. 'I've been asked to ask. Whether the prefect might have the medal back, until things are clearer.'

She wore the medal one day a year, pinned to her good coat, because the prefect's office liked it on camera. Its case was in her pocket. She unpinned the medal, laid it in the case and held the case out to him across the tape. He took it. She let him go without a word.

The crowd was leaving, down the road to the Port and its bar. People looked back as they went. The cameraman was folding up the stand.

Her mother hadn't gone home. She was waiting below the stone with her bag on her arm.

'Jean Castagnet shut the gates of my field behind him,' Arlette said. 'He was the only one on those fields who did. They'll be in my kitchen tomorrow morning with this, the whole of the Port. I've listened to them about the lake for you, and about the four houses, over my own coffee. I'm not listening to this one. They can take it up your steps.'

'Yes.'

'And I'm not asking you why,' her mother said. 'I don't want to be told.'

She went home around the back of the church, the way that didn't pass the stone. After that there was nobody at the foot of the steps.

Marie-Jo came up onto the stage. Since the night of the plastic bag she had spoken to Lesca only in council meetings, and then only to the wall.

'Maylis,' she said.

Lesca waited for the rest.

'There are two hundred chairs in this square. I counted them out of the hall at 7. Kévin's down on the bank with the loudspeakers, in the coat you gave him when he came out of prison. Somebody has to stack the chairs.'
