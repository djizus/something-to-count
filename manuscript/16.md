# 16

Lesca was still stacking chairs in the square when the care home came out of the hall after its lunch. Nadine pushed the wheelchairs past her to the minibus one at a time. As Madame Etcheto's went by, the old woman put her hand on the wheel and stopped it.

'Inguma never came last night,' she said. 'I said my words, and I waited for it in bed until it was light. Where was it?'

'It was with me.' Lesca put down the chair she was carrying. 'I was in the hall where the dead play, and they called twelve.'

Madame Etcheto's hands closed on her handbag.

'That's my door.'

'Not this door. There are two of me, Madame Etcheto, and the other one has a room twelve under her bank. I took my river over there with me. And in the hall, when they called twelve, somebody sat down.'

'Then it wasn't Inguma that came for her,' Madame Etcheto said. 'It was you.'

'Yes.'

Madame Etcheto waited for more. There wasn't any.

'I heard that river go away, the night you turned your handle. It went like a bath emptying.' She took her hand off the wheel. 'Nobody emptied hers.'

Nadine turned the wheelchair towards the minibus. Madame Etcheto kept looking at Lesca until the chair had turned her away.

* * *

That evening Maïté let Lesca in without asking why she had come. Maïté still had her black coat on. On the kitchen wall beside the door hung an old phone with a cord, with a clock above it.

'I had a verb,' Maïté said. 'For the stone. I had it ready at that table, and your timer rang. *Left*. He was left out there. I wanted that word cut under his name, with yours under it.'

'It would have been true of both names. I left him.'

'Yes.' Maïté went to the door and held it open. Then she looked up at the edge of the roof, in the light from the kitchen. 'That back gutter's blocked again. Have a look at it before you go.'

Lesca set the ladder against the edge of the roof and Maïté held it. The gutter was packed solid. Lesca cleared it by hand, dropping the rotten leaves past Maïté's shoulder in black handfuls.

At the bottom of the garden somebody lifted the catch of the gate.

Larrère had come up the back lane on foot, without a torch, in the coat he had worn at the stone. He stopped at the garden gate with his hand on it and his face turned up to the ladder.

'He used to come up that lane on duck nights, to the kitchen,' Maïté said. She kept her eyes on the ladder. 'Since the flood he comes as far as the gate, and I don't open it.'

She turned her head and spoke down the garden.

'You knew he was out there, Guy. You stood in my kitchen that night and rang her from my phone, with his van gone down to the fields, and you never told the woman you'd sent to the gate.'

Down in the Port a firework went up from the reception on the bank and lit him red, then white.

'I'll be brief,' he said.

Maïté gave him time. He looked at the lit kitchen window, then at Maïté.

He said nothing.

He took his hand off the gate and went back down the lane in the dark.

Maïté put her hand back on the ladder. Lesca finished the gutter, hung the ladder on its hooks and went home by the front way.

* * *

The village council met on Tuesday at 6 with one item on the sheet. The deputy mayor took the chair for it, which left his own seat empty, halfway down the table.

Thirteen people were already sitting when Lesca came in with the sash on. For eleven years they had stood behind their chairs until she sat. She took the empty seat. Then she lifted the sash over her head, folded it in three and laid it in front of her, with the keys of the town hall on top.

Marie-Jo looked at the sash. She had handed her own sash back across this table in a plastic bag.

'That the council ask the prefect to remove the mayor,' the deputy mayor read, with a finger under the line. 'The member concerned may speak on it.'

'For three minutes,' the secretary said. 'On any item. That's the mayor's—' She looked down at her register. 'That's the rule.'

Marie-Jo turned the white kitchen timer to three and stood it beside the sash.

Lesca had made the rule in her first month, for Larrère. For eleven years she had watched that timer tick from the head of the table. It was louder from here.

Larrère sat across from her with his association's book shut and both hands on it. He had waited eleven years for this item. He didn't ask to speak on it.

The table waited to hear her account. Every December in this room she had given it forty houses and a care home, in less than a minute. On Saturday she had given the square the rest, into a microphone. She had nothing to add.

She took the dial and turned it back to nothing. The bell rang.

'That's the member's time,' Marie-Jo said. She said it to Lesca this time, not to the wall. 'It goes to the vote.'

'Those in favour,' the deputy mayor said.

Marie-Jo's hand went up first. The others followed around the table, one at a time, until there were eleven. Three of them lived in the Port, in houses that had stayed dry. Their hands were no slower than the rest.

Larrère kept both of his on the book.

'Against,' the deputy mayor said.

No hand went up. The deputy mayor looked down the table at the book.

'I abstain,' Larrère said.

The secretary counted the hands and wrote the number down. Then her pen came up again.

'The motion is carried, Madame le—' She stopped. The pen stayed where it was.

'Lesca, Maylis,' Lesca said. 'Surname first.'

* * *

That night Lesca was sitting on the edge of her bed when Coralie came up the stairs and stopped on the landing.

'It was in the care home before the council had its coats on,' Coralie said. She had her hand on the spare-room door. 'So that's eleven years of Madame le maire gone, and I need something to call you. The gendarmes say Madame Lesca. I'm not saying that across a landing.'

She stayed there a moment longer, four steps away.

'Good night, Maylis,' she said, and went into the spare room.

Lesca left her door open and the lamp on.
