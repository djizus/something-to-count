# 17

At first light on Sunday 13 December Maylis walked down into the Port. Grey mud covered the road from wall to wall. Her boots sank in it to the ankle.

The water had gone back out through the gap as the river dropped. All along the road furniture stood on the pavements: mattresses on end, a cupboard with its drawers out. The doors of the care home were open and there was nobody inside. On Saturday the ambulances had taken the forty-one to Bayonne. The door of room twelve was shut, with a strip of the gendarmes' tape across it.

Larrère's car was at the crossroads with the driver's door open, stuck in mud up to the bottom of its doors. He was coming down the road on foot, carrying a cardboard folder at shoulder height. The folder was dry. His trousers were grey with mud to the knee.

Sarthou stood in the road outside the last house, in uniform, with his cap on. He let Larrère come to within a metre of him.

'They tell me,' Larrère said, and stopped.

Behind Sarthou the door of his house stood open. The water had left its mark along the wallpaper at the height of a table. Two doors up, Madame Darrigrand stood on the pavement beside a baby carriage, with the baby inside her coat. The carriage was full of mud up to the hood.

Larrère turned to Maylis, opened the folder on his forearm, took out her resignation and held it up.

*Saturday 12 December 2026* was written in the gap, in his handwriting, above her tall L. She had been afraid of that date since 1 December. It was the smallest thing that had happened to her since Friday.

'Received and accepted yesterday,' he said. 'That gate went up without a word from me, with your key in another man's hand. The key goes in my tray.'

'And the other sheet?' Maylis said. 'The one with a name on it.'

Larrère looked at Sarthou and shut the folder.

'There is no other sheet,' he said.

'There is a file,' Sarthou said. He said it to her, with his eyes on his notebook. 'It has a name in it. The keyholder was under an order to remain at the gate. At 2:57 she left it. That is recorded. I am not saying that I would have stayed there myself.'

'You had the key,' Madame Darrigrand said.

'Yes.'

'I'd rather you heard it from me than from the café.' She wiped the handle of the baby carriage with her sleeve. 'At 5 on Friday it was light, and Castagnet was up on the bank at your van window. He's said so in the Port. You had the key on your belt then. At 3 you were a kilometre away, fetching a man out of a dry hole. Sarthou carried my baby out over his head.' She turned to Larrère. 'And I signed a paper for that gate. It went in through your car window.'

Larrère turned around in the mud, holding the folder up out of it, and went back to his car.

Maylis measured it in her head, from the trapdoor to Madame Darrigrand's door. It was closer to a kilometre and two hundred metres. She didn't correct Madame Darrigrand.

* * *

The village van was still across the track in the dip, nose to nose with Jeannot's. The river had been over both of them. Then it had gone back out through the open gate with the tides and left the track grey. Kévin backed his own van down, hooked a chain onto the back of the village van and pulled it tight.

'How long has Castagnet been writing to the town hall about this dip?' he said. 'Is it out of gear? It'll come.'

It came on the third pull, backwards, with its door hanging open and mud on both seats. The seats could be cleaned. The engine was finished.

'What do I owe you?'

'Nothing. You looked the other way for four winters, for nothing, so I can do a morning.' He rolled up the chain. 'The boxes stood on the pavement at the care home until the battery died. I tipped them back in the river.'

'You told me on Friday not to keep that gate shut for you,' Maylis said. 'I didn't. That wasn't why.'

Kévin hung the chain on its hook and sat down on the step of his van.

He held up his left hand.

'On Saturday in that yard, in the water, with the chair in my hands, I counted. I got five, and I counted them again.' He closed the hand. 'I wanted six, Maylis. I've never wanted anything so much.'

'It was five,' she said.

He towed the village van up onto the bank for her. Then he went back down with the chain for Jeannot's.

* * *

The Bayonne bus was standing at the church with its engine running. Maïté came along the road from the high ground in her good coat, carrying a light brown suitcase with two straps. She didn't look at the café, and nobody came out of it.

She handed the suitcase up to the driver. She had the fare ready in her glove, in coins, but it wasn't enough. The driver looked at the coins, then at the suitcase, and let her on.

The bus left. Josiane watched it go from the café door with a cloth in her hand.

'I said it was in that house,' she said. 'I only had the one name wrong.'

Maylis went past her into the café for bread. It stood in its crate on the counter. The card game was on at its table, with old Bordenave beside it, holding the pad. Jeannot's stool was empty.

The door opened behind her and somebody came in out of the cold.

'Late,' the dealer said to him, without looking up from the cards. 'Had the key and went for a walk, did you?'

Then he looked up and saw her at the counter.

The cards stayed in his hand. Bordenave's pencil stayed on the pad. Josiane took a loaf out of the crate, laid it on the counter and counted the change into Maylis's left hand one coin at a time.

Maylis took the loaf out past the village hall. Marie-Jo was at its door with the keys, counting stacked chairs back against the wall. She looked at the loaf, then at the way Maylis held her side.

'I had the coffee pot on in here at 3:30 on Saturday morning, and I counted them in,' she said. 'My son's van came up that hill three times. He says you sent it.' She pulled a chair off the stack and stood it on its legs. 'One,' she said, in the voice she called the numbers in. 'Just the one. Sit down on it.'

Maylis sat down.

* * *

Coralie came in at 9 that evening. Maylis was in the kitchen with the kettle on. Coralie had been in her uniform since Friday afternoon, and it had dried on her twice.

'Larrère's put a date on my letter,' Maylis said.

Coralie went to the oven, opened the door a few centimetres and bent down to the gap.

'The date,' she said, in the voice that came from the back of the throat. 'There's the question of the—'

She stayed bent over for a moment with her hand on the door. Then she shut it and sat down at the kitchen table in her coat. The shopping pad was on the table. It had a week of empty boxes on it, and under them the one she had drawn for Friday night.

'Room twelve,' she said. 'I'll say what it is to me, and then I won't say it again. Every late shift since I started there I've stood at that door at lights-out while she said her words to the wardrobe, and her care plan said I wasn't to join in. On Friday at 10 she wouldn't sleep in a chair, and I walked her back to her room on my arm. You wrote to me to move her, and I wouldn't wake her. That part is mine. Her daughter is having her buried here on Tuesday. I'm not telling you this so that you'll carry less of yours. You had the key.'

'I had the key.'

'And on Friday you asked me back, on a doorstep, and I said 7.' Coralie laid both hands flat on the table. 'At 7 I was in a hall counting heads, and you were in it with me, and nobody was in the house.' Her voice stayed steady. 'And before 7 I'd asked you one thing, down a phone, with the water at my back door. The gate, or Castagnet. You said both. I know that word. I've cooked it.'

'It wasn't Either at the dip,' Maylis said. 'The van went in, and I had to pick one. I picked.'

'I know what you picked. I was the other one.'

Coralie reached over to the fruit bowl and took a new envelope from behind it, from the place where her form used to stand. It had been cut open along the top. She laid it beside the pad.

'Bayonne's written. The geriatric floor, on nights. I start in January.'

'Stay.'

'That's what you want,' Coralie said. She turned the envelope around on the table. 'I want that floor. I trained on it, under Ithurbide. A floor and a night of my own to run, where I say who gets turned and who's left to sleep. I wanted it before Friday, and I want it more now. I can't stand at that door at lights-out again.'

'It's twenty minutes. You could drive in from here.'

'Don't give me the measurement. I could. I'm not going to.'

She went upstairs. Maylis followed her as far as the bedroom door and found she had brought the pad with her.

Coralie packed one bag on the bed, out of the chest of drawers. Three uniforms went in, then the good coat, folded, then the clock she woke up to. Maylis stood in the doorway holding the pad in both hands and watched the bag fill. It wasn't much. She took more than that to the dump in a morning.

'I've asked you one question for sixteen years, and drawn you boxes for it,' Coralie said, without turning around. 'You've got a week of them in your hands. I'll ask it once more, with no box. What do you want, Maylis?'

'Madame Etcheto's being buried here on Tuesday. I want to dig the grave myself.'

Coralie shut the bag.

'Then you'll dig it. With that side. I'll strap it first.'

She carried the bag down and stood it by the front door. In the kitchen she got out the first-aid box she had brought home from work. Maylis sat while Coralie strapped the rib, with three turns of the wide bandage, pulled tight. She tucked the end in. Her hand stayed on it a little longer than a bandage needs.

Then she went upstairs. Maylis heard her cross the landing to the spare room and come out again. She heard her on every stair coming down, all eleven, slower than she had gone up. She heard the passage, then the front door, then the car door shutting on a pillow.

* * *

That night Maylis got into the middle of the bed, on her back, and waited for Inguma. There was one pillow on the bed.

At 2:51 Inguma came down and sat on her. She kept still under it. Then she was in the chair with Lesca. They got up out of it and faced each other, while Inguma waited just inside the door.

The wall where the hall had been was steel again. Lesca's right hand hung open at her side. She didn't call anything to order.

Maylis had the card from her raincoat in her hand. *left*. Lesca was holding the one from the shelf. *right*. It was the same handwriting, with the t crossed low.

'Forty-one,' Maylis said.

Lesca didn't answer.

'Is yours in the house?' Maylis said.

'Across the landing. Four steps. Yours?'

'She took the pillow.' Maylis looked at the door. 'Don't come again.'

'I won't.'

It came out whole.

One lamp went out. Then the other.

In the dark the dry sound started on the steel floor, but Inguma didn't come to the chair. It went out over the step of the door. As it went, the steel under its leaves knocked against the steel of the frame, like one part of the room touching another. Then the door shut behind it on the rubber seal.

Neither of them sat down.

Maylis woke under her own lampshade. Her phone said 02:54. There was nothing on the wardrobe.

* * *

The cemetery was above the village and had stayed dry. On Monday morning, when Maylis came up, the priest was at the gate under his umbrella.

'Tomorrow at 10,' he said. 'Madame Etcheto's daughter wants the words her mother said each night to be said over her. I've had them written out for me, and I'm not sure they're a prayer.'

'They're for a thing that sits on you.'

'They'll do.' He looked at the place by the wall, next to Monsieur Etcheto's grave. 'You'll dig it? Monsieur Larrère tells me the village has no outdoor worker this morning.'

'Then it gets dug for nothing,' Maylis said. 'Yes.'

'It's the eighth this year. I keep count.'

He went down to his car. She took the pegs and the string from the tool hut, set the first peg at the head, measured the length and the width from it and ran the string around the four pegs.

She cut the grass along the string with the edge of the shovel, left-handed. It was a morning's work for two arms. She had one.

Jeannot came up the path from the gate with an envelope in his hand and stopped at the foot of the string.

'I've got a letter,' he said, 'and I've brought it by hand. I paid to register forty-one of them, to get Larrère's name on a card, and I never got it once. I'm not paying for this one.'

He held it out. In the top left corner he had written *No. 42*.

'It's not a complaint,' he said. 'I started it as one. It's the first one that isn't. It's for my cousin Sarthou, with a copy for the mayor's tray. It says that at 3 on Saturday morning the keyholder was at the trapdoor of my hut, a kilometre from her gate, pulling me out of it by the neck. It says why she thought she had to. It says the cellar was dry. It says the handle stopped at nineteen, and who had been written to about it. It says the gate went up under my hands at 3:20, and that it took two arms and a knee. You had the one arm.'

'The arm is the excuse you've written for me. It isn't mine,' Maylis said. 'At 3 I never tried the handle.'

Jeannot turned the envelope over. 'It says who had rung her, and from whose kitchen. My cousin got that off the screen of your phone on Saturday, and he's my cousin.'

'Maïté left on the bus.'

'I saw the wardrobe.' He turned the envelope back. 'You told me twice to put it in forty-two. It's in. It's four pages, and it doesn't thank you. I was in no danger, and I'll say so to anyone. But they're saying you went for a walk, and I was what you walked to. That goes in writing.'

She took the letter and buttoned it into her chest pocket.

He looked at the string, at the cut grass, at the way she held the shovel.

'Whose is it?'

'Madame Etcheto's.'

'She gave my mother her first communion dress.' He looked around. A second shovel was leaning against the wall of the tool hut, where Maylis's father had kept it. 'You won't get that down two metres with one arm, Lesca.'

He went and got it. Then he stepped over the string in his rubber boots and began to dig.
