# Trial: chapter 1, the daytime

Changed: Larrère's speech at the car window is played as dialogue. The post van now comes while he is still at the window, so the mayor sits a metre from his own registered letter and will not sign for it. Marie-Jo says why she printed two hundred cards, and Jeannot says why he pays to register. These are added exchanges, and the post van comes an hour earlier than in the manuscript.
Kept: every event and fact later chapters use, including the tall L in the rain, No. 41, letter 17 and the diesel, the dark third string, the whole box paragraph, the bar from eight, the van where the stone stood, 'put it in forty-two' and the folder over his head.

* * *

It rained all Tuesday, straight down.

At a quarter past nine Maylis was raking plane leaves off the drain gratings in the square, left-handed. She had left the van on the far side by the church, though there was room in front of the mairie, where she left it on other days.

The mayor drew up beside her. He was Guy Larrère, who grew maize and was also president of the landowners' association, which owned the sluice gate on the flood meadows below the village. Maylis kept the key to its winch, for forty euros a month on top of her wage as the commune's one outdoor employee.

He lowered his window a few centimetres, on account of the rain.

'I'll be brief,' he said. 'The lights. Saturday is the loto, and Hontaube is sending a table. I want nine trees lit when they drive in.'

'They're in the shed.'

'Your father had them up by Saint Catherine's, which is tomorrow. He hung those trees from the tractor bucket, and in thirty years he never asked the commune for a ladder.'

'I haven't asked for one.'

'I'm saying it before you do.' He took his right hand off the wheel, closed it on nothing and turned it for her behind the glass. 'And he turned that winch with one hand. Like that.'

On Monday at ten to four he had telephoned to have the gate raised forty centimetres before the tide turned, since the Adour was tidal this far up and the meadows drained only on the ebb. At the nineteenth turn the handle stopped. She got her shoulder under it and pushed. It gave without warning and put her into the platform rail, which took her under the right arm, and she heard the rib go. She raised the remaining centimetres left-handed and drove home.

'It stuck on Monday,' she said. 'At nineteen turns.'

'The council has taken note of the winch.'

'In 2011. I wrote again in 2012 and in 2013.'

'Then it's in the minutes.'

'It's the association's winch. You're the president.'

'Nobody has written to the president.'

'He was in the room. All three times.'

'He was in the room as mayor,' Larrère said.

After 2013 she had bought grease and stopped writing.

'Now, the first of January,' he said. 'We're to be a hamlet of Hontaube, and Hontaube has two outdoor men of its own. So there's the question of staff. It isn't settled, and you're not to worry about it.'

His engine ran throughout. She kept her right elbow against her side. By the church clock he had been nine minutes, and the window had started up when the post van came round the church.

The mairie was shut, and a card in the door gave its hours as Tuesday and Friday, two till six. The postman was young and a replacement. He tried the door and read the card. Then he looked round the square, which held a woman with a rake and a man in a car, and he went to the car.

'I've a registered for the mayor,' he said into the gap. 'Do you know where I'd find him?'

Larrère looked at the envelope. In the top left corner the sender had written No. 41.

'The mairie opens at two.'

'I'm in Urcuit at two.'

'Then you leave a slip in the box, and he fetches it from the post office at Urt.'

'I can do that.' The postman turned the envelope over and read the back. 'It's come four hundred metres. He could have walked it round and put it through the door for nothing.'

'He could,' Larrère said.

The postman looked round the square again. This time he read the back of the yellow waterproof, which had COMMUNE DE PEYREBARTHE printed across it.

'Is the man from the commune about?'

'That's me,' Maylis said.

He looked at the rake. He looked at the man in the car, who was looking at the church. Then he held out the letter with the acknowledgement card on top of it, and a pen.

She signed in the box, M. Lesca, with the tall L her father had put on thirty years of delivery notes. Rain fell on the card and the L spread.

'That's it received,' the postman said. He bent to the gap once more. 'If you see the mayor, you might tell him it's come.'

'I'll tell him,' said Larrère.

Jeannot Castagnet was in his white van outside the café, forty metres off, with the wipers going. He had watched her sign. When the postman pulled away, he pulled away behind him.

'You needn't have been so quick,' Larrère said.

'He asked for the commune.'

'In my tray,' he said, and the window went up.

The envelope was addressed to Monsieur le Maire, and the sender's name was on the back, Castagnet J. Of the forty before it, twenty-six had concerned the level of the water in the low meadows. Duck come down at night only to standing water, and he held the level too low for them. Letter 17, in January 2017, had concerned ruts made by the commune's tractor on the track out to his tonne, the half-buried cabin he shot from. The tractor had made the ruts while pulling his van out of the mud. The diesel was deducted from her pay.

She raked the mayor's parking space last, at about half past ten, and raked it from the kerb. Then she let herself into the mairie and put letter 41 in the mayor's tray, on top of a letter from the prefecture asking how many road signs the commune possessed, since they would all need changing.

She spent the rest of the morning in the commune's shed with the Christmas lights, nine strings in nine boxes, each numbered in her father's hand. She plugged them in one after another. The third stayed dark.

* * *

She was due at the village hall at two with the trestles. She loaded the van one piece at a time, which was the rib again, and got there at twenty past.

Marie-Jo Labat, president of the fête committee, was walking the length of the hall with a clipboard, counting stacked chairs.

'Sixty-one,' she said. 'Sixty-two. Here she is, we thought you were still in the box. Sixty-three.'

It was what Peyrebarthe said to anyone who came late, and had been since June 2014, when the council let the old tobacco-drying barn to a university team and, by the same deliberation, placed Mademoiselle Lesca at the team's disposal for one afternoon. The deliberation gave the purpose in the team's words. It was to establish whether a person shut in a sealed room, who has seen one of two lamps light, is for those outside two persons until the door is opened. The café had it as the cat that is alive and dead until somebody lifts the lid, with Maylis for the cat. The team shut her in a steel room the size of a garden shed, and three hours later opened the door, counted one of her and paid her a hundred and eighty euros. The woman in charge had called her the friend all afternoon, which was what the one inside was called in the argument the thing came from. She had been thirty-one. She was forty-three now and had heard it at the café counter, through car windows, and once across a grave she had dug, on a morning when the hearse was late from Bayonne.

'I did a rib,' she said. 'On the winch.'

Marie-Jo stopped counting. 'That winch you wrote to them about.'

'That one.'

Marie-Jo looked at her for about as long as it takes to count a chair. Then she said 'Sixty-three' again and went on down the hall.

There were twenty-four tables, each a top and two trestles, to go in three rows under the strip lights. Maylis carried the tops against her left side. Marie-Jo came behind her, kicked each table square with the side of her shoe and unrolled paper cloth along it. The hall smelled of floor wax and the coffee urn. A feed sack of maize kernels stood on the edge of the stage for marking the cards. Beside it stood the prizes, a ham, two ducks and a strimmer, and a carton of printed cards.

'There's two hundred in that,' Maylis said.

'Hontaube had a hundred and forty at theirs. They gave a television.'

'We had eighty-eight.'

'We had eighty-eight because the hall was set for eighty-eight. People can count chairs.' Marie-Jo kicked a trestle. 'Their mayor's wife is coming on Saturday. I'll not have her go home and say we were a ham and four tables. How many is Coralie bringing up from the home?'

'Twelve. It's what the minibus holds.'

'Then it can go down for twelve more.'

'They're in bed by half past eight.'

'They're in bed every other night of the year,' Marie-Jo said. 'I've put you on the bar from eight. You've the time.'

She had written it on the clipboard before she said it.

* * *

By ten past five it was dark and still raining. Maylis locked the hall and drove up to the square, and this time she stopped in front of the mairie. The mayor's space was empty, a white rectangle with the paint worn through on the driver's side.

She got out and stepped off the kerb into it. She went two paces in and stood square to the mairie door, with an oil stain by her left boot. Rain ran off her hood. The church clock had struck five while she was driving, so she owed the rib ten breaths, and she took them there.

Headlights came round the church and stopped a metre short of her. The horn went once. She stepped up onto the kerb, and a white Trafic with mud up to the door handles drove into the mayor's space and stopped with its front wheels where the stone had stood.

Jeannot Castagnet put his window all the way down. He was dressed for the night, in waders and two jackets.

'He's had it, then.'

'It's in his tray. On top.'

'I watched him have it. He was a metre from the pen and he looked at the church.' He leaned an elbow out into the rain. 'Forty-one. Forty-one times I've paid to register, so as to have that man's name on a card, and it's little Lesca who signs for them.' He tipped his chin towards the river. 'I had a hand of water at the tonne on Sunday night. Last night I sat over mud.'

'I was told to raise it.'

'You're told. Your father was told and all. He stopped four turns short and left me my hand of water, and Larrère never walked down to look.'

'Put it in forty-two.'

He laughed. She would have, and the rib stopped her, and he saw it stop her.

'What have you done to yourself?'

'A rib. The winch stuck.'

'Then that goes in forty-two as well,' he said, and he had stopped laughing.

He climbed out and crossed to the café with his waders creaking. Rain ran off the roof of the van onto the tarmac where she had been standing.

A second pair of headlights came round the church. Guy Larrère stopped behind the Trafic and sounded his horn twice. Nobody came out of the café. He lowered his window a few centimetres and looked at the van, and then at her.

'The lights are for Saturday,' he said.

He drove on round the square and parked by the church. Then he walked back across in the rain with a folder held over his head, went round the van in his space without a word about it, and went in to sign his post.
