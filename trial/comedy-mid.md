# Trial: chapter 17, the café at noon

Changed: the manuscript reports the café's verdict on the chrysanthemums in one paragraph ("By then the café had settled Sunday"). Here it is a scene, with Jeannot, Kévin, Sarthou and Larrère in the room. Three of them want Jeannot back at his tonne, and two cannot say why. Larrère's warning moves here from his car window at two, and Jeannot decides here to go down again, which the manuscript has Sarthou report that morning.
Added: Sarthou leaving the room twice, Kévin and the teal, Larrère's glass, and Maylis telling Jeannot to stay in. Kept: the river, the six nights, the twenty minutes in the kitchen, and the last sentence, which is the manuscript's. A reader may now guess from this scene why Larrère wants the house empty.

* * *

At noon she went into the café for bread, and the café went on with what it was doing.

The four at the stove table had their cards face down. The one who kept the score had turned the pad over and was writing on the back of it.

'Seven minutes past ten,' the owner's wife said, from behind the bread crate. 'I'd the till open. Two euros. They were five at All Saints.'

'Seven past.' He wrote it. 'Terrace road. Four minutes to the fourth house.'

'Five. She'd a loaf under one arm and the pot in the other hand.'

'Five. And out again just after the half hour. My sister-in-law has the third house, and she was at her window for the whole of it.' He drew a line and added up under it. 'Twenty minutes in the kitchen, with the door shut.'

Jeannot Castagnet sat on his stool at the counter in his day jacket, with a glass of white and his back to the table. It was his kitchen they were adding up, and his wife in it. Nobody had put a question to him.

Maylis laid her coins on the counter with her left hand. She did not look at him, and he did not look at her, and the table watched them at it.

'The pot,' the scorer said. He was speaking to her. 'Was it bronze or yellow? I've been given both.'

'Bronze.'

'Bronze,' he told the table, and wrote it.

'If that's a statement,' Maylis said, 'I'll have a copy.'

Sarthou was at the table in the window, in uniform, with his cap beside his cup and a sandwich in its paper. He stopped chewing.

'Nobody's taking a statement,' he said. 'Not with me sitting here. I shut a file on Friday and I've ten days to do.' He picked up his cap, went to the door and stood in it with his back to the room. 'Go on,' he said over his shoulder. 'I'm not here.'

'And two lights on the landing at Arlette's since Friday,' the owner's wife said. 'One each side. You can see them from the road.'

'Coralie's on earlies,' Maylis said. 'It's for the sleep.'

'It always is,' said the owner's wife, and handed her the loaf.

Maylis took it. She had come in with a thing to say, and she said it to the owner, who had stopped wiping a glass.

'The river's twenty under my father's line. That was at eight, at the bottom of the ebb. It'll be over on this tide.'

'She's changing the subject,' said the owner's wife.

Nobody at the table wrote it down. Jeannot was the only one who turned his head, and he turned it to the owner.

'Twenty under, at the ebb,' he said. 'Then there's a hand of water at my tonne.'

The cards stayed where they were. He had not been down to his tonne for six nights, and the café had counted those as well. It had him sitting up in his kitchen to keep the commune's girl off his wife. Maylis knew what he was sitting up for. It lay under his duckboards, folded wet, and she had told him so at this counter. He had let the café keep its own reason. On the pad at home she had his cellar, with water standing in it to the second rung.

Kévin Labat came in past Sarthou with rain on his cap. He had heard the last of it from the pavement.

'Teal,' he said. 'With water like that they'll be down in hundreds.'

'What do you know about teal?' Jeannot said.

'I'm only saying. You'd not want to miss them.'

'He's never shot a bird in his life,' the scorer said. 'What's he want you out there for?'

Kévin asked the owner for a coffee and looked at nobody. It was six tides since a bucket had come up that track to his van.

Sarthou had half turned in the doorway. 'Is it duck now?' he said. 'I can sit down for duck.'

'It's duck,' said the owner.

Sarthou came back to his sandwich. He had it in his hand when Guy Larrère walked in, shaking the rain off a folder. The owner's wife gave him the morning before he had the door shut. The girl said the river was coming over, and Castagnet said he had a hand of water at his tonne.

'So he has,' Larrère said. 'The association couldn't keep water off the low meadow this week if it wanted to. I'd say a man with a tonne would be sorry to sit at home tonight.'

Jeannot turned on his stool, the whole of him.

'Say that again.'

'I say you've your hand of water.'

'Twenty-six letters I've sent that mairie for a hand of water. Registered. What I've had back would go on a stamp.' He looked round the café. 'And now the president walks in out of the rain and wishes me good shooting. What does he want me out of my kitchen for?'

At the window Sarthou folded the paper round what was left of the sandwich. He put on his cap, took the sandwich with him and went out, past the doorway this time, to the pavement. He stood under the awning and looked at the church.

Nobody answered Jeannot. The scorer found a clean corner of the pad.

'It's the mairie,' he said. 'She's the commune's and he's the mayor. They look after their own up there.'

'He's getting you out of the way for her,' the owner's wife said.

'I'm getting nobody out of anybody's way.' Larrère had gone red to the collar. 'I've no part in it. A man's wife is no business of the commune's, and I'll say so in front of all of you.' He turned to Maylis. There was no car window between them. 'You'll keep away from that house. Flowers, and twenty minutes in a kitchen with the husband out. I'll not have it said that the mairie shuts its eyes to that.'

She did not answer him. She spoke to Jeannot's boots, since she could get no higher.

'I'd not go down tonight. It'll be over the line by two, and it hasn't stopped raining.'

The café took this in.

'Now she wants him kept in,' said the man who had asked on Sunday who was dead.

'That's so we'd not think it,' the scorer said, and he wrote that down too.

Jeannot got off his stool. He laid his coins by the glass, which was empty.

'The mayor wants me in my tonne and his girl wants me in my kitchen,' he said. 'I've a hand of water. I asked for it twenty-six times, and I'll not leave it standing there for the pair of you. I'm going down.'

'Pour him one before he goes,' Larrère said. 'On me.'

The owner had the bottle tilted before he looked up to see who had spoken. Jeannot did not wait for it. He stopped beside Maylis on his way to the door, and spoke low, to the loaf.

'I had your pot, Lesca. I know what's meant by a pot of those, sent to a man's house. You've made your point. You've no need to send another.'

'We all know what's meant,' said the owner's wife.

'I'll come down the track behind you,' Kévin said. 'In case you stick.'

'If I stick, the commune pulls me out,' Jeannot said, 'and I write to it about the ruts.'

He went out past the gendarme, who did not turn round. The glass stood on the counter, full. After a time Larrère drank it himself.

Sarthou came in off the pavement when the Trafic had gone.

'Is he going down?'

'He's going down,' said the owner.

'Then he's in a hole in a meadow every night till the eighteenth,' Sarthou said, 'and he can't write to anybody from there.' He unwrapped the sandwich.

Maylis had the loaf under her arm. Nobody had asked her about the river.

'It'll be over the line on this tide,' she said, from the door.

'You said,' the owner's wife told her. The scorer had turned the pad back over, and he was dealing.

* * *

At a quarter to two she was on the platform at the gate. The tide was at the top. The river stood two centimetres over her father's line, brown, and going by faster than a person walks.
