# 1

At nine minutes to three Maylis Lesca was awake, on her back, and nothing below her eyes would move.

The clock stood at the left edge of what she could see, red figures, 02:51. Over her was the ceiling, with the brown ring where the roof had let water in two winters ago. Rain was falling on the tiles. Coralie lay with her back turned, close enough that Maylis had the warmth of her along one arm.

She told her right hand to close. It stayed open on the blanket. She tried the left hand and then her feet, and they were as far from her as the wardrobe.

Something was lying on her chest. It was on the breastbone, a hand's width below the throat, and it had the dead weight of a feed sack let down off a trailer. The blanket over her was flat. The chill of the thing came through the blanket and through her T-shirt, and it smelled of cold steel and old tobacco. She knew that smell. The drying barn on the Hontaube road had smelled of it since before she was born.

She pushed. No muscle answered, and she pushed anyway, and the weight came down harder.

On Monday she had gone over on the wet planks of the winch platform and landed with her left side across the handle. Coralie had felt along the rib with two fingers that evening and said it was cracked. The hospital would say the same and send her home, Coralie said, so she was to sleep on her back and breathe in as far as she could ten times an hour to keep the lung clear. Maylis had slept on her right side for forty-three years. Now the cracked rib was under the weight. Each breath lifted the rib into it, and she could not get a whole one.

She tried to say Coralie's name. Air left her without a sound. She tried again and heard a dry click at the back of her own throat, quieter than the rain, and Coralie did not stir.

She was awake. The clock said 02:51 and then it said 02:52. Her work trousers hung over the chair. She was in her own bed, in the house behind her mother's, and she was going to suffocate thirty centimetres from a woman who would sleep through it.

She pushed with all she had. It pressed back with the same, and the pain went round the rib from her spine to her breastbone, and the rib made her stop. As she slackened, the weight slackened. She lay under it without pushing and counted the drips from the broken gutter outside the window, and on the ninth drip the little finger of her right hand bent.

The rest of her came back at once. She sat up too fast. With her feet on the floor she held her forearm against her side until she could breathe to the bottom. Her chest was clear. The smell hung about a few seconds more, and then the room smelled of damp plaster and Coralie's fabric conditioner.

Coralie turned over. 'What time is it?'

'Early. Sleep.'

She took her trousers off the chair and went through to the kitchen in the dark. She left the light off there too, because her mother would see it from the front house and come across the yard in her coat. She drank from her hand at the tap. The tiles were cold under her bare feet and the stove was out, and she sat at the table until her feet ached.

Before the weight there had been a stone.

She had been standing in the square in front of the church, where the tarmac was marked out for six cars. In the second bay from the steps stood a slab of pale stone as high as her shoulder, in a bed of gravel with a chain round it. Lettering was cut into the face. She had read the bottom line and no more. It said MAYLIS LESCA, in capitals cut as deep as a fingernail, and rain stood in the foot of each letter.

She knew how water sat in cut letters. On the tenth of November each year she scrubbed the war memorial with a nylon brush for the ceremony the next morning. It carried thirty-one names, and they were the only names cut in stone in that square.

She had driven across the square on Monday. The baker's van had been parked in the second bay.

At five the light came on in her mother's kitchen, as it did each morning, and Maylis put her own on. Coralie came through at half past in her work tunic and looked at the way she was sitting.

'Did you stay on your back?'

'All night.'

'And?'

'It's a rib.'

Coralie laid two fingers on the place and waited, as she did with the old people at the home, until Maylis had breathed in as far as she could. 'Further,' she said. She drank her coffee standing and left for the Port at ten to six with her hood up. Maylis had told her nothing about the night.

It was still dark, and still raining, when she backed the commune's van out of the yard. The curtain in her mother's kitchen lifted and dropped.

She parked at the mairie and walked over to the second bay from the church steps. The tarmac was wet between its two white lines, and plane leaves had matted over the drain cover. She stood on it in the rain. Then she cleared the drain with the side of her boot, since drains were her job, and went to open the workshop.

The post van pulled up at twenty past nine. The mairie was shut on Tuesday mornings and the secretary came in at two. The driver was a young man she had not seen before. He put his window down a hand's width and asked through the gap where he could find the man from the commune.

'That's me.'

He looked past her at the workshop door. Then he pushed an envelope, a yellow card and a pen through the gap. 'Registered, for the mayor. Sign on the line.'

She signed against the wet side of his van, M. Lesca, with the tall L her father had put on thirty years of delivery notes, and the rain got into the ink. He took the card back and drove on.

The sender had printed his name in capitals in the top corner, CASTAGNET J., Chemin de la Barthe. She had no right to open it and no need. She let herself into the mairie with her own key and took the box file down from the cupboard behind the secretary's desk. The last envelope in it had 40 pencilled in the corner. She put the file back and left the new envelope square on the secretary's blotter.

The road to the gate ran down off the terrace, through the Port with its shuttered houses, and up onto the dyke. Beyond the dyke lay the barthes, three hundred hectares of flood meadow that drained into the Adour through one iron gate. Maize stubble covered the higher ground. On the low ground the grass stood in water, and her mother's ponies were out in it with their rumps to the wind.

It was twenty past ten when she got there. Larrère was parked on top of the dyke with his engine running and his wipers going. He was the mayor, and he chaired the landowners who owned the gate. He put his window down as far as his chin.

'We said ten.'

'A registered letter came. I had to sign for it.'

'Castagnet,' he said. 'And you still in the box.'

Twelve years earlier the council had let the old tobacco barn to some university people for a fortnight and lent them Maylis for one afternoon. They had shut her in a steel room the size of a garden shed, and three hours later they had opened the door and paid her a hundred and eighty euros. Since that June, anyone in Peyrebarthe who came late to anything was still in the box. People said it at the school gate and at funerals. Larrère said it now without a smile, as he would have said good morning.

'I'll be brief,' he said.

He wanted the high barthe dry by Friday so the stubble could be turned in. She was to open the gate full and shut it at four. Her father had opened it full and stood by it, he said, and had not sat in a van. Then he was on to the merger with Hontaube, which paid two outdoor men of its own, and the question of staff after the first of January, which was not settled and which she was not to worry about. His exhaust ran at the height of her knees. Rain came off the peak of her hood onto the winch handle she was holding, and she kept her left elbow against her side.

'The winch binds at forty turns,' she said, when he stopped to light a cigarette. 'One of these days it won't go past.'

'The council has taken note of the winch.'

'Three years running.'

'Then it's in the minutes.'

He put the window up at eleven and stayed to watch her climb.

The platform stood six steel steps above the drain, with a rail on the river side. Its planks were black where she had gone over on Monday. She fitted the handle to the shaft and turned with her right arm, counting. It was sixty-two turns to full. At forty the handle stopped dead. She got her right shoulder over it and leaned, and for the length of that turn she could not breathe at all, and then the handle went past. The gate came up out of the drain a centimetre at a time, and under the planks the brown water of the barthe began to go through to the river.

When she looked up at sixty-two, his pickup had gone.

She sat in the van with the engine off, because the commune counted the diesel, and watched the water drop down the gauge painted on the concrete. The windscreen misted with her breath and she wiped it with her sleeve. By two she could not feel her feet in her boots. On each hour she breathed in ten times as far as the rib let her. At four she climbed up and wound the gate down, sixty-two turns, and by then the grass on the low ground stood clear of the water and the ponies had moved up to the fence.

It was dark when she got back to the square. She hung the handle in the workshop and walked over to the second bay and stood in it again. The lamp on the church wall showed her the wet tarmac and the drain.

A van came round the church with its headlamps on her and stopped a metre short. The horn went once. She stepped back over the white line, and the van took the bay. It was a white Trafic with mud up to the door handles.

Jeannot Castagnet put his window down. He was dressed for the night, in waders and two jackets.

'It came, then.'

'This morning. I signed.'

'Forty-one,' he said. 'And it's little Lesca who signs for them.' He tipped his chin towards the river. 'I had a hand of water at the tonne last night.' The tonne was his hide, a concrete cabin sunk to its roof in the low barthe, where he sat up for duck from August to January. 'Tonight I'll be looking at mud.'

'I was told to open it.'

'You're told. Your father was told and all. He stopped four turns short and left me my hand of water, and Larrère never walked down to look.'

'Put it in forty-two.'

He laughed. She would have, and the rib stopped her.

He climbed out and crossed to the café with his waders creaking. The van stood in the second bay with its nose to the church wall, and rain ran off the roof onto the tarmac where she had been standing.

Coralie was asleep by ten. Maylis lay down beside her on her back, since the rib gave her no other way to lie. Rain was coming down on the tiles and dripping from the broken gutter.

When she woke the clock said 02:51 and the room smelled of cold steel and old tobacco. Her hand would not close. The weight came down on her chest.
