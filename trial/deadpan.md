# 1

At nine minutes to three on the morning of Tuesday 24 November, Maylis Lesca was awake in her own bed and could not move.

She tried her right hand first, since it was the one she used. Nothing went down the arm. She tried the left hand, then both feet, then her jaw, and had the same result from each. Her eyes turned when she told them to. They gave her the ceiling, the paper lampshade, the top of the wardrobe and, on the chest of drawers, the red figures of the clock radio, which read 02:51.

There was a weight on her breastbone.

She looked for what was doing it. The dark above the bed had nothing in it. No hands were on her and no face was over hers. By turning her eyes down as far as they went she could see the blanket over her chest, and the blanket lay flat. The weight sat on the bone all the same, a hand's width from the rib she had cracked on Monday, and she put it at thirty-five kilos, because that was a sack of cement and she lifted one most weeks. It smelled of cold steel. It smelled also of old tobacco, in a house where nobody had ever smoked.

She pushed up against it with the breath she had. It pressed down exactly as hard. She pushed harder and so did it, and she saw that of the two of them she would tire first.

Coralie was asleep on her left, turned to the wall, near enough for Maylis to have the warmth of her along one arm. Maylis tried to say her name. She had said it every day for sixteen years. What came out was air, and little of that. Coralie breathed on at the pace she kept when she had the early shift and meant to have all her sleep.

Maylis thought it was a stroke. Coralie worked at the care home down in the Port and talked about strokes over supper, about residents who woke with one arm gone and the mouth gone on the same side. This was both sides. Coralie had never mentioned both sides. Maylis thought next that people died like this, at night, beside somebody, and were found when the alarm went at six.

She also had a stone in her head.

It had not been there when she went to bed. It stood in the square in front of the mairie, in the space where the mayor parked, a slab of grey granite as tall as the mairie door. Cut into it in capitals was MAYLIS LESCA. The rest of the square was as she knew it from sweeping it on Mondays and Fridays, down to the green bench with the slat missing. A line was cut above her name and she had not read it. She remembered the stone the way she remembered where she had left the van.

She dug the graves in Peyrebarthe, seven so far that year, and she knew what a name on a stone was for.

The clock read 02:52. She pushed, the weight answered, and the steel got into the back of her throat. The clock read 02:53. At 02:54 the big toe of her left foot moved against the sheet. The foot followed it, and a noise came out of her that she would not have owned to in daylight. The weight went all at once, as a sack does when someone takes the other end.

Coralie turned over. She did not put the light on. She found Maylis's wrist and laid two fingers on the inside of it, watching the clock as she did forty times a day at the home, and for half a minute neither of them spoke.

'A hundred and twelve,' Coralie said. 'What is it? Do you want a tablet? Do you want the light?'

'It's the rib.'

'What do you want, Maylis?'

'Nothing. Go back to sleep.'

Coralie kept the wrist a little longer and then gave it back. Within four minutes she was asleep. Maylis lay on her back, which was the one position the rib allowed, opening and closing her hands on the blanket to be sure of them. She did not shut her eyes. At twenty past four rain began on the roof of the lean-to, and at six the alarm went.

Coralie left for the home at half past six. On the kitchen table she left a page from the shopping pad. It said Tonight, and under that soup, and under that the rest of the duck, with a box drawn beside each. Maylis wrote Either across the bottom and went out to the van.

* * *

The rib was the winch's doing.

Below Peyrebarthe, past the dyke, lay the barthes, three hundred hectares of flood meadow that drained to the Adour through a sluice gate. The gate was a steel plate raised by a hand winch on a platform. Maylis held the key to the platform, and for that the commune paid her forty euros a month on top of her wage as its one outdoor employee. The landowners' association decided when the gate moved, and its president telephoned her.

In 2011 she told the council in writing that the winch would seize. The council took note. She wrote again in 2012 and in 2013, and the council took note in 2012 and in 2013. The three notes could be consulted in the register of deliberations at the mairie during opening hours. After 2013 she bought grease and stopped writing.

On Monday at ten to four the president telephoned to have the gate raised forty centimetres before the tide turned. At the nineteenth turn the handle stopped. She got her shoulder under it and pushed. It gave without warning and put her into the platform rail, which took her under the right arm, and she heard the rib go. She raised the remaining centimetres left-handed and drove home. Coralie pressed along her side with two fingers until she found the place. She said it was cracked and would take six weeks, and that Maylis was to sleep on her back until then.

It rained all Tuesday, straight down.

At a quarter past nine Maylis was raking plane leaves off the drain gratings in the square. She had left the commune's van on the far side by the church, though there was room in front of the mairie, where she left it on other days.

The mayor drew up beside her. He was Guy Larrère, who grew maize and who was also the president of the landowners' association. He lowered his window a few centimetres, on account of the rain, and said he would be brief. The Christmas lights were to be up in the square by Saturday, since Saturday was the loto and people would be coming from Hontaube. Her father had always had them up by Saint Catherine's, which was tomorrow, and had hung the plane trees from the tractor bucket without asking for a ladder. Her father, he went on, had turned that winch with one hand, and he took his own hand off the wheel to show her the grip. The engine ran throughout. By the church clock he was nine minutes. He did not ask how the gate had gone on Monday, and the window went up.

She raked the mayor's parking space last, at about half past ten, and raked it from the kerb. She was still on the kerb when the post van came.

The mairie was shut, and a card in the door gave its hours as Tuesday and Friday, two till six. The postman was young and a replacement. He sat with his engine running and looked round the square until he found a yellow waterproof with COMMUNE DE PEYREBARTHE printed across the back.

'I've a registered for the mairie,' he said through the window. 'Is the man from the commune about?'

'That's me.'

He looked at the rake. Then he held out the letter with the acknowledgement card on top of it, and a pen. She signed in the box, Lesca, in the round hand taught at the school on the other side of the square, and put the date under it. Rain fell on the card and the L spread.

The envelope was addressed to Monsieur le Maire in blue capitals. In the top left corner the sender had written No. 41. He numbered them himself so that the mairie could not claim to have lost one. His name was on the back, Castagnet J., with an address on the road down to the Port, four hundred metres from the mairie's letterbox, in which a letter could be left for nothing. Of the forty before it, twenty-six had concerned the level of the water in the low barthe, which he held to be too low for duck. Letter 17, in January 2017, had concerned ruts made by the commune's tractor on the track out to his tonne, the half-buried cabin he shot from. The tractor had made the ruts while pulling his van out of the mud. The diesel was deducted from her pay.

Jeannot Castagnet was in his white van outside the café, forty metres off, with the wipers going. He watched her sign. When the postman pulled away, he pulled away behind him.

She let herself into the mairie with her key and put letter 41 in the mayor's tray, on top of a letter from the prefecture asking how many road signs the commune possessed. From 1 January Peyrebarthe was to be a hamlet of Hontaube, and the signs would need changing.

She spent what was left of the morning in the commune's shed with the lights. There were nine strings in nine boxes, each numbered in her father's hand. She laid them out on the concrete and plugged them in one after another. The third stayed dark.

* * *

She was due at the village hall at two with the trestles. She loaded the van one piece at a time, which was the rib again, and got there at twenty past.

Marie-Jo Labat, president of the fête committee, was walking the length of the hall with a clipboard, counting stacked chairs.

'Sixty-one,' she said. 'Sixty-two. Here she is, we thought you were still in the box. Sixty-three.'

There was nobody else in the hall to laugh, and Marie-Jo had not said it for a laugh. It was what Peyrebarthe said to anyone who came late, and had been since June 2014. In that month the council let the old tobacco-drying barn to a university team for a fortnight, at six hundred euros, and by the same deliberation placed Mademoiselle Lesca at the team's disposal for one afternoon. The team put up a steel room inside the barn, the size of a garden shed, with a chair in it. Maylis sat on the chair for three hours with the door shut. Then they opened the door and gave her a hundred and eighty euros and a sandwich, and by the weekend they had gone. She had been thirty-one. She was forty-three now and had heard it in the hall, at the café counter, through car windows, and once across a grave she had dug, on a morning when the hearse was late from Dax.

'I did a rib,' she said. 'On the winch.'

Marie-Jo stopped counting. 'You wrote to them about that winch.'

'Three times.'

Marie-Jo looked at her for about as long as it takes to count a chair. Then she said 'Sixty-three' again and went on down the hall.

There were twenty-four tables, each a top and two trestles, to go in three rows under the strip lights. Maylis carried the tops against her left side. Marie-Jo came behind her, kicked each table square with the side of her shoe and unrolled paper cloth along it. The hall smelled of floor wax and of the coffee urn, which Marie-Jo had switched on at noon. A feed sack of maize kernels stood on the edge of the stage for marking the cards. Beside it were the prizes, which were a ham, two ducks and a strimmer. Marie-Jo had printed cards for two hundred. The year before, eighty-eight people had come, twelve of them from the care home in its minibus.

'I've put you on the bar from eight,' Marie-Jo said. 'You've the time.'

She had written it on the clipboard before she said it.

* * *

By ten past five it was dark and still raining. Maylis locked the hall and drove up to the square, and this time she stopped in front of the mairie. A light was on inside. The mayor's space was empty, a white rectangle with the paint worn through on the driver's side.

She got out and stepped off the kerb into it. She went two paces in and stood square to the mairie door, on tarmac, with an oil stain by her left boot. Rain ran off her hood. She stood there for three minutes.

Headlights came round the church and stopped a metre short of her. The horn went twice. She stepped up onto the kerb, and Guy Larrère drove into his space and stopped with his front wheels where the stone had stood. He got out holding a folder over his head, reminded her that the lights were for Saturday, and went in to sign his post.
