# 1

It was nine minutes to three in the morning on Tuesday the twenty-fourth of November 2026. Maylis Lesca was awake in her own bed, and she could not move.

She tried her right hand first, because she was right-handed. Nothing happened. She tried her left hand, then both feet, then her jaw. Nothing moved. Only her eyes did what she told them. She could see the ceiling, the paper lampshade and the top of the wardrobe. She could see the red numbers of the clock radio on the chest of drawers. They said 02:51. Rain was falling on the roof.

Something was sitting on the wardrobe.

It was the size of a sheep, and darker than the ceiling behind it. It came down. It hit the floor like a full sack dropped off a trailer. It crossed the floorboards with a dry sound, like dead leaves being dragged. Then it jumped onto the bed in one go, the way a dog does, and sat down on her chest with its back to her.

She wanted to scream and she couldn't.

Its back was all there was to see. The back was brown leaves, each one overlapping the next. Where the leaves parted there was grey steel plate with a row of rivets. The thing weighed about thirty-five kilos, as much as a sack of cement. It smelled of cold steel and old tobacco. Nobody had ever smoked in this house.

She knew that smell. It was the smell of the old tobacco-drying barn at the end of the village. She had once sat in that barn for an afternoon and been paid for it.

Twelve years earlier, in June 2014, the village council had rented the barn to a team from a university. The team built a steel room inside it. The room was the size of a garden shed. It had one chair in it, and a panel with two lamps.

'One of these two lamps will come on,' Dr Kessler had told her at the door. 'You'll see which one. What we want to know is whether, somewhere, you also see the other one.'

Maylis asked where somewhere was. Dr Kessler said that was the question.

One lamp came on. She wrote down which one on a card and sealed the card in an envelope. Nobody had asked her for the envelope since. Then they opened the door, counted one of her, paid her, gave her a sandwich and went back to their university.

Now she had a cracked rib. She had cracked it on Monday, on her right side, and until it healed she had to sleep on her back. She pushed up against the thing with all the breath she had. It pressed down just as hard. Beside her Coralie slept on, turned to the wall.

The clock said 02:54 when the little finger of her right hand bent against the blanket. The rest of the hand followed. The thing got down and left the way it had come. When Coralie turned over, there was nothing on top of the wardrobe.

Coralie found Maylis's wrist, put two fingers on the inside of it and watched the clock.

'A hundred and twelve,' she said. 'What is it?'

'It's the rib.'

'What do you want, Maylis?'

'Nothing. Go back to sleep.'

Coralie left before it was light. She worked at the care home down in the Port. The Port was forty houses and the care home, on the low ground behind the embankment. The embankment was a bank of clay built between them and the river.

The shopping pad was on the kitchen table. It said *Tonight*, then *soup*, then *the rest of the duck*, with a box drawn beside each choice. Maylis wrote *Either* across the bottom and went out to the van.

* * *

It rained all day Tuesday. The rain came straight down.

That morning Maylis was in the square, raking leaves from the plane trees off the drain covers. She raked left-handed because of the rib. She was the only outdoor worker the village of Peyrebarthe employed.

The mayor's car was parked in front of the town hall, with the engine running and the mayor at the wheel. The mayor was Guy Larrère. Guy Larrère was also president of the landowners' association, and the association owned the sluice gate on the flood meadows below the village. Maylis kept the key to its winch.

Across the square the café door opened and a man went in out of the rain.

'Still in the box, were you?' somebody inside said. People laughed, and the door shut on the laughter.

People in Peyrebarthe said that to anyone who came late. They had been saying it for twelve years, since the June of the steel room.

The post van came around the church. The postman was young, and he was new on this route. He looked around the square. He saw a woman with a rake and a man in a car, and he went to the car.

Larrère lowered his window a few centimetres.

'I've got a registered letter for the mayor,' the postman said into the gap. 'They told me the town hall. Which one is that?'

'The one with the flag.'

'It's shut. Where can I find him?'

Larrère looked at the envelope. In the top left corner the sender had written *No. 41*.

'The town hall opens at two.'

'What does he look like?'

'You'd know him.'

The postman looked around the square again. This time he noticed a yellow raincoat with COMMUNE DE PEYREBARTHE printed across the back.

'Is the man who works for the village around?'

'That's me,' Maylis said.

He looked at the rake. He looked at the man in the car, who was looking at the church. Then he held out the letter, with the receipt card on top of it and a pen.

She signed in the box, *M. Lesca*. She made the tall L her father had made on every delivery note he ever signed. Rain fell on the card and the ink of the L ran.

'That's received, then,' the postman said. He bent down to the car window one more time. 'If you see the mayor, can you tell him it's come?'

'I'll tell him,' said Larrère.

The post van drove away.

'You didn't have to be so quick,' Larrère said to her. 'In my tray. I'll be brief. The winch.'

On Monday afternoon he had phoned her and told her to raise the gate. The winch had stuck and thrown her against the platform rail. That was how she had cracked the rib.

He took his right hand off the steering wheel, closed it around nothing and turned it behind the glass, to show her. 'Your father turned it with one hand. Like that.'

'It stuck on Monday,' she said. 'At nineteen turns.'

'So there's the question of the winch,' he said. 'It isn't settled, and you're not to worry about it.'

The window went up.

Jeannot Castagnet had watched her sign. He was sitting in his white van outside the café. Now he drove across the square to her and rolled his window all the way down.

'I watched him not sign it,' Jeannot said. 'Forty-one times I've paid for a registered letter, just to get that man's name on a card, and it's little Lesca who signs for them.' He nodded towards the river. 'I had a hand of water at the blind on Sunday night. Last night I sat over mud.'

The blind was his duck-hunting blind, a cellar under a hatch out on the flood meadows. Ducks only come down at night onto standing water. A hand of water is enough, the depth of a palm laid flat. On Monday the river had been low. When the river is low, a raised gate lets the water run off the meadows. Raising the gate had drained his blind.

'I was told to raise it.'

'You're told.'

'Put it in forty-two.'

He laughed. She wanted to laugh too, but the rib stopped her. He saw it stop her.

'What have you done to yourself?'

'A rib. The winch stuck.'

'Then that goes in forty-two as well,' he said. He wasn't laughing any more.

* * *

Maylis came home at seven that evening. Coralie was standing in the kitchen with the shopping pad in her hand. Both boxes on it were still empty, and *Either* was written across the bottom.

'I don't cook Either,' she said. 'There's no recipe for it. Soup or duck.'

'Whichever's less trouble.'

'That's Either again, with more words. They shut you in a box once, to see whether there were two of you. Would the other one know what she wants for dinner?'

She dropped the pad on the table and pulled the dish towel off the oven door. Maylis was still standing there in her raincoat. Coralie rubbed her wet hair with the towel, hard.

'Sixteen years, and I've had one straight answer out of you, and that was me. Eggs, then. Eggs is what Either gets in this house.'

She broke eggs into the pan. Maylis sat down slowly, a little at a time, because of the rib. She told Coralie about the registered letter, and the postman, and the man in the car who was looking at the church.

'He never said he'd tell him.'

'He said, I'll tell him.'

Coralie put the fork down. She opened the oven door a few centimetres, bent down, and spoke through the gap into the dark oven.

'I'll be brief,' she said. She was doing Larrère's voice, which was lower than hers and came from the back of the throat. 'The eggs. There's the question of the eggs. It isn't settled, and you're not to worry about it.' She looked at the oven shelf. 'In my tray,' she said, and shut the door on him.

Maylis laughed. The laugh got as far as the rib, and the rib hurt. She sat with her forearm pressed against her side and her eyes shut, and laughed as much as she could.

'Don't,' Coralie said. 'No. Wait.'

She took the pan off the heat and went to get the first-aid box she kept from work. She strapped the rib where Maylis sat, over her T-shirt. She used the wide bandage, three turns, pulled tight.

'Now you can.'

'It's gone.'

'Then I'll do the winch,' Coralie said. She closed her hand around nothing and turned it for the oven door, and the laugh came back.

They ate the eggs out of the pan. Afterwards Coralie ticked both boxes herself. She wrote *eggs* under them, drew a third box and ticked that one too.

At ten she pulled the blanket off the bed and shook it out over the floor. A few crumbs of brown leaf fell out of it. That had happened most nights for twelve years.

'Those plane trees,' she said. 'You bring the square home in your hair.'

She had been up since before six. She lay down on the side by the wall and fell asleep before the light was off. Maylis turned it off at ten past ten. She lay on her back, because she had to, and looked at the top of the wardrobe.

She was scared of going to sleep.

That morning, when the thing came, a picture had come into her head with it. The picture was of a stone. The stone stood in front of the town hall, in the space where the mayor parked. It was grey granite and as high as her shoulder. A name was cut into it in capitals: MAYLIS LESCA. Above her name was a man's name, and she could not read it.

Maylis dug the graves in Peyrebarthe. She knew what a name on a stone meant.

At nine minutes to three the thing came down off the wardrobe again and sat on her chest.

This time somebody was under it with her. It wasn't Coralie. Coralie was asleep by the wall with her back turned.
